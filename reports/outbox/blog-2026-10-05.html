What Can You Actually Learn From a DNA Sample?

A DNA sample reliably tells you four things today: who you are related to and roughly where your ancestors came from, which recessive conditions you carry, whether you have one of a short list of serious and actionable single-gene risks, and how you are likely to process certain medications. It does not reliably tell you how tall your children will be, what you will die of, or anything about your personality. The honest headline is that a sample is a physical object whose readable content grows over time, and most of what people hope to learn from DNA is not yet readable at useful precision.

Three different things get called "a DNA test"

Most confusion here comes from one word covering three products.

A genotyping array reads a selected set of known positions, commonly on the order of 700,000 of them. That is the technology behind most consumer ancestry and health-trait reports. It is cheap, fast, and blind to anything it was not designed to look at.

Whole genome sequencing reads essentially all of it, roughly 3.1 to 3.2 billion bases. It is the difference between checking 700,000 specific addresses and photographing the whole city. MyHeritage announced a move to whole genome sequencing in October 2025, described in trade coverage as the first mainstream consumer company to offer it widely (company announcement and trade reporting, not peer review; dated 2025, so a receipt rather than news).

A clinical diagnostic test is ordered by a clinician, run in a CLIA-certified laboratory, and interpreted against a specific question. It is the only one of the three designed to be acted on medically.

These are not grades of the same product. They answer different questions, and a consumer array reporting "no increased risk" often means "we did not look."

What a DNA sample reliably tells you today

Relatedness and ancestry. This is the most robust thing DNA does. Relationship inference between two samples is close to settled science, which is why it carries courts and genealogy alike. Ancestry percentages are softer than they look, because they are comparisons against reference panels that get updated, which is why your percentages change when a company improves its panel.

Carrier status. Whether you carry a recessive variant you could pass on. This is well defined, well understood, and genuinely useful to know before having children.

A short list of serious, actionable single-gene risks. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics maintains a list of genes where an unexpected finding is considered worth reporting back because something can be done about it. The current list is version 3.3, and three genes (ABCD1, CYP27A1 and PLN) were added effective 12 January 2026 (laboratory product update; we did not establish the list's total gene count and are not stating one). The CDC separately designates a small set of "Tier 1" genomic conditions, including hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, Lynch syndrome and familial hypercholesterolemia, where evidence supports acting on a finding.

Drug metabolism. How you process certain medications is among the most practically useful genetic information available. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium publishes prescribing guidance for defined gene-drug pairs, and the FDA maintains its own Table of Pharmacogenetic Associations (we did not establish current counts for either and are not stating one).

What it does not tell you

Most common disease, at useful precision. Heart disease, diabetes and most cancers are the product of many variants and a life. Polygenic scores capture a fraction of the variation, and a fraction of population-level variation is not a prediction about you. We wrote the long version of this in what polygenic embryo screening can and cannot tell you, and the limits there apply to adults too.

Personality, intelligence, talent. No.

Anything about a person who did not give the sample. Your sample describes you. It constrains guesses about your relatives, which is a privacy problem rather than a product feature, and it says nothing specific about a descendant who does not exist.

Accuracy outside the populations the research was done in. Most large genetic studies were done in people of European ancestry, and predictive performance falls outside those groups. This is a documented limitation, not a hedge.

The uncertainty nobody puts in the brochure

The most honest fact about genetic testing is how much of it comes back as a shrug.

A result can be pathogenic, benign, or a variant of uncertain significance: a difference the laboratory found and cannot interpret. Roughly 30 percent of patients undergoing germline genetic testing receive only VUS results (reported in the clinical literature).

Those uncertain results do get resolved, and the direction of the resolution is the part worth knowing. In one large dataset, 7.7 percent of unique variants of uncertain significance were reclassified, and of those, 91.2 percent were downgraded to benign or likely benign while 8.7 percent were upgraded to pathogenic or likely pathogenic. A 2025 multicenter study in Frontiers in Oncology following 932 participants with 1,032 VUS in breast cancer susceptibility genes found 20 percent reclassified, and reported the rate by group as 19 percent in White, 23 percent in Black or African American and 27 percent in Asian participants (peer-reviewed; the group differences reflect how much reference data exists for each population).

Read that plainly: most uncertainty resolves toward "this was nothing," and the groups with the least reference data see the most churn. Both halves of that matter, and neither is usually mentioned when a test is sold.

The part that actually changes: the same sample, read later

This is the one claim about DNA preservation that is load-bearing, so it deserves to be stated carefully.

The sample does not change. What can be read from it does.

Two dated examples make the point without any speculation. The ACMG list gained three genes on 12 January 2026, which means a sample analyzed in 2025 against the prior list was analyzed against a smaller question than the same sample would be today. And the consumer industry's method itself moved from arrays to whole genome sequencing, with MyHeritage's October 2025 announcement as the visible marker. A 700,000-position array result from 2018 cannot be upgraded. A preserved physical sample can be re-read with whatever method exists when somebody asks.

That is the whole case, and the honest boundary around it is this: re-reading a sample later is a real capability, not a promise about what will be discovered. Nobody can tell you what will be readable in 2046. What is defensible is the narrow structural claim that a stored sample keeps the question open and a finished report closes it.

FAQ

What can you learn from a DNA sample?

Reliably: relatedness and ancestry, carrier status for recessive conditions, a short list of serious actionable single-gene risks, and how you metabolize certain drugs. Not reliably: most common disease risk at useful precision, personality, intelligence, or anything specific about a person who did not give the sample.

Is whole genome sequencing better than a consumer DNA test?

It reads far more: roughly 3.1 to 3.2 billion bases versus about 700,000 selected positions on a typical array. Whether that is "better" depends on the question. For ancestry and relatedness an array is usually sufficient. For anything clinical, the test that matters is one ordered and interpreted by a clinician in a CLIA-certified laboratory.

What does a variant of uncertain significance mean?

The laboratory found a difference in your DNA and cannot say whether it causes disease. About 30 percent of patients having germline testing receive only these. When they are later reclassified, the large majority are downgraded to benign or likely benign.

Can a DNA sample be re-tested years later?

Yes, if the physical sample still exists and was stored properly. That is the practical argument for preserving a sample rather than only keeping a report: the report is fixed at the method and the gene list of the year it was produced, and the sample is not.

Does a DNA test tell you what you will die of?

No. For most common causes of death, genetics is one contributor among many, and genetic scores explain only part of the population-level variation. They are not individual predictions.

Will my DNA results change over time?

Your DNA does not change. Interpretations do. Ancestry percentages shift as reference panels improve, uncertain variants get reclassified in both directions, and the list of genes considered worth reporting is revised, most recently in January 2026.
