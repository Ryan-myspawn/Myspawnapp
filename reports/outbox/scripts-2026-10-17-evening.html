X DRAFT 1: the one with the best chance of travelling

A cooling unit on a biobank freezer failed 32 times in six months.
Nothing was lost. The paper signed the system off for routine use.
That is the good news. The unit that failed was the backup, failing at its weekly switchover. A backup failing repeatedly is a backup doing its job.
The thing to be worried about is a storage company with no published failures at all. That is not a better freezer. That is usually an empty page.
No failure data is not the same as no failures.
(Frontiers in Medicine, peer-reviewed, Jan 2020. Read via search index, not opened at source.) [1]

Why this one: it is the week's most portable sentence, it names no company, and it argues against the instinct of everyone reading it.

X DRAFT 2: the number

$124 trillion is changing hands by 2048. Four slices of it get quoted: $54T to women, $52T to children, $18T to charity, $105T to heirs.
You cannot add them.
The $54T goes to widowed spouses FIRST. A household losing a person and keeping its money, inside one marriage, inside one generation. Then years later some of it moves again and gets counted again.
One dollar. Two events. One headline.
Before you plan around a number, ask which event it describes. [2]

2 THREADS-STYLE TEXT POSTS

THREADS 1

A peer-reviewed paper five days ago wrote digital data into synthetic DNA, read it back, corrected the errors, and recovered the message.
The message was 60 bytes. About one line of text.
That is not a criticism. It is the paper's own number, and it is the most useful figure published in that field this month. Theoretical capacity is a property of the molecule. Recovered payload is a property of the engineering. Those two numbers are several orders of magnitude apart right now, and that gap is not a scandal. It is just where the field is.
Best detail: in one of their own comparisons, commercially bought DNA beat their chip. They published the experiment where they lose. That is why I believe the rest of it. [3]

THREADS 2

Every storage company tells you its system is validated.
The team that validated an automated biobank store wrote in their own paper that the validation requirement is subjective, and leaves the operator without specific criteria. Which is why they had to write their own protocol and publish it.
Read that as a buyer. The company being validated often chooses what validated means, and then tells you it is validated. Both halves true. The sentence nearly empty.
Four questions that beat the word: what are your acceptance criteria in numbers, who wrote them, when were they last tested rather than last documented, and what is your moving plan.
We have not published ours. Ask us anyway. Ask everybody. [1]
