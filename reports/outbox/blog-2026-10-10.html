What Does a Genetic Counselor Actually Do, and Is It Worth Paying For?

Four of our own articles send readers to a professional none of them describes. That gap is this article.

Primary audience: Men 25-55.

Trend-hook used: one live item, dated: S. 3607, the Access to Genetic Counselor Services Act of 2026, introduced 8 January 2026, which would let genetic counselors bill Medicare directly. It is at the earliest stage of the legislative process and the article says so.

(Spelling note: this article uses the American "counselor" throughout, because that is what American readers type. Two earlier articles use "counsellor". The inconsistency is deliberate and is recorded in our topic log rather than quietly fixed.)

A genetic counselor is a master's-trained, board-certified clinician whose job is to work out which genetic question you actually have, decide whether a test can answer it, and then tell you what the result does and does not mean. They are not doctors and they do not prescribe. The single most useful thing they do is stop people ordering tests that cannot answer their question, which is the opposite of what you would expect from someone who makes a living in genetics. An initial session typically runs $230 to $650, and insurance may cover it if you meet criteria based on personal or family history.

What actually happens in the appointment

Three things, in this order.

They build the family history properly. Not the version you remember. A structured pedigree, usually three generations, with ages at diagnosis rather than just diagnoses. Age at diagnosis is the part people leave out and the part that changes the answer: a grandmother with breast cancer at 78 and an aunt with breast cancer at 38 point in completely different directions.

They decide whether testing is indicated, and which test. This is the technical core. There are panels, single-gene tests, exome and genome sequencing, and consumer genotyping arrays, and they answer different questions at different costs with different false-negative rates. Our guide to [what a DNA sample can actually tell you](/blog/what-can-you-learn-from-dna-sample) covers that distinction in detail.

They interpret the result, including the uninformative ones. Most results are not a clean yes or no. Many are a variant of uncertain significance, and someone has to explain what that is, what happens next, and who is responsible for telling you if it changes. We wrote about that last question separately in [will anyone tell you if your result changes](/blog/genetic-test-result-reanalysis), and the short answer is usually nobody.

The qualification, precisely

A master's degree in genetic counseling, then board certification by the American Board of Genetic Counseling. That is the credential to look for, and it is verifiable directly with the board.

State licensure is separate from board certification, and the number of states requiring it is genuinely contested. A 2026 preprint says 39 states plus Washington DC. A vendor page dated October 2026 gives the same 39. A Rutgers paper says 35. CDC material from 2020 said 26. Another source cited 22. [1] [2]

We are not going to pick one. The 39 figure is the most recent we found and it comes from a preprint and a commercial page, which is not the same as a state board saying so. If licensure matters to your situation, check your own state board. The honest summary is that the number has been rising fast and the published counts lag reality by years.

How many of them exist, and why you may wait

A 2026 preprint estimates 9,293 genetics professionals in the United States as of 2023, of whom about 71.5 percent are genetic counselors. That is roughly 6,600 people for a country of 340 million. [1]

The profession doubled between 2014 and 2024, per a National Society of Genetic Counselors 2024 survey, and is projected to double again by 2034. [1] Even so, access is uneven: entire regions have very few, and telehealth has become the practical answer for most people outside major academic centers.

Epistemic label, stated plainly: the workforce figures above come from a 2026 preprint, which means not peer-reviewed, and the preprint itself cites an NSGC survey rather than a census. We did not open the preprint at its own domain. Treat these as the best available estimates, not as counts.

What it costs, with real numbers

Published ranges, and they are wide.

  • Memorial Sloan Kettering's own pre-visit information sheet lists $230 to $650 for an initial consultation and $85 to $375 for a follow-up. [3]
  • One general source puts a session at $100 to over $450.
  • A commercial vendor page says counseling is frequently billed separately at $150 to $300 and up. (Vendor source. Weigh accordingly.)

Insurance is conditional, not automatic. Insurers generally cover genetic counseling when you meet specific criteria based on personal or family history, and cost-sharing still applies. Network status matters: one large telehealth provider states it is in network with most commercial plans but does not accept Medicare, Medicaid, Tricare or related managed plans. [4]

Medicaid reimbursement for a standard session runs roughly $25 to $56 depending on state and billing code, which is part of why availability varies so much by geography. [2]

And there is a structural reason Medicare is a problem: CMS does not currently recognize genetic counselors as healthcare providers, so they cannot bill Medicare independently. S. 3607, the Access to Genetic Counselor Services Act of 2026, introduced by Senator John Barrasso on 8 January 2026, would change that. [5] It is at the earliest stage of the legislative process and we found no evidence it has advanced. Do not plan around it.

If cost is the barrier, say so out loud when you call. At least one nonprofit offers zero-cost counseling for households at or below 200 percent of the federal poverty level, and many academic centers have income-based tiers.

When it is worth paying for, and when it is not

Worth it:

  • A close relative has a diagnosed hereditary condition, or several relatives have the same cancer, especially young.
  • You are considering embryo screening. Our article on [polygenic embryo screening](/blog/polygenic-embryo-screening-explained) makes the same point and it is worth repeating: the counselor should not be employed by the company selling the test.
  • You already have a result you do not understand, particularly an uncertain one.
  • You inherited a relative's test results and do not know what to do with them, which we covered in [what to do with a parent's DNA results](/blog/parents-dna-results-after-death).

Probably not worth it yet:

  • Pure curiosity about ancestry. That is a different product and a different question.
  • You have not yet written down your family history. Do that first; it is free, and it is the input the appointment runs on. It may also change whether you need the appointment at all.

FAQ

What does a genetic counselor do?

They take a structured family history, determine whether genetic testing is indicated and which test fits the question, and interpret results including uncertain ones. They are master's-trained and board-certified by the American Board of Genetic Counseling. They do not prescribe.

How much does genetic counseling cost?

Published ranges are wide. Memorial Sloan Kettering's pre-visit sheet lists $230 to $650 for an initial consultation and $85 to $375 for a follow-up; other sources quote $100 to $450-plus per session. Insurance may cover it if you meet criteria based on personal or family history, and cost-sharing usually applies. [3]

Does Medicare cover genetic counseling?

Not directly. CMS does not currently recognize genetic counselors as healthcare providers, so they cannot bill Medicare independently. A bill to change that, S. 3607, was introduced on 8 January 2026 and is at the earliest stage of the legislative process. We found no evidence it has advanced. [5]

Do I need a license check, and how many states require one?

Board certification and state licensure are different things. Published counts of states requiring licensure disagree: 39 states plus DC in a 2026 preprint, 35 in a Rutgers paper, 26 in 2020 CDC material, 22 in another source. The number has risen fast and published counts lag. Check your own state board rather than any article, including this one. [1] [2]

Is a genetic counselor the same as a geneticist?

No. A clinical geneticist is a physician. A genetic counselor is a master's-trained specialist who often works alongside one. For most questions the counselor is the right first appointment, and they will refer upward when a physician is needed.

Can I do this by telehealth?

Usually yes, and for most people outside major academic centers that is the practical route. Check in advance whether the provider accepts your plan: at least one large telehealth provider is in network with most commercial insurers but accepts no government plans at all. [4]
