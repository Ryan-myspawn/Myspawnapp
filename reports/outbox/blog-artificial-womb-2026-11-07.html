Artificial Wombs, Explained: What the Technology Actually Does

Published: November 7, 2026 · Lane (b) the science explained · myspawn.me/blog

The devices described in the news as artificial wombs do not gestate anybody. They support an infant who has already been delivered, usually by caesarean section, by taking over one job the placenta does: gas exchange. A more accurate name for the current technology is an artificial placenta. It is designed for extremely premature infants, it has been tested in animals for years, and as of November 2026 we could not confirm from any source we can read that a human trial has begun.

That gap, between what the technology is and what the phrase implies, is the whole subject of this article.

What the device actually is

The best-documented system comes out of the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia lineage and is being developed commercially under the name EXTEND. The design has three unusual features and each one tells you what it is for.

There is no pump. The infant's own heart drives blood through the umbilical vessels into an external oxygenator and back. Adding a pump would risk overloading a fetal heart, so the engineering works around it rather than through it.

The infant stays in fluid. Lungs built to develop in liquid are injured by mechanical ventilation. The system does not fight that physiology; it continues it.

Access is through the umbilical cord. That is why delivery by caesarean is a requirement rather than a preference: the cord has to be cannulated intact.

What it replaces, and what it does not

The placenta does several jobs. This device does one of them.

It performs gas exchange: oxygen in, carbon dioxide out. That is the job that keeps an extremely premature infant alive while the lungs continue to develop.

It does not perform the placenta's endocrine functions, and it does not provide the immune interface between two genetically different bodies. Those are not engineering details left for version two. They are the reason the same technology does not scale backwards into gestation.

So full ectogenesis, growing a human from an early embryo to term outside a body, is not a longer version of this project. It is a different project, and nobody is close to it.

The honest status, including what we could not confirm

Here is where most coverage either overstates or goes quiet.

An FDA advisory committee met in September 2023 to discuss human trials of the technology (Scientific American; MIT Technology Review explainer). To test in humans, the developer needs an investigational device exemption from the FDA, and reporting at the time indicated the company might be ready to request one in late 2023.

The technology holds FDA breakthrough device designation, a status that allows a novel device to move faster through review. Label: breakthrough designation is a procedural status. It is not an approval, and it is not evidence that anything works in humans.

Animal testing is extensive. Coverage from 2023 puts it at roughly 300 lamb fetuses. Our own research file records a later figure of 350-plus animal tests. Both are secondary figures from different dates and we are not reconciling them here.

What we could not confirm, stated plainly: whether an investigational device exemption has since been granted, and whether any human has been placed in such a system. The sources we could retrieve are from 2023 and early 2024. A three-year-old "trials could start soon" is not a current status, and treating it as one is how this subject gets reported badly.

The constraints that rarely make the headline

Three practical requirements shape who this could ever be for.

Delivery must be by caesarean section, because the umbilical cord has to be cannulated.

The infant would need anticoagulation to prevent clots forming where the tubes enter the body. That is a real and ongoing risk, not a footnote.

The target population is infants born at less than 28 weeks of gestation. And here our own file carries an unresolved conflict we are not going to tidy: one secondary source describes a target population from 22 weeks, another describes 23 to 25 weeks. Neither is the company's own protocol. We have not reconciled them and we are not picking the more convenient one.

Our article on how long a DNA sample actually lasts makes a related point about secondary figures: a number that has travelled through three outlets is a number about the outlets.

Two things in circulation that are not real

"EctoLife" is not a company, a prototype or a product. It is a concept video produced by a filmmaker in 2022, showing rows of pods growing babies. It has circulated for years as though it were a facility under construction. It is a rendering.

The viral "pregnancy robot" reported from China is a fabrication, assessed as AI-generated by fact-checkers.

We mention both only to mark them as fake. If a source you are reading cites either as evidence of progress, that tells you what the source is worth.

Frequently asked questions

Is an artificial womb available now?

No. The technology being developed is an artificial placenta for extremely premature infants, tested extensively in animals. As of November 2026 we could not confirm from retrievable sources that a human trial has begun.

Could an artificial womb grow a baby from scratch?

Not with this technology. It supports an infant already delivered and replaces gas exchange only. It does not replace the placenta's endocrine or immune functions, which is why it does not extend backwards into full gestation.

How early could it support an infant?

Reporting describes a target population of infants under 28 weeks of gestation. Secondary sources disagree about the lower bound, with one describing 22 weeks and another 23 to 25 weeks, and we have not resolved that conflict.

Is EctoLife real?

No. It is a concept video from 2022, not a facility, a company or a prototype. The viral Chinese "pregnancy robot" is likewise a fabrication.

Does this change anything about freezing eggs, sperm or embryos?

No. This is neonatal support technology, at the opposite end of pregnancy from conception and fertility preservation. The two are regularly confused in coverage and they have nothing to do with each other.

No. Storing a DNA record is not a reproductive service and confers no capability related to gestation. We store a sample. We make no promise about descendants or outcomes.

What this comes down to

The thing in the headlines is a machine that keeps an extremely premature infant alive by doing one job the placenta does. It is a serious piece of engineering and it is not what its popular name suggests.

The habit worth taking from this: when a technology is named after what it resembles, find out what it replaces. That one question separates the real work from the rendering.
