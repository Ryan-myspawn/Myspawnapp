What It Means When a Genetic Test Is "Validated"

Direct answer: "Validated" is not one claim. It is up to three separate claims, and a test can satisfy the first without satisfying the others. Analytical validity asks whether the laboratory read your sample correctly. Clinical validity asks whether that reading predicts anything about your health. Clinical utility asks whether knowing it changes what anyone does. A test can be analytically excellent and clinically meaningless. When a company says its test is validated and stops there, the useful question is: validated for which of the three?

The three questions hiding inside one word

The framework behind this distinction is called ACCE, and it is not a marketing invention. From 2000 to 2004 the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Office of Public Health Genomics ran the ACCE Model Project, which produced the first publicly available analytic process for evaluating genetic tests. The acronym covers analytic validity, clinical validity, clinical utility, and the ethical, legal and social implications. The model works through a standard set of 44 targeted questions about the disorder, the test and the clinical scenario. The later CDC-supported EGAPP initiative built on that structure.

The reason it matters to you is that the three validity questions are answered by different people, at different times, to different standards. Nobody is obliged to answer all three before selling you a test.

1. Analytical validity: did the lab read the sample correctly

This is a question about the assay. It asks how accurately and reliably the laboratory detects the genetic variant it is looking for. Does it find the variant when the variant is present, does it stay quiet when it is absent, and does it give the same answer on Tuesday as it gave on Monday?

This is the one most likely to be genuinely well established, because it is the one that regulation actually reaches. It is also the one companies quote, because it produces impressive-looking numbers.

2. Clinical validity: does the result predict anything

This asks how well a positive result correlates with an actual outcome: a disease, a drug response, something in the world. It is measured with sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive value, which are not the same quantities as analytical accuracy and frequently diverge from it.

Here is the gap in one sentence. A laboratory can read a variant with near-perfect accuracy while the published evidence linking that variant to an outcome is thin, contested, or drawn from a population that does not include you. The reading is right. The prediction may still be worthless. This is also why a result can be correct today and reinterpreted later, which is a separate problem we have written about in genetic test result reanalysis.

3. Clinical utility: does it change anything

The third question is whether testing produces a measurable improvement in health or care. The CDC's model files a long list under this heading, including effective interventions, economic evaluation, natural history of the disorder, and quality assurance.

Utility is the question most often skipped, and skipping it is not always dishonest. A result can be interesting, accurate and predictive while no intervention exists that would change your outcome. That is a legitimate thing to sell, as long as it is not sold as actionable.

Critics have extended the framework. The PHG Foundation's "Moving Beyond ACCE" work argues that a test's stated purpose should be evaluated as its own item, separately from validity and utility, because the same assay can be fit for one purpose and useless for another.

So who actually checks which one, in 2026

This is where the honest answer has changed recently, and changed in a direction most coverage has not caught up with.

In May 2024 the FDA issued a final rule that would have treated laboratory developed tests as medical devices, bringing them into a framework that reviews clinical validity. On 31 March 2025 a federal district court in Texas vacated that rule, holding that such tests are not "devices" under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and that the agency had exceeded its statutory authority. Reporting indicates the Department of Health and Human Services chose not to appeal. On 19 September 2025 the FDA formally removed the phrase "including when the manufacturer of these products is a laboratory" from 21 CFR 809.3(a), reverting the regulation to its pre-rule text.

The practical consequence: for a large share of genetic tests, the governing framework is CLIA, administered by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services. And CLIA is a laboratory quality framework. It asks whether the laboratory can produce a reliable measurement. It does not ask whether the measurement predicts anything about you.

So the current state of affairs, stated plainly: question 1 has a regulator. Questions 2 and 3 largely do not, unless a specific test went through an FDA review pathway, which some do. If you want to know how those pathways differ when they are used, we have a separate piece on FDA clearance versus approval.

One thing we could not verify and are therefore not relying on. A secondary source refers to a bill, H.R. 8890, said to have been introduced on 19 May 2026, as a possible replacement federal framework. We could not confirm its existence, text or status, so we are not treating it as real. Check Congress.gov and the FDA's own laboratory developed tests page before you believe anything about a new federal framework, including this paragraph.

How to read a validation claim without a science degree

Four questions, in order, and you can ask all four by email.

1. "Validated for what?" If the answer describes how precisely the lab detects a variant, that is analytical validity and it does not answer the question you care about.

2. "What is the positive predictive value for someone like me?" This forces the conversation from analytical performance to clinical validity. "Someone like me" is doing real work in that sentence, because predictive value depends on how common the outcome is in the population being tested.

3. "What would I do differently if the result were positive?" This is the utility question. "Nothing, currently" is a respectable answer. A vague answer is not.

4. "Which of these is reviewed by anyone outside your company?" After the 2025 vacatur, for many tests the honest answer is the laboratory quality framework only.

If a company cannot distinguish these three ideas in its own reply, that tells you something independent of the test's quality.

FAQ

Is analytical validity the same as accuracy?

Roughly, yes, but it is accuracy at a specific job: detecting the variant the assay targets. It says nothing about whether detecting that variant tells you anything. A tape measure can be accurate to a millimetre and still not predict your health.

Can a test be clinically valid but have no clinical utility?

Yes, and this is common rather than exotic. A result can genuinely predict an elevated risk while no intervention exists that changes the outcome. That makes the test informative and not actionable, which are different properties.

Does CLIA certification mean a genetic test works?

No. CLIA is a laboratory quality framework administered by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, concerned with whether the laboratory produces reliable measurements. It is not a finding that a test result predicts anything clinically. Treating a CLIA certificate as evidence of clinical validity is the single most common error in this area.

Who regulates genetic tests in the United States now?

As of the last check we could make, laboratory developed tests are governed primarily under CLIA, after a federal court vacated the FDA's 2024 rule on 31 March 2025 and the FDA reverted the relevant regulatory text in September 2025. Specific tests that went through an FDA pathway are a separate case. This is an area where the answer has changed twice in two years, so verify it at the FDA's own page before relying on it.

What does "sensitivity and specificity" actually mean for me?

Sensitivity is how often the test catches the thing when it is there. Specificity is how often it stays quiet when it is not. Neither tells you the question you actually want answered, which is: given that my result is positive, how likely is the outcome? That is positive predictive value, and it depends on how common the outcome is in people like you.

If my result changes later, was the original test wrong?

Not necessarily. Analytical validity can hold while the clinical interpretation of a variant moves as evidence accumulates. The lab read it correctly and the field's understanding changed.
