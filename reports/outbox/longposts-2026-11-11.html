#205 [X] We told you the reliable half. Here is the other one.

In September we told you epigenetic clocks were reliable. We cited the right study and quoted the wrong number, and a 2026 paper has made the mistake legible.

Here is the distinction that matters, and almost no coverage makes it.

Technical reliability asks whether the same sample, split in two and run twice, gives the same answer. Biological reliability asks whether the same person, sampled on two ordinary days, gives the same answer. They are different questions and the industry sells the first as if it answered the second.

The finding. A 2026 analysis published in Aging Cell (peer-reviewed; Sehgal et al.) pooled four independent studies and reported that technical reproducibility did not predict biological reliability. A clock can be laboratory-perfect and still move on you.

The numbers. Pooled across those studies, most clocks landed at roughly 0.4 to 0.7 on an intraclass correlation, the moderate-to-good band. None reached excellent.

The detail that should worry a buyer most. When replicates differed only by where the sample sat on the processing slide, several clocks dropped below the "good" threshold. Your result moved because of a position in a machine.

What held up. Principal-component reformulations, PCPhenoAge, PCGrimAge, PCHorvath1 and Systems Age, were reproducible on both measures. First-generation clocks and Dunedin-based measures were less stable. So our September sentence was right about which implementations are tightest and wrong about what tightness proves.

Two more facts worth carrying. A 2026 review in Biogerontology found an absence of independent peer-reviewed validation for several commercial clocks, in some cases with no published correlation or mean-absolute-error figures at all. A separate 2026 ethics paper observed that several direct-to-consumer companies market a "biological age" while running first-generation clocks, which were trained to predict calendar age, not health.

And a number we are refusing to print. One commercial comparison page offers a specific test-retest precision figure in years. We could not trace it to any primary study, so it is not in this article. A precise-sounding number with no paper behind it travels further than a vague one, which is exactly the problem.

And the clocks are not one instrument. This is the second thing the market blurs. Horvath's 2013 first-generation clock was trained to predict calendar age, and in one longitudinal cohort analysis it was the only clock tested that showed no association with mortality when measured once at baseline. GrimAge was trained on mortality and healthspan directly. PhenoAge was trained against clinical markers. DunedinPACE does not output an age at all; it estimates a rate. Asking which clock produced your number is not pedantry, it is the whole question.

The strongest evidence in the field is not a reading, it is a slope. A 2024 analysis of 699 adults followed for up to 24 years in the InCHIANTI cohort found that faster longitudinal change across several clocks was significantly associated with higher mortality, independent of baseline age. The movement carried information the snapshot did not.

The practical rule. If your reading improves by less than the test's own instability, nothing has been demonstrated. On the published ranges, that bar is higher than most buyers assume. The cohort evidence that carried real signal was about change measured repeatedly over years in the same lab, not one number on one morning.

MySpawn sells none of this and runs no clocks. We are correcting our own September article because we wrote it.

#206 [FACEBOOK] Somebody has to mow it in 2126, and somebody worked out how to pay for that

You have walked past the answer to a very hard question without noticing, probably several times.

A cemetery sells you a plot once. Then it owes you maintenance for as long as the cemetery exists. One payment, an obligation with no end date. That arithmetic should not work, and an entire body of state law exists because it does not work on its own.

The mechanism is called a perpetual care or endowment care fund, and the rules are unusually blunt.

Mississippi requires an irrevocable trust whose principal must stay intact, with only the income available for upkeep. The fund cannot be reached by creditors and cannot be pledged as collateral. Where the cemetery sells on instalments, a percentage of each payment has to go in as it arrives. And when the Secretary of State has reason to believe a cemetery is insolvent or underfunded, the office can order the deposits made.

Michigan requires an irrevocable endowment and perpetual care trust, and where the seller is not the cemetery itself, 15% of gross proceeds must be deposited into the cemetery's trust.

South Carolina requires the trust to be irrevocable and to hold at least $15,000, with no principal withdrawn without the Board's permission.

Virginia lets a cemetery recover its own original deposit, but only once the trust holds twice that amount, after which deposits resume. Income can go only to care, maintenance, administration and embellishment.

Georgia's Attorney General issued an unofficial opinion that deposits run on a percentage of sales price with a per-item minimum, and that a cemetery may not charge the customer an add-on fee to cover it.

Read those together and a design appears. You cannot spend the principal. You cannot borrow against it. You cannot bill the customer separately for it. And a regulator can force the deposit if you do not make it.

This is what "forever" looks like when somebody has actually costed it. Not a promise, not a brand, not a founder's intention. A ring-fenced fund, a prohibition on touching it, and a named official with the power to intervene.

The law is still moving. A 2026 Mississippi bill, SB 2530, would raise the certificate-of-deposit and irrevocable-trust thresholds from $50,000 to $75,000; we could not confirm its final status, so treat it as pending.

One honest caution before anyone quotes figures at a salesperson: percentages vary enormously by state, and several widely circulated numbers, including a "10 to 15% typical" and a "70 to 90% of pre-need payments", appear on advice sites without a statute behind them and do not match the statutes above. Check your own state.

Here is the question I keep coming back to. Every business that promises to hold something for a long time is making the same promise a cemetery makes. Almost none of them are regulated like one.

So: if a company told you it would keep something safe for a hundred years, what would you actually want to see before you believed it?

#207 [X] An industry audited its own safety net and published the number anyway

97.4%.

That is the share of software escrow deposits that, when sent in for analysis, were found to be incomplete. The figure is Iron Mountain's, published in 2007, and quoted by the Society for Computers and Law. The company selling the safety net published the statistic about the safety net.

Here is the idea being punched at, and it is not any company. It is the belief that depositing a thing somewhere is the same as the thing being recoverable.

What source code escrow is. A software vendor deposits its source code with a neutral third party. If the vendor goes bankrupt or breaches, the customer can obtain the code and keep the system running. It has been standard practice in enterprise contracts for decades.

What the industry's own figures say about the deposits. Alongside the 97.4%, the same material reports that 74% of examined deposits required additional input from the developer before they were usable. A more recent provider figure, undated in the sources we could reach, puts the share of unverified deposits that fail to compile during an emergency release at 87%.

The failure is not the vault. It is everything around the vault.

Failure one: the deposit rots. Critics writing on escrow note that released code is frequently outdated or defective. In one cited case, escrowed code could not perform basic booking functions for the vendor's own hotels.

Failure two: release is not automatic. Escrow agents are commonly prohibited from releasing until the vendor approves, which means the moment you need it most is the moment the counterparty has least reason to cooperate.

Failure three: the code may not help you. Many customers hired an outside vendor precisely because they had no in-house development capacity. Handing those customers a source tree solves a problem they were never equipped to have.

Failure four: the clock runs while you argue. Commentary on escrow disputes notes that vendors may not keep the deposit properly updated during the period the parties are fighting over release. The deposit ages fastest exactly when it is most needed.

And the format moved underneath the practice. The classic escrow was designed for software you ran on your own machines. Most enterprise software is now delivered as a service, where the code is only one of the things you would need; the running environment, the data and the operational knowledge are the rest. An escrow that hands you a source tree for a system you never operated is a different product from the one the contract imagined in 1998.

The defence, stated fairly. Providers argue most releases go through without dispute, and one puts arbitrated disputed releases at 0.688% over two decades. They also argue, correctly, that an escrow is only as good as what is in it, and that verification testing is the fix. Every release-rate figure we found came from a party selling escrow, and we are labelling them rather than laundering them.

Now the part that applies to us. MySpawn stores a physical DNA record with a third-party biorepository. That is structurally an escrow. The same three failure modes apply and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. The relevant questions are not where the thing is kept. They are whether anyone has verified that what was deposited is what the paperwork says, what event triggers release, and who has to agree.

A deposit is a claim about the future. Verification is the only thing that turns it into a fact.

#208 [REDDIT: r/Entrepreneur] If your business holds a customer's physical property, you are already in a legal category you probably have not named

Founder here, and this is a disclosure rather than a pitch: I run a company that stores physical samples for customers, so I had to go and learn this. Posting it because almost nobody who holds customer property seems to know the category exists.

It is called bailment. It is the body of law covering what happens when you hold property that belongs to somebody else without owning it. You are the bailee. Your customer is the bailor. If you run storage, repair, cleaning, shipping, valet, fulfilment or a laboratory, you are in it whether or not your contract says the word.

The part that surprised me most: the standard of care depends on who benefits.

Mutual benefit, which is any paid arrangement, carries ordinary care. That is the default for commercial storage.

Sole benefit of the bailee, where you hold something for your own advantage, carries extraordinary care, a higher bar.

Gratuitous, where you are doing the customer a favour, carries a lighter duty, described in secondary sources as answering only for gross neglect, or sometimes as the care you would give your own property of that kind.

Three practical consequences I did not expect.

One: the category can change under you. In Preston v. Prather, later transactions turned a gratuitous bailment into a mutual-benefit one and raised the duty. In Mandujano v. Guerra, providing lunch in exchange for use of a truck was enough to make the arrangement mutual. A small favour thrown in can quietly raise your standard of care.

Two: in some jurisdictions you start out presumed negligent. A South Carolina case is summarised as holding that a mutual-benefit bailee is presumed negligent when the property is lost or destroyed in its custody, and must show ordinary care to rebut it. The burden sits on you, not the customer.

Three: you are not an insurer, and that cuts both ways. Bailment is not absolute liability. But parties can contract around the default, which means a lot of storage contracts are quietly shifting the standard, and most customers have never read that clause.

And the duty runs both ways, which nobody told me. Where the bailor gets a benefit, the bailor has a duty to inform the bailee of known defects and to make a reasonable inspection for others. If a customer hands you something hazardous or already damaged without saying so, that is not purely your problem.

The three things I would now put in writing on day one. What exactly is being held, described precisely enough that a dispute could identify it. Who benefits from the arrangement, because that sets the standard of care. And what happens on non-payment, on death, and on the business failing, because the default rules will answer those questions for you if your contract does not.

What I did with this. We describe what we store, where it is held, and under what arrangement, in plain language, because the law is going to characterise the relationship regardless of what the marketing says. We store a time capsule with your genome in it at a third-party biorepository. That is a bailment, and naming it correctly is cheaper than discovering it in a dispute.

What we do not know yet. Standards vary by state and most of what is above comes from case summaries and textbooks rather than from the opinions themselves. I have not had a lawyer in any specific state review how these rules apply to long-duration biological storage, and I would be genuinely interested if anyone here has.

#209 [X] Nails: 2 to 2,295 nanograms. Hair: 31 out of 49.

Those two lines are most of what is known about reading chemical age markers out of the material people actually keep.

Why it matters. Methylation panels, the chemistry behind biological-age testing, are normally run on blood or saliva. Almost everything an ordinary person stores, or finds in a drawer after someone dies, is keratin: hair and nail clippings. The question of whether keratin carries usable methylation is small, unglamorous and almost never covered.

Nails are the better of the two, and the numbers are specific. One study extracted DNA from 433 nail samples taken from 113 subjects, 108 living and 5 deceased, with yields ranging from 2 nanograms to 2,295 nanograms. That is a thousand-fold spread between the worst and best sample. The authors built age estimation on a deliberately small panel: 15 CpG sites across four markers, chosen because the material is limited. They also argued nails resist decay comparatively well, which matters when post-mortem degradation is the obstacle. LABEL: this work is a PREPRINT, not a journal publication.

Hair is harder, and the reason is the hair itself. DNA in hair is extensively degraded by keratinisation, the process that makes hair hair. One plucked-hair study analysing 10 loci succeeded in 31 of 49 individuals, and the analysis was restricted to hairs that still had a root carrying intact cells. A shed hair shaft with no root is poor material.

Heat makes it worse, measurably. Studies on thermally treated nails report that greater heat exposure was associated with more allele drop-out and more partial profiles. A separate study of DNA recovered from nail tissue describes it as highly fragmented, comparable to degraded cell-free DNA.

The thousand-fold spread is the practical finding, not a footnote. A yield range of 2 to 2,295 nanograms across 433 samples means you cannot know in advance which kind of sample you have. Two clippings from two people, collected the same way on the same day, can differ by three orders of magnitude in how much DNA they give up. Any claim about what "a nail sample" supports is a claim about an average that almost no individual sample is.

Forensic practice has known the hair problem for years. Reviews of DNA and protein analysis in forensic genetics describe hair as an unfavourable sample for DNA work precisely because of keratinisation, which is why forensic labs reach for the root, or for mitochondrial DNA, rather than treating a shaft as ordinary material.

One genuinely new direction, flagged as early. A 2025 paper reported that fragmentation patterns in rootless hair correlate with methylation at CpG sites, raising the possibility of inferring methylation from where the DNA broke rather than from bisulfite conversion. That is an early-stage finding and nothing to plan around.

What this adds up to, stated without a sales angle. Small targeted panels on nail material are feasible in a laboratory today. General-purpose epigenetic clocks on keratin are not a consumer product and nobody is offering one. The honest summary is that the material may carry a small amount of readable information, under good conditions, with a wide failure rate.

And the part that applies to anything stored for the long term. Every one of those figures was measured on fresh or recently collected material. None of them tells you what the same sample supports after twenty years at room temperature, because nobody has run that experiment. Anyone who tells you otherwise, including anyone selling storage, is extrapolating.

MySpawn stores keratin. We make no claim that a stored sample will support any of the above, and the reason is in the paragraph before this one.
