#157 [X] The six-day rule almost nobody knows exists

The person who buries you can lose that right to a calendar.

Texas writes its answer down. Health and Safety Code section 711.002 sets who controls the disposition of remains, including cremation, unless the person left written directions. The order: someone named in a signed written instrument, then the surviving spouse, then a surviving adult child, then either surviving parent, then a surviving adult sibling, then the executor or administrator, then any adult in the next degree of kinship in the order the law names to inherit.

Read the first item again. A named person outranks the spouse. Not as an override you file later. As the top of the list.

Now the part that catches people.

Subsection (a-1): a person who fails to make final arrangements, or to appoint someone else to make them, before the earlier of the sixth day after receiving notice of the death or the tenth day after the death, is presumed unable or unwilling, and the right passes to the next person on the list.

Six days from notice. Ten from death. Whichever comes first.

Nobody has to act in bad faith for that to bite. A spouse in shock, a flight from overseas, a hospital that takes four days to reach anyone: the statute does not ask why. It runs.

Three things this means in practice.

One: the document beats the relationship. If you want a specific person deciding, name them in the instrument your state recognises. In Texas that puts them above everyone, including a spouse.

Two: speed is a legal fact, not just a courtesy. Someone has to know a clock exists before they can beat it. Most families find out afterwards.

Three: the right can be given away on purpose. A person holding it can waive it so the next person acts, which is how families handle the common case where the eldest child lives two thousand miles away. Waiving is a choice. Being presumed unwilling after six days is not.

Four: this is one state. Florida handles the same question through a definitional provision, section 497.005, which sets its own order of priority. Different code, different structure, same shape: a ranked list of people who never agreed with each other about anything.

California also runs a defined order of priority for control of funeral, burial or cremation arrangements. We are not printing that list, because our search returned the existence of the order and not its contents, and a half-remembered priority list is worse than none.

What it is not. It is not inheritance. Who buries you and who inherits from you are separate questions under separate statutes, and in plenty of families they are separate people.

Epistemic status: statute, read through a search index. Section 711.002 has been amended repeatedly, including by H.B. 3070 in 2015 and S.B. 1139 effective 1 September 2021. Check the current official text before relying on either the order or the deadline.

MySpawn stores a biological record, $99 a year, billed annually. It is not a legal instrument and it creates authority over nothing. The paperwork above is the part that assigns authority, and no storage company can write it for you.

#158 [FACEBOOK] Three states, three different answers to the same question

Here is a question with no national answer: if you could not speak for yourself tomorrow, who decides?

Most people assume there is a list, and that it is roughly the same everywhere. There is a list. It is not the same everywhere, and the differences are not small print.

Washington, under RCW 7.70.065 as amended in 2022, places a court-appointed guardian above the health care agent you named yourself, and ends its list with an adult close friend.

New York, under the Family Health Care Decisions Act, ranks a guardian, then spouse or domestic partner, then adult child, then parent, and permits only one surrogate at a time.

California, after amendments in 2022, has no legally mandated hierarchy of default decision makers at all, though the provider must choose from a defined set of persons.

One state can rank a court's appointee over your own choice. One insists on a single decision maker. One declines to rank.

The common pattern, where there is one, runs spouse unless divorced or legally separated, then adult child, then parent, then adult sibling, then other relatives, and in some states a close friend. A health care agent you named generally outranks all of it, acting within the scope of the document. A guardian generally does too, within the scope of the order.

What happens when two people tie. Statutes prefer consensus. Some let a clinician rely on a majority decision among surrogates of equal rank, which is the most common dispute-resolution provision. So three adult children at two against one can produce a decision the third never agreed to, and nothing about that is a malfunction.

And here is the part that surprises people most. Under HIPAA, a personal representative is the person with authority under applicable state law to make health care decisions for the patient. So the records question and the decision question have the same answer. Whoever state law puts at the top generally acquires the patient's own rights of access. The route to medical records is the decision-making authority, not the family tie.

Note what sits above the defaults, because this is the lever. An agent you named in an advance directive generally outranks the whole list, acting within the scope of the document. A guardian acts within the scope of the court order. In most states the document you can write this afternoon beats the statute you cannot change. Washington is the exception that proves why you check your own state rather than an article.

One more omission worth saying out loud: unmarried partners generally hold no automatic standing in most states. Twenty years together, no marriage, no paperwork, and a sibling nobody has spoken to since 2009 can rank higher.

Epistemic status. The state summaries come from a 2023 American Bar Association update on default surrogate statutes and from clinical reference material, read through a search index rather than opened at source. The ABA's broader overview of surrogate consent laws dates from 2014 and is too old to support any current ranking, which is why it is named here and not used. Statutes change without telling you.

The fix is boring and it works: name an agent, in writing, and tell them.

So, genuinely: do you know who is on your list, or are you assuming?

#159 [X] The best sentence we read this month has no villain in it

ZERO PRODUCT MENTION. Nothing is being sold in this article.

Over the 2023 Christmas holidays, a freezer failure at a major research institute warmed 16 cryogenic tanks.

The institution's own published estimate of what was destroyed: 34,400 biobank samples, 3,800 samples from animal models, 2,600 from cell lines and 6,300 from manipulated or edited cell lines.

Then it published its internal review. One line from it:

the failure "did not occur due to any single event or source of error, but was the result of multiple issues."

Sit with that.

No single event. No single source of error. No one thing to point at, nobody to fire, no tidy story. An external review was to follow, led by a biobank professional from another institution.

For contrast, here is what the same category of failure usually looks like in the documents. In two US fertility-clinic tank incidents, both facilities had remote alarm systems. In at least one, the alarm may have been switched off, and the clinic's own letter said nobody knew who had disabled it or for how long. In one case the facility already knew the tank needed preventative maintenance and was troubleshooting it with the manufacturer.

And the quiet version is worse than the headline version. A cord blood bank reported that temperature excursions had gone undetected in seven of its twenty-two storage tanks. Not a catastrophe. About a third of the estate drifting with nobody aware.

That sentence is much harder to write than a denial, and it is the only kind of sentence anyone learns from. A sector that hides its failures produces no case studies at all, which is precisely why the failures you can read about come from the organisations that take them seriously enough to investigate and publish.

The reason that matters is that an external review was promised rather than avoided, led by a biobank professional from a different institution. A self-investigation that invites an outsider in is a materially different object from one that does not, and it is the single cheapest signal of seriousness available to any custodian.

Which produces an uncomfortable inversion. The institutions you can find failure reports about are, on average, the ones behaving best. The absence of a published post-mortem is not evidence that nothing has ever gone wrong. It is evidence of nothing at all.

Three labels on this, all of which matter.

It is not American, and nothing in it should be read as a claim about US institutions or US law. It is cited here as an engineering and governance fact.

The counts are the institution's own estimate, and are written here as an estimate rather than as a finding by anyone else.

And the institution published them itself. We are not naming it, deliberately. Naming an organisation for the disclosure it volunteered is how you teach every other organisation not to volunteer one.

The useful question this leaves you with is not whether a custodian has ever failed. It is whether they have ever written down a failure where the answer was "multiple issues" and the villain was nobody.

Epistemic status: institutional self-published review, reported figures are the institution's own estimate, read through a search index.

#160 [FACEBOOK] It is not the DNA that goes first

Ask people what degrades in a stored biological sample and almost everyone says the DNA. The data says otherwise, and the gap matters if you are deciding what is worth keeping.

A reproductive biobank looked at specimens stored for over 11 years. DNA integrity held up. RNA quality declined with storage time, enough that the authors recommended using samples stored within about three years when high-quality RNA matters. They also found storage duration affected endocrine marker levels.

Then there is the long one. In the DiPiS study, DNA was extracted from 1,012 capillary blood samples stored at minus 20 degrees Celsius for 7 to 21 years. 75.7 percent met quality standards for quantity (at least 20 nanograms per microlitre) and purity (A260/280 between 1.7 and 1.9). The best-performing group was not the youngest: the 12-year samples, at 83.5 percent.

Read that again, because it is the opposite of what intuition predicts. Across a 14-year spread, age was not the dominant variable. Collection and handling were doing more work than the calendar.

And then the number that should temper the good news. Of 270 samples assessed for integrity, 57.8 percent had a DNA Integrity Number of 7 or higher. So roughly four in ten cleared quantity and purity and still came up short on fragment integrity.

That is the real lesson, and it is a measurement lesson. Quantity and purity are easy to check and reassuring. Integrity is the harder measurement and the one that tells you whether long fragments survived. Passing the easy tests is not the same as being intact. We wrote up how those three measurements differ at myspawn.me/blog/is-stored-dna-still-usable, and this is the first time we have had a real multi-decade cohort to put next to it.

One 2026 data point, for currency rather than for DNA. A PLOS ONE study published in June 2026 compared 200 long-term cryopreserved serum samples against 200 short-term ones, drawn from the Shenzhen Chronic Disease Risk Factor Surveillance Project, 400 samples in total, looking at the serum proteome and metallome. It is about proteins and metals, not nucleic acid, and it is here because it is the most recent work our sweep found on what storage duration does to a sample.

For completeness, the other direction. Blood RNA collected in stabilising tubes stayed stable up to six years at minus 80 degrees, though some transcript levels shifted. That is a 2014 result and is flagged as old.

Epistemic status: peer-reviewed. The reproductive biobank study is 2024. The DiPiS figures are peer-reviewed but their publication year was not confirmed in our search, and they are presented here without one rather than with a guess. The RNA tube study is 2014 and labelled old. All read through a search index, none opened at its publisher.

What it adds up to. DNA is the durable part of a biological sample. RNA, proteins and hormone markers are not, and their clocks run faster. If someone is selling you long-horizon storage, the honest version of the pitch is narrow: the nucleic acid is the thing with a long shelf life, and the rest of the tube is a different problem with a different answer.

What would you want kept, if only the most durable part survived?

#161 [X] Cold is a mechanism, not a synonym for safe

Declare the interest first, because it is real: we sell ambient-temperature storage. Discount everything below accordingly, then check the sources.

The intuition is that colder equals safer. It is doing more work in people's heads than it can carry.

Here is the measurement problem underneath it. A 2019 study in the Journal of Assisted Reproduction and Genetics compared a weight-based tank alarm against a temperature-based one. An intact cryogenic tank lost about ten percent of its liquid nitrogen in 4.2 to 4.9 days. Warming to minus 185 degrees Celsius took 37.8 to 43.7 days.

Put those side by side. The liquid starts going in days. The thermometer reports it in weeks. And then it moves fast, because once only a few centimetres remain at the bottom there is nothing left holding the cold.

So the instrument most alarms use is the instrument that reports last. It is a fuel gauge that reads full until the tank is empty.

Cold is not a state. It is a continuously maintained process with an inventory that boils off by design, a top-up schedule, a vacuum that can degrade invisibly, and an alarm chain that has to reach a human at 3am on a public holiday. Every one of those is a thing that can fail.

Now the part that stops this being a cheap shot at cryogenics. Cryogenic storage exists because living cells need it. Eggs, embryos, sperm and cell lines are not durable dry; the cold is doing real and irreplaceable work. A dry, sealed DNA record is a different preservation problem with a different answer, and anyone flattening those two into one argument, including us, is selling rather than explaining.

The regulator-documented version of the same design flaw is not about samples at all. In a 2021 Georgia incident investigated by the US Chemical Safety Board, a failed liquid-nitrogen level-control system allowed an overflow and six workers died. The Board's findings named the single point of failure and the absence of atmospheric oxygen monitoring and alarms. We cite it once and carefully: it is a worker-safety case, not a sample-loss case, and it is not being used as a metaphor. It is here because it is the clearest official statement that a safety system depending on one sensor is not a safety system.

One more failure mode that has no sensor at all. A cryogenic dewar holds its contents cold the way a thermos does, with a vacuum between two walls. That vacuum can degrade, clinic-focused sources note that heavy handling makes it more likely, and a tank losing vacuum does not look different from the outside.

What is actually transferable is not a conclusion about temperature. It is one question, and it works on any custodian of anything:

What does your alarm measure, and how long is the lag between the thing going wrong and the alarm noticing?

If the answer is temperature only, ask what the expected lag is. If nobody can tell you, you have learned something, and it is not about chemistry.

Epistemic status: peer-reviewed bench study, 2019, read through a search index. The numbers are from an intact tank under test conditions and are not a prediction about any specific facility.
