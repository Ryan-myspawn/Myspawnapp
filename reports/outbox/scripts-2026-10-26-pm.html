X / TWEET DRAFTS (3)

1.

A published price is not really information about cost.

It is a commitment that the same thing costs the same to everyone, and a stranger can check it in four seconds.

An unpublished price moves the work onto you, and makes your leverage a variable. Ask who got the good one.

2.

"Your data is safe" has two meanings.

One is what you permitted. The other is how well the systems holding it are built.

They are different promises and they get the same word. Ask a custodian about both, separately, and see which answer comes slower.

3.

Storage agreements get read front to back until attention runs out.

The clause that decides what actually happens to your sample is usually near the back: what occurs if the company is sold, merged or closed.

Read that one first. Then read the rest.

THREADS-STYLE TEXT POSTS (2)

1.

I spent an hour today trying to find out what a company in an adjacent business charges. Not a competitor, and I am not going to name them.

There is no price on their site. What exists instead is five different figures from five different places, none of them from the company: fifty thousand for the full product, twenty thousand for a cut-down version, about twenty-five thousand described as what customers actually pay on average, and a per-embryo number from one person, once, which the person reporting it flagged as a single anecdote.

Here is what I want to be careful about. Those numbers disagreeing is not the interesting part. Companies price different products differently and that is ordinary.

The interesting part is the sixth figure. A news organisation reported that the company charged one couple the full fifty thousand and then gave them half off, in exchange for agreeing to share information about their future children.

I am not going after that couple. They made a private decision that is none of my business, and I am not claiming this is anyone's general policy.

But once a price is negotiable, it stops being a price. It becomes a question about what else you have that they want. And in that particular trade, the thing being spent belongs to someone who does not exist yet and cannot be asked.

Our number is ninety-nine dollars a year, billed annually, and it is on the page.

2.

A small distinction that I think explains most of the confusion in this category.

When a company tells you your genetic data is safe, that sentence is doing two completely different jobs and nobody separates them for you.

Job one is consent. What did you actually permit? Research use, sharing with third parties, what happens on withdrawal. That is a question about the agreement.

Job two is security. How well is the thing holding it built? Since January 2025, institutions handling genomic data under the NIH sharing policy have had to meet a specific standard called NIST SP 800-171, with additional access terms, mandatory training and prompt incident reporting.

That was a security change. It was not a consent change. I have watched both get described with the same word in marketing copy for months.

So ask both questions, separately. What am I allowed to do with it, and what standard are your systems held to. Watch which one takes longer to answer.

For the record: we store physical samples rather than running a genomic database, so that particular standard is not ours to claim and I am not claiming it. The question still works on us.
