What Is In Vitro Maturation (IVM)? The Egg Technology Behind a $1.38 Billion Deal

The one adjacent fact already published is a single FAQ answer ("Is Fertilo the same thing as IVG?") inside the 27 September article, which this piece links to rather than repeats.

Primary audience: Men 25-55 (written to be read by anyone weighing fertility options)

Trend-hook used: Ferring Pharmaceuticals agreed to acquire Gameto for up to $1.3825 billion, announced 27 October 2026.

In vitro maturation, or IVM, is a fertility technique in which immature eggs are retrieved from the ovaries and finished in a laboratory dish rather than in the body. It is not experimental: the American Society for Reproductive Medicine removed that label in its committee opinion on IVM. It is also not as effective as conventional IVF in the published comparisons, and it is not the same thing as making an egg from a skin cell. Those three sentences are the whole article, and the rest is the evidence for them.

Three different things that get called the same thing

Coverage of fertility technology collapses three categories that behave differently.

Conventional IVF. The patient takes injected hormones for roughly ten to fourteen days so that many follicles mature inside the ovary. Mature eggs are then retrieved. The maturation happens in the body.

In vitro maturation. The patient takes a much shorter course of hormones, or sometimes none. Immature eggs are retrieved and matured in a dish. The maturation happens outside the body. This is IVM, and it has been in clinical use for years.

In vitro gametogenesis. A gamete is created from a cell that was never a gamete, such as a skin or blood cell. This has produced no mature human eggs or sperm in anyone's laboratory. We cover it separately in What Is IVG? In Vitro Gametogenesis, Explained in Plain English and in In Vitro Gametogenesis, Explained: How Close Are Lab-Grown Eggs and Sperm, Really?.

The deal announced on 27 October concerns the second category. Almost every headline you will read about it borrows the vocabulary of the third.

What IVM actually involves

In a standard cycle, an egg completes its final maturation inside the follicle under hormonal signals, reaching the stage where it can be fertilised. IVM moves that last step into the laboratory. Immature cumulus-oocyte complexes are collected and cultured in media designed to carry them through to maturity, after which they can be fertilised as in ordinary IVF.

The appeal is straightforward: less hormonal stimulation, shorter cycles, lower drug cost, and a reduced risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome. The ASRM committee opinion is explicit that this is a real technique with real candidates, and equally explicit that it is not applicable to every patient. Its stated candidate profile is patients with a high antral follicle count. If your follicle count is low, the published guidance does not point you here.

The same opinion attaches two conditions that rarely survive into consumer coverage: clinicians offering IVM should have specific specialised training, and patients should receive counselling about expected results before consenting. Those are not throat-clearing. They exist because the results differ from what patients assume.

How well does IVM work, in numbers

This is where honesty costs something, so here are the figures as published rather than as marketed.

In a retrospective study of 919 women with high antral follicle counts, the cumulative live birth rate after one cycle was 239 of 608, or 39.3 per cent, for IVM, against 155 of 311, or 49.8 per cent, for conventional IVF. IVM worked. It worked less often.

The ASRM opinion also notes substantial attrition between retrieved immature complexes and good-quality blastocysts, driven partly by low maturation success in the dish. Expressed per immature egg, the chance that a single immature oocyte results in a live birth was 1.1 per cent. That number is not a scandal, because a cycle retrieves many eggs, but it is the number that explains why counselling is mandated.

An older comparison reported in a review gives clinical pregnancy and live birth rates per retrieval of 26.2 per cent and 15.9 per cent for IVM against 38.3 per cent and 26.2 per cent for IVF, with the caveat, stated in the source, that those differences were not statistically significant.

Two further limits matter if preservation rather than immediate treatment is your interest. Vitrified-thawed IVM eggs performed worse than fresh ones in a study the ASRM cites, with a survival rate of 59.8 per cent and pregnancy rates of 10.7 per cent against 36.1 per cent. And the ASRM states that vitrification of immature oocytes is not recommended, on the basis of reported poor outcomes.

Label, stated plainly: the ASRM committee opinion and the published comparisons above are peer-reviewed or society guidance. Everything in the next section that comes from a company is marked as such.

One honest inconsistency: the ASRM's own framing is not uniform across its documents. The IVM committee opinion says the technique is no longer experimental, while its 2026 committee opinion on fertility preservation for medically indicated patients describes IVM as an emerging strategy. We are not going to reconcile those for you. Both are the society's words.

What Fertilo adds, and what its trial is actually testing

Fertilo is Gameto's product. It uses ovarian support cells engineered from induced pluripotent stem cells to mature retrieved eggs outside the body. The stem cells are in the support system, not in the egg. The egg is the patient's own throughout.

The pivotal US study, registered as NCT06858111 and referred to as FIRST, is described by the sponsor as the first randomised, controlled, double-blind trial of egg maturation outside the body in the United States. A September 2026 registry update is reported to list recruitment, an estimated 500 participants, and primary completion expected May 2027, with up to twenty US sites.

The comparator is the detail worth pausing on. FIRST compares Fertilo against MediCult IVM, a standard maturation-media system. The trial is not asking whether maturing eggs outside the body works. It is asking whether this way of doing it beats the existing way. That framing is the strongest available evidence that IVM is the category in question, and it comes from the trial design itself.

Status, as of this writing: Fertilo has no FDA marketing approval. The trial is still recruiting. The interim readout the company guided to for late 2026 has not been published. Trade coverage reports clearance in Australia and several Latin American markets, and a first birth in Lima in December 2024. Label: company and trade press, read through a search index. We did not open the registry record itself, and the registry is the authority for arms, eligibility and endpoints. Sources also disagree on the primary endpoint, some describing pregnancy at twelve weeks and others ongoing pregnancy with live birth secondary. That disagreement is unresolved here rather than averaged away.

A preclinical comparison reported 1.5 times the maturation rate and more than 2.5 times the formation of chromosomally normal embryos against commercial IVM media. Label: sponsor claim, from an abbreviated-stimulation cohort.

What the acquisition does and does not tell you

On 27 October 2026, Ferring Pharmaceuticals agreed to acquire Gameto for up to $1.3825 billion. Reporting is consistent on three qualifiers that headlines drop:

  • The upfront payment was not disclosed. The headline figure includes payments tied to development, regulatory and sales milestones that have not occurred.
  • The transaction has not closed, and the companies operate independently until it does.
  • The product it centres on is not approved in the United States.

A valuation is a market's view of a future. It is not a clinical result, and it is not a regulatory decision. Label: wire and trade press (Bloomberg's report is dated 27 October 2026; BioPharma Dive, Endpoints News and pharmaphorum carry the same figure). No outlet was opened at its own domain for this article and no company release was seen.

There is one further detail almost nobody will mention. In January 2026 the acquired company licensed meiosis-induction intellectual property from a Harvard laboratory. Meiosis is the cell division that produces an egg or a sperm, and it is the step that has not been completed in a human dish. So the buyer acquired a maturation therapy that exists and a licence pointed at a step that does not. Label: company announcement and an intellectual-property transaction, not a laboratory result. Both statements are true, and they are not the same claim.

What IVM is not

IVM does not create an egg. It finishes one that the patient already had. If a headline suggests that eggs were grown from nothing, or built from skin cells, it has crossed into the third category, and the correct response is to check which story the writer was in. Nobody has produced a mature human egg or sperm from a cell that was not one. We will say so on the day that changes, and not before.

FAQ

Is in vitro maturation experimental?

The ASRM committee opinion on IVM states that the technology is no longer considered experimental. Its 2026 committee opinion on fertility preservation for medically indicated patients describes IVM as an emerging strategy, so the society's framing is not identical across documents.

Is IVM as successful as IVF?

In the published comparisons, no. One retrospective study of 919 women with high antral follicle counts reported cumulative live birth rates of 39.3 per cent for IVM against 49.8 per cent for IVF after one cycle.

Who is a candidate for IVM?

The ASRM opinion states that IVM is not applicable to every patient and points to patients with a high antral follicle count. It also recommends that clinicians offering it have specific specialised training and that patients receive counselling about expected results.

Is Fertilo approved by the FDA?

No. Fertilo has no FDA marketing approval. Its US Phase 3 trial, NCT06858111, is still running, and the interim readout guided to for late 2026 has not been published.

Does the Ferring acquisition mean Fertilo works?

No. The agreement, announced on 27 October 2026, is worth up to $1.3825 billion including unachieved milestones, the upfront payment was not disclosed, and the transaction has not closed. A valuation is not a clinical result.

Can immature eggs be frozen for later?

The ASRM states that vitrification of immature oocytes is not recommended, citing reports of poor outcomes, and notes that vitrified-thawed IVM eggs performed worse than fresh ones in a cited study.
