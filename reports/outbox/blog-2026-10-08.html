Short answer: you have three separate things, and most people only realise they are separate after they have lost one of them. There is the account, which a company controls and which usually requires a death certificate and executor paperwork to reach. The three things, in the order they disappear

1. The raw data file. A text file of genotype calls. If anyone can still log in, download it today. 23andMe's own process requires confirming the date of birth on the profile and says the file usually takes about a week and can take up to thirty days ([23andMe support](https://support.23andme.org/hc/en-us/articles/42965156401687-Accessing-Your-Raw-Genetic-Data)). That timeline matters: a thirty-day job started after an account is closed is a job that never finishes.

2. The account. Companies have a formal process and it is slower. Ancestry publishes the clearest one.

3. The physical sample. For most consumer tests, gone. This is covered in [what happens to my DNA sample if I cancel](what-happens-dna-sample-if-i-cancel), and the short version is that destruction schedules are set by the company and rarely by you. Nothing in this article recovers it. A sample that was not deliberately preserved while someone was alive cannot be created afterwards.

Getting into an account you do not have the password for

Ancestry's published process has three routes ([Ancestry help](https://support.ancestry.com/s/article/Managing-a-Deceased-Person-s-Account?language=en_US)):

  • If you can reach the email address on the account, send the request from that address. Ancestry describes this as the simplest option.
  • Without email access, Ancestry asks for a copy of the death certificate plus legal documentation that you are the executor: a Letter of Appointment, Letter of Administration, Letter of Representation, Letters Testamentary, or a copy of the will. A non-executor also needs written authorisation from the executor.
  • If a Legacy Contact was set up, that contact can take over the account on proof of death, in the form of a death certificate or published obituary.

Once you are in, you can download family trees, DNA data, and notes or files saved to the trees.

23andMe is less clear, and we are going to say so rather than guess. Older reporting quotes the company saying it can only release genetic information to an Executor, Personal Representative or Beneficiary of the estate, with an authorisation form and legal documentation. The same reporting said there was no option to name a posthumous data manager, and we could not confirm whether that is still true. Every 23andMe source located in this sweep predates the company's bankruptcy news, so treat its current terms as unverified and contact the company directly.

Two practical notes that apply whichever company it is. Ask specifically for the team that handles deceased account holders. And expect to need a certified copy of the death certificate rather than a photocopy.

The part almost nobody is told: those results are partly about you

A parent passes roughly half of their autosomal DNA to each child. So a parent's genetic results are not only a document about a person who has died. They are a partial document about everyone descended from them. That is not a metaphor; it is the reason clinical genetics has spent decades arguing about it.

What the professional guidance actually says. Clinicians have an ethical duty to tell the patient, both before testing and on receiving results, that the information may have familial implications ([American Journal of Human Genetics](https://www.cell.com/ajhg/fulltext/S0002-9297(07)63510-1)). The AMA Code expects counselling to cover what results mean for a patient's biological relatives.

Courts have gone further in at least one state. In Pate v. Threlkel, the Florida Supreme Court held that the duty extends to informing the patient's children, and that it is satisfied by warning the patient of the familial implications ([AMA Journal of Ethics](https://journalofethics.ama-assn.org/article/physicians-duty-warn-third-parties-hereditary-risk/2009-09)). A later reading suggests the duty might extend beyond the patient and might not be satisfied by telling the patient alone. Label this honestly: the legal position depends on jurisdiction and on facts, and this is commentary rather than a rule you can rely on.

Three limits, and they protect people rather than companies.

  • Relatives have a right NOT to know. Telling a sibling what a dead parent's results say can conflict with that sibling's autonomy. It is their decision, not yours, and the considerate move is to offer rather than announce.
  • There is little support for warning underage family members about adult-onset conditions. A result that matters at fifty does not need to be handed to a fourteen-year-old.
  • Confidentiality survives death. HIPAA keeps a patient's information confidential for FIFTY YEARS after death, with release to a personal representative, usually the executor. No disclosure happens unless that representative affirmatively requests it; a physician cannot volunteer it ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7683580/)).

That fifty-year figure is worth sitting with, and we flagged the opposite of it yesterday. HIPAA sets no retention period at all for how long your medical records must be kept, which is left to state law. It does set a fifty-year confidentiality period after death. So the federal rule says almost nothing about whether the record will still exist and a great deal about who may read it if it does. Those are not the same question, and conflating them is how people end up surprised twice.

What to do this month, in order

  1. Download the raw data today if anyone can still sign in. Start it now; the clock is up to thirty days.
  2. Store two copies in different places, at least one of them not on a phone.
  3. Find out whether a Legacy Contact exists before you start assembling paperwork. It is much faster.
  4. If not, get a certified death certificate and the estate letters before contacting support, not after.
  5. Separate the genealogy from the health content. The family tree is for everyone. The medical findings are not, and should go to a genetic counsellor before they go to a group chat.
  6. Ask a genetic counsellor what, if anything, is actionable for living relatives. Most findings are not. Some are, and those are worth real money and real time.
  7. Offer, do not announce. Tell siblings the results exist and that you will share them if they want. Then stop talking.
  8. Write down what you did, because somebody will ask you in five years and you will not remember.

FAQ

Can I access my deceased parent's DNA test account?

Usually yes, with paperwork. Ancestry accepts a request sent from the email address on the account, or a death certificate plus executor documentation such as Letters Testamentary or a copy of the will. If a Legacy Contact was named, that person can take over on proof of death. 23andMe has historically required an Executor, Personal Representative or Beneficiary with an authorisation form, though its current terms are unverified here.

Can I download a dead relative's raw DNA data?

Only if someone can still sign in, in practice. The normal download flow requires confirming the date of birth on the profile and takes about a week and sometimes up to thirty days. Start it before the account is closed, because closing is usually final.

Does a parent's DNA result tell me about my own genetics?

Partly, yes. A parent passes roughly half of their autosomal DNA to each child, which is why clinical guidance treats genetic results as having familial implications. It is not a substitute for being tested yourself, and a counsellor can say which findings are actually relevant to you.

Am I obliged to tell my siblings what the results say?

There is no general legal duty on you, and there are good reasons for care. Relatives have a right not to know, and professional guidance gives little support for warning underage relatives about adult-onset conditions. Offer the information and let them decide.

Does HIPAA protect my dead parent's medical records?

Yes, for fifty years after death. Release goes through a personal representative, usually the executor, and no disclosure happens unless that representative affirmatively asks. Separately, HIPAA does not say how long the records have to be kept at all; that is state law.

Can I get a new DNA sample from a parent who has died?

No. Consumer testing samples are generally destroyed on the company's schedule, and a sample that was not deliberately preserved while someone was alive cannot be created afterwards. This is the one item on the list with no workaround.
