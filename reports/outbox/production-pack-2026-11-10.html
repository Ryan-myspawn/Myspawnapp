STANCE: report the numbers, do not round them, do not dramatise them.

  • The International Society of Uterus Transplantation published its second registry report, covering international activity from 2000 to 2024. (Peer-reviewed journal report, Human Reproduction, DOI 10.1093/humrep/deag017. LABEL SPOKEN: this is a registry report read through a search index; the paper itself was not opened.)
  • 91 uterus transplantations: 67 from living donors, 24 from deceased donors.

  • 28 women had live births from living-donor uteri. 8 from deceased-donor uteri.
  • CLIP MOMENT: "Ninety-one operations, in twenty-four years, worldwide. That is the whole field. It is the size of a large school year group."

Segment 2: The number nobody has

STANCE: this is the segment the episode exists for, and it is an honest mess rather than a scandal.

  • A JAMA study states plainly that no registry precisely reports the number of live births worldwide, and estimates more than 70. (Peer-reviewed journal, LABEL SPOKEN.)
  • The same source gives 48 uterus transplants and 33 live births in the United States as of 1 May 2024.
  • AND THOSE TWO DO NOT OBVIOUSLY RECONCILE. The international registry counts 91 worldwide across 24 years. The JAMA figure counts 48 in the US alone. We are not going to make them agree on air. Different inclusion criteria and different cut-off dates are the innocent explanation and we cannot confirm it from here.
  • A 2023 review of the first 24 published deceased-donor cases reports 12 live births, a 66 per cent birth rate, and 25 per cent graft loss during follow-up. A second count inside the same review gives 50 per cent. (Review article, LABEL SPOKEN, including the internal inconsistency.)
  • CLIP MOMENT: "A field this small, this well documented, this carefully studied, and you still cannot get a clean worldwide birth count. That is not negligence. That is what the edge of medicine actually looks like from the outside."

Segment 3: Living donor, deceased donor, and why it matters

STANCE: a real trade-off, stated without picking a side.

  • One review reports surgical success at 78 per cent for living-donor procedures against 64 per cent for deceased-donor. (Review, LABEL SPOKEN, single source.)
  • Shorter total ischaemic time is reported for living-donor procedures, which is the mechanical reason behind the gap.
  • The counterweight, stated on air: a living donor is a healthy person undergoing major surgery for somebody else's benefit. A better success rate that is paid for by a second person on an operating table is not a free improvement, and anyone quoting the 78 against the 64 without saying so is quoting half a number.
  • The firsts, for the record: first living-donor birth September 2014, from a Swedish team. First deceased-donor birth December 2017, University of São Paulo, Brazil. NO INDIVIDUAL IS NAMED ON AIR.

  • The general form: when you are pleased with a distinction, count its terms. A clean comparison between a fake and a limitation is exactly the shape that hides a third thing.
