#167 [X] The rule that changed for your genetic data, and the part nobody mentions

Science and trust explainer. Light product mention.

On 25 January 2025, the rules changed for anyone handling human genomic data out of an NIH controlled-access repository.

Approved users now have to handle that data under NIST SP 800-171, a federal security standard. It is a contractual obligation, not a suggestion. Approved users must attest that their institution complies, and that attestation extends to third-party IT systems and cloud service providers.

The scope is specific: human genomic data accessed from an NIH controlled-access repository, applied to both competing and continuation proposals.

(Epistemic status: official policy update, reported consistently across multiple university research-office summaries dated January 2025. Links via search index: verify before posting.)

Here is the part that gets lost.

That was a security change. It was not a consent change.

Those are two different promises and they get the same word in marketing copy constantly. Consent is what you permitted: research use, sharing, what happens if you withdraw. Security is how well the thing holding your data is built. A company can be flawless on one and silent on the other, and the sentence "your data is safe" covers both without telling you which it means.

Three more facts, because the detail is where this gets interesting.

One. It is not retroactive. Approved users operating under Data Use Agreements signed before 25 January 2025 continue under the old terms until renewal. So two labs holding comparable data can be under different security obligations today, purely because of when they signed.

Two. The sector asked for more time and did not get it. The Council on Governmental Relations asked NIH to extend the effective date. NIH declined. What NIH did allow, per its FAQs, is for institutions to deviate from specific controls where a Plan of Action and Milestones is in place, pointing to NIST 800-171 section 03.11.04 for partially implemented controls.

Three, and this is the one to sit with. One summary reports that NIH is not currently planning to actively audit compliance.

And it has kept moving since. A further notice, NOT-OD-25-159, dated 24 September 2025, set required security and operational standards for NIH controlled-access data repositories themselves. Separately, NIH ran a request for information on proposed revisions to the sharing policy, NOT-OD-26-023, whose comment period closed on 18 March 2026. As far as I can verify, the resulting changes are unpublished, so nobody can yet tell you what the next version requires.

So: a binding contractual standard, no extension, a documented deviation path, and no active audit. That is not a scandal. It is how a lot of compliance actually works. But it means the honest question to ask a custodian is not "are you compliant", because the answer is always yes. It is "who checked, and when, and can I see it."

We store physical samples rather than running a genomic database, so this particular standard is not ours to claim and we are not claiming it. The question still works on us.

#168 [FACEBOOK] A public lab just told people it is throwing their DNA away, and the reason is shelf space

Trust and custody, story-driven. ZERO PRODUCT MENTION. Ends with a question.

I want to tell you about a document almost nobody will read.

On 16 March 2026, Alberta Precision Laboratories published a laboratory bulletin. The Molecular Genetics Laboratory, North Sector, announced it would discard residual DNA from samples received between September 2017 and December 2022.

The stated reason was space constraints.

That window covers 64 months of samples, a little over five years of people walking into a clinic and giving something up.

(Epistemic status: official institutional bulletin, dated. Canadian, not American, and I am not presenting it as a US practice. Link via search index: verify before posting.)

This was not sudden. It is the fourth step in a sequence, and the sequence is the story.

October 2020: the same lab announced it would no longer extract and store DNA from specimens arriving without a test ordered, except in limited cases such as where a sample could not be recollected or could only be collected with great difficulty.

June 2022: it stopped storing paternal blood samples for prenatal chromosomal microarray testing, on the grounds that they had rarely been required.

April 2024: it published retention periods. Long-term DNA storage, 25 years. Short-term, 2 years. And a sentence I think everyone should read at least once: the lab does not provide biorepository services, also known as banking, and does not have the infrastructure to routinely store residual specimens long term.

March 2026: the 2017 to 2022 material goes. Samples received from January 2023 onward are not in this batch; they fall under the April 2024 rules instead, which means the retention question for them was already settled before anyone asked it.

There are exceptions, and they tell you what a system decides is worth keeping. DNA is held for 25 years from collection, where enough remains, if banking was specifically ordered, or if the sample is fetal tissue, umbilical cord or placenta, certain amniotic fluid or chorionic villus samples, or was taken for ALS, frontotemporal dementia, mitochondrial disorder or inherited cancer testing.

Read that list again. Everything on it is either a sample that cannot be taken again, or a disease where someone might need to go back. That is a defensible rule. It is also a rule, written by people with a finite number of freezers, about somebody else's biological material.

I am not criticising the lab. They published it. They gave their reasons. They set out the exceptions in writing and they did it in advance. That is considerably more than most organisations holding biological material ever do.

What stays with me is simpler. Most people whose samples are in that date range have no idea any of this happened, because a laboratory bulletin is written for clinicians, not for patients.

If a sample of yours is sitting somewhere right now, do you actually know who decides how long it stays, and whether anyone would tell you before it went?

#169 [X] Once a price is negotiable, it stops being a price

Contrarian discourse take, punching at a MECHANISM. No company named, no individual named.

I spent part of a day trying to find out what one company in the genetic-testing world charges. Not a competitor of ours. I wanted one number: a list price, or a promotional price, with a date on it.

There isn't one.

What exists instead is five different figures from five different places, none of them from the company: $50,000 for the full product, from $20,000 for a cut-down version that includes ten embryos, about $25,000 described as what customers on average actually pay, and $10,000 per embryo from one person, once, which the source reporting it flagged as a single anecdote.

(Epistemic status: all five are secondhand, from third-party guides and news coverage. Not verified at the company's own domain. The company is deliberately unnamed.)

The cut-down product is worth a second look on its own: from $20,000, including ten embryos, and excluding screening for IQ, height and ADHD. The expensive version is the one that adds those back.

One more detail, offered without snark because it is a legitimate commercial choice. The company publishes a blog explainer titled, roughly, how much does this category of testing cost. It is about the category. It is not about them.

I am not going to average them. Five numbers from five unlike sources, describing different products at different dates, averaged together, produce a sixth number that nobody has ever been charged. That is not analysis. That is arithmetic in a costume.

The disagreement is not the interesting part. Companies price different products differently and that is ordinary.

The interesting part is the sixth figure. A news organisation reported that the company charged one couple the full fifty thousand, and then gave them half off, in exchange for agreeing to share information about their future children.

(Reported by a major US broadcaster. One transaction. Not verified by us, and not presented as anyone's general policy.)

I am not going after that couple. They made a private decision that is none of my business.

The sources describe the model plainly: the company charges customers based on their individual needs, rather than against a rate card. That is a real business model and plenty of professions use it.

But look at the mechanism. A published price is not mainly information about cost. It is a commitment that the same thing costs the same to everyone, and a stranger can check it in four seconds. An unpublished price moves that work onto you, and quietly makes your leverage a variable in the transaction.

And in that particular trade, the thing being spent belongs to somebody who does not exist yet and cannot be asked.

There is a second-order effect worth naming. When a price is set per customer, the people who negotiate best pay least, and the people who are least comfortable negotiating subsidise them. That is a transfer from the diffident to the confident, and it is invisible because nobody publishes the distribution.

Our number is $99 a year, billed annually. It is on the page. It does not move if you have something we want.

#170 [FACEBOOK] We corrected our own video once. Here is the second correction

Personal essay, founder voice. Ends with a question.

Seven weeks ago we published a video explaining why we store DNA at ambient temperature instead of freezing it. Six weeks later we corrected part of it on air, because it called a preprint a peer-reviewed study and because it stretched a 28-day laboratory comparison into a claim about decades.

This week I am correcting it again, and this one is harder, because the first correction was about a date and this one is about the shape of the claim.

What we said, and still believe: ambient storage removes the power dependency. No compressor, no cold chain, nothing to fail at three in the morning. That is real.

What we also implied, and should not have: that ambient is simply fine. Settled. Equivalent.

I went looking for the independent literature behind our own claim. Three things came back.

One. An MIT spinout founded in 2021 by James Banal and Professor Mark Bathe stores DNA at room temperature in a polymer that sets into a glass-like solid. MIT reported it could store and recover all 50,000 base pairs of a human genome without damage. (MIT news office, 12 September 2025. That is institutional communications, and it is thirteen months old. Evidence, not news.)

Two. That company's own product page labels its ambient preservation reagent research use only, with a stated range of 2C to 35C. (Commercial source.)

Three, and this is the one that bites. An independent protocol study called it premature to advocate a transition to DNA storage at room temperature, and described dry storage as an additional layer of security for frozen samples rather than a replacement for them. (Peer-reviewed, and from 2013. Thirteen years old. The chemistry has moved a long way since, and I am not pretending a 2013 caution settles a 2026 question.)

So what does that add up to?

Not that we are wrong. I want to be precise, because the lazy version of this post is a company flagellating itself for engagement, and that is its own kind of dishonesty.

It adds up to this: "ambient is simply fine" is a stronger sentence than our sources support. The oldest independent source I found treats ambient as a supplement to frozen. The newest commercial product in the space is labelled research use only. Neither makes ambient wrong. Both make our phrasing too confident.

So here is the rule we have written down internally, and you can hold us to it. We will not claim ambient storage is equivalent to, or better than, frozen storage for long-term viability. We will claim what is documented: that ambient removes the power and cold-chain failure mode, and that the facility is accredited.

Storage is $99 a year, billed annually.

When a company you buy from quietly tightens one of its own claims, does that make you trust them more, or does it just make you wonder what else is loose?

#171 [X] The number that sets your probate timeline is not the one you are looking at

Practical legacy guide. Light product mention.

Everyone asks how long probate takes. The guides say six to twelve months for a simple estate, and quote national averages of about 16 months and about 20 months once contested estates are included.

Those figures disagree with each other, and that is worth knowing. The state tiers quoted run from 3 to 6 months (Colorado, Wisconsin) and 4 to 8 months (Texas, Utah, Wyoming) up to 12 to 24+ months (New York, Pennsylvania) and 9 to 18 months for California, or 18 to 24+ where the docket is backed up. Florida appears at 6 to 12 months in some guides and 9 to 18 in others. Same state, nine-month gap. Small estate procedures, where an estate qualifies, are quoted at 30 to 90 days. But there is a more useful number underneath them.

The creditor claim period.

After a death, creditors get a statutory window to bring claims against the estate. Across sources, that window commonly adds three to six months to any case.

It is not a backlog. It is not the court being slow. It is a waiting period by design, so the estate can close knowing what it owes. An executor who distributes before it expires can be personally exposed.

California's rule is the one I can state precisely: the window is generally the later of four months after first publication of the notice to creditors, or sixty days after actual notice is mailed to that creditor. After that, claims are at the court's discretion, with an outer limit of roughly one year from death.

Now a caution, because this is exactly how bad citations spread. One source attributes that four-month rule to California Probate Code section 19100. That section sits in the division governing claims against trusts, not against a decedent's estate in probate. The rule as described matches California practice. The pinpoint section number is not something I will vouch for, and nor should anyone citing this.

New York I could not pin down at all. One source says seven months from the date letters were issued. Another says six months after publication of notice. Those are different clocks starting at different events, and one of the two sources is an AI-generated answer site. Unresolved, and reported as unresolved rather than averaged.

Texas and Florida: no authoritative source found. Stated as a gap rather than filled in with something plausible.

(Epistemic status: the timeline ranges come from probate-service marketing guides, not court statistics offices. The California rule is statutory in substance. Links via search index: verify before posting.)

Why this matters more than the headline range. The creditor period is the floor under every estimate. You cannot plan under it. And it is the number almost no planning conversation ever mentions, because it is not interesting until it is the thing holding everything up.

A sample stored under a contract with a third party sits outside that clock entirely, because it is governed by the agreement rather than distributed through the estate. That is a narrow benefit and it is the only one I will claim. $99 a year, billed annually.
