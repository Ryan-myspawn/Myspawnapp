Biobank Consent: What You Are Actually Agreeing To

Short answer: a biobank consent form is not one permission, it is usually four. You are separately agreeing to storage, to research use, to sharing with third parties, and to what happens if the company is sold or closes. Most people read the first and assume the other three. The fourth is the one that has actually cost people control, and in 2025 a US bankruptcy court confirmed why.

Consent is at least four separate permissions

When you bank a biological sample, the document in front of you is doing several jobs at once. Separating them is the whole skill.

1. Storage. Permission to hold the physical material, for some period, under some conditions.

2. Research use. Permission to study it, which may be specific or open-ended.

3. Sharing. Permission to transfer it, or data derived from it, to parties you will never meet.

4. Transfer of the arrangement itself. What happens to all of the above if the company is acquired, merges, or goes through bankruptcy.

A form can be generous on one and silent on another. Silence is not a restriction. If a document does not say that your sample will not be shared, it has not said that it will not be shared.

Broad consent, and why your form probably does not use it

US research consent sits under what is called the Common Rule. The 2018 revision created a specific mechanism at 45 CFR 46.116(d) called broad consent: a way to get one open-ended permission covering the storage, maintenance and secondary research use of identifiable biospecimens, instead of returning to you for each new study.

Three things about it are worth knowing before you assume it applies to you.

It is narrower than it sounds. Broad consent is permitted only for secondary research, not for other kinds of research. It is an alternative to the standard consent requirements, not a universal licence.

It has no official form. The rule provided no template, so institutions write their own. But none of the required elements may be omitted or altered, because each is treated as essential. NIH's own sample language carries an explicit warning that it does not by itself satisfy the regulatory requirements of the broad consent provision.

Many institutions simply declined to adopt it. This is the part that surprises people. The University of North Carolina states that it has not adopted broad consent. Children's Hospital of Philadelphia lists it as not implemented institution-wide. A 2019 analysis by Holly Fernandez Lynch, Leslie Wolf and Mark Barnes argued that regulators had largely preserved the status quo for secondary research with biospecimens, and called for evidence on how implementation was actually going.

Labelled honestly: I could not find figures for how widely broad consent has been adopted across US biobanks. Named institutional decisions are not an adoption rate. If someone quotes you a percentage, ask where it came from.

There is also a companion route: a storage exemption at 46.104(d)(7), which depends on limited IRB review and specific determinations under 46.111(a)(8). The practical point for a reader is that "an IRB approved it" and "you consented to it" are different statements, and a form may rest on either.

The clause that matters most is the one nobody reads

In March 2025, 23andMe filed for bankruptcy. What followed is the clearest real-world test of consent-on-transfer that this industry has produced.

The court approved the sale of the genetic data to TTAM Research Institute, a nonprofit founded by the company's former chief executive. Several states objected, arguing the transfer should not happen without explicit, informed consent from customers. The court found the transaction as structured did not violate the applicable privacy policies or state genetic privacy laws, on the reasoning that the acquiring entity would honour the existing privacy commitments and maintain the policy. On that logic, no fresh opt-in was legally required.

The court-appointed consumer privacy ombudsman, Neil Richards, took a different view in his report: that the company should obtain affirmative consent before transferring the data. His report also noted that consumers had allegedly struggled to access their accounts, delete their genetic data, and request destruction of their biological samples.

California's challenge did not succeed either. The bankruptcy court rejected its objection, and a federal district court later dismissed its appeal on the ground that the state lacked standing. A separate $6 million settlement addressed the 2023 data breach alongside the transfer challenge.

Two honest caveats. Reported sale figures disagree across sources, with $305 million and roughly $256 million both appearing, the latter attached to rival bidder Regeneron; I did not resolve that and am not going to present either as settled. One source also dates the filing to March 2026 against March 2025 in the others; the 2025 date is better supported.

The transferable conclusion is not about one company. It is that a privacy policy you agreed to can travel to a new owner, and that "they promised to honour the existing policy" was treated as sufficient. Read the change-of-control clause first. A bipartisan Don't Sell My DNA Act has been introduced to require written notice and reaffirmative consent before any transfer, and deletion of data not subject to a sale. It is a bill. It is not law, and it may never be.

What the research-sharing expectation actually is

If your sample touches federally funded genomic research, the NIH Genomic Data Sharing Policy is the relevant backdrop. Its baseline expectation is that researchers obtain consent for future research use and broad sharing of de-identified data, applied to specimens and cell lines collected or created after 25 January 2015.

Two updates matter for anyone assessing a custodian today. Effective 25 January 2025, institutions must ensure that IT systems accessing or storing genomic data comply with NIST SP 800-171, with additional access terms, mandatory training and prompt incident reporting. That is a security change, not a consent change, and the two get conflated constantly. Separately, NIH issued an RFI (NOT-OD-26-023) on proposed updates including clarifying consent, and its comment period closed on 18 March 2026. As far as I can verify, the resulting changes have not been published, so nobody can yet tell you what the revised consent language will require.

What to look for, in the order it matters

1. Change of control. What happens on acquisition, merger, insolvency or wind-up. If this is absent, treat it as the most important gap in the document.

2. Scope of research use. Specific studies, or open-ended? Does it name secondary research?

3. Sharing. Named parties, categories of party, or unrestricted? Is de-identified data treated differently?

4. Withdrawal mechanics. Not whether you can withdraw, but how, by what channel, within what timeframe, and whether withdrawal reaches the physical sample or only future use.

5. Destruction of the physical sample. The ombudsman's report is the reason this gets its own line: people reported difficulty requesting it.

6. Retention period, stated in a number. If the duration only exists inside a terms document, that was a design choice.

Ask for the answers in writing, and time how long it takes to get them. The speed of that answer tells you more than any logo on the homepage.

FAQ

What is broad consent in biobanking?

Broad consent is a mechanism in the 2018 revised Common Rule, at 45 CFR 46.116(d), allowing one open-ended permission to cover storage, maintenance and secondary research use of identifiable biospecimens, instead of re-consenting for each study. It is limited to secondary research and has no official template form.

Can a company sell my DNA data if it goes bankrupt?

The 23andMe bankruptcy indicates it can, at least where the acquirer commits to honouring the existing privacy policy. The court found the sale as structured did not violate applicable privacy policies or state genetic privacy laws, and a state appeal was dismissed for lack of standing. The court's own privacy ombudsman disagreed and said affirmative consent should have been obtained.

Does withdrawing consent destroy my physical sample?

Not automatically, and this is the most common misunderstanding. Withdrawal often stops future use while leaving the stored material in place. Destruction of the physical sample is usually a separate request with its own process, and the 23andMe ombudsman's report noted consumers had difficulty making it.

Is de-identified data still covered by my consent?

It depends entirely on your form. The NIH Genomic Data Sharing Policy expects consent for broad sharing of de-identified data for specimens collected after 25 January 2015, which tells you that de-identification is not generally treated as putting data outside the consent question.

What does IRB approval mean for my consent?

It means an ethics board reviewed the research arrangement. It is not the same statement as "you consented to it." Some storage arrangements rest on an exemption with limited IRB review rather than on your broad consent, so the two should be checked separately.

How long should a biobank keep my sample?

There is no single legal answer, and that is why the retention period should appear as a number in your agreement rather than as a policy you have to go hunting for inside a terms document.
