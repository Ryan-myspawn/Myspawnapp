THE ADJACENCY, DISCLOSED BEFORE THE SCRIPT BECAUSE IT NEARLY KILLED THE EPISODE

On 21 August this show published "Are Sperm Counts Actually Crashing? Both Studies, Honestly.", built on the Levine and Swan meta-analysis against a 2024 Fertility and Sterility stable-counts review. That episode owns the question "are counts falling". This episode is NOT allowed to re-answer it and does not.

What the August episode could not carry is this week's actual problem: a new study is about an EXPOSURE, and it is going to be reported as evidence about a TREND. Those are two different claims and only one of them was measured. That distinction is the episode.

COLD OPEN (word for word, ~80 seconds)

"On the seventh of October a study came out that found men with more insecticide in their semen had lower sperm counts than men with less.

A hundred and seventy-nine men. One fertility clinic. Jinan, China.

Within about a day, I could already see the shape of how that will travel. It will become: sperm counts are collapsing and here is why.

Those are two different sentences.

The study measured a difference between men, at one moment, in one clinic. It did not measure a change over time. It could not. That is not a flaw in the study. It is what the study was built to do.

I run a DNA storage company. If I wanted to sell you something this week, the falling-counts story is the one that sells it. I am not going to use it, for a reason I will get to, and the short version is that four recent studies cannot agree with each other and I am not going to pretend they do."

SEGMENT 1 (~5 min): What was actually measured

  • Frontiers in Endocrinology, reported 7 October 2026 by The New Lede. Peer-reviewed journal. Read via coverage. The paper itself was not opened, and that is said on air.
  • Semen and urine from 179 men at an assisted reproductive technology clinic in Jinan, China.
  • Higher neonicotinoid levels in semen associated with lower sperm concentration. Highest exposure quartile against the lowest: a 27 percent difference.
  • The analysis looked at mixtures of insecticides rather than one chemical at a time. Say on air that this is the genuinely good part of the methodology, because real exposure is never one chemical.
  • THE LIMIT GOES BEFORE THE NUMBER, NOT AFTER IT: these were men already attending a fertility clinic. They are not a sample of men in general and the authors do not claim they are.
  • CLIP MOMENT: "Twenty-seven percent sounds like a collapse. It is a gap between two groups of men on the same day, not a slope over thirty years."

SEGMENT 2 (~5 min): Four recent studies, four answers

Read them out flat, with dates, and do not resolve them.

  • The new one: exposure associated with lower concentration, 179 men, clinic population, 2026.
  • Beijing sperm donors, published 2026: concentration up 12.3 percent and total count up 18.7 percent from 2011 to 2018.
  • An Irish single-centre review: concentration up by nearly 23 percent over its study period. The authors themselves call it unexpected and say it contrasts with widely cited global-decline reports.
  • A 2025 meta-analysis of fertile American men: no statistically significant change in sperm concentration.

(All peer-reviewed. All read via abstracts and coverage. None opened at its own domain, and the script says so once, plainly.)

  • The point, said slowly: these are different populations, different countries, different decades and different selection criteria. They are not four attempts at the same measurement. A donor cohort is screened. A clinic cohort is self-selected for trouble. A national meta-analysis is neither.
  • CLIP MOMENT: "When four studies disagree, the honest read is usually not that three are wrong. It is that they are not all answering the same question."

SEGMENT 3 (~4 min): How a headline eats a caveat

  • Mechanism, not complaint. A clinic sample becomes "men". A between-group gap becomes "a decline". A chemical class becomes "chemicals". An association becomes "caused by".
  • Give the reader a tool rather than a scolding: three questions that survive any version of this story. Who was in the sample. Was anything measured over time. Is the comparison between people or between years.
  • Against interest, on air: this show has published on sperm counts before, on 21 August, and that episode was about whether counts are falling. Name it. Say we are not redoing it. Say what changed.

SEGMENT 4 (~4 min): What we will and will not say as a company

  • We are storage. We do not treat fertility, we do not test it, and we will never imply a stored sample is a hedge against it. That sentence is the segment.
  • We have no position on whether sperm counts are falling. Four studies, and we are not picking.
  • What we can say is small and true: a biological record taken today is a record of today. That claim does not require the trend to go any particular way, which is exactly why it is the only one we make.
  • CLIP MOMENT: "If our pitch needed sperm counts to be falling, our pitch would be a bet on bad news. It is not, and I would rather say that out loud than have somebody work it out."

2. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM EXPLAINER (7-9 minutes): "Does It Work Inside a Family?"

ADJACENCY DISCLOSED FIRST: on 31 August this channel published "What a Polygenic Score Actually Tells You", which lists sibling range among its caveats. There, it is a caveat in a list. Here, it is the whole mechanism and the test it implies. Same move as this week's reclassification explainer: a thing another asset mentions is not a thing another asset explains.

Thesis: a polygenic score can look impressive across a population and tell you very little about which of two brothers is which, and the only way to find out is to test it inside families.

Script with B-roll

  1. The setup. A polygenic score is built by finding small statistical associations across very large groups of mostly unrelated people. (B-roll: a crowd, overhead, no faces readable.)
  2. The problem nobody mentions at the top. People who are unrelated differ in ancestry, geography, schooling, income and a hundred other things that travel with genetics without being caused by it. A score trained that way can be partly measuring the neighbourhood.
  3. Why siblings are the hard test. Brothers and sisters share ancestry, share parents, mostly share environment, and differ by the genetic shuffle alone. If a predictor still works between siblings, it is much harder to argue it is really measuring something else. (B-roll: two identical chairs, one slightly turned.)
  4. What that does to effect sizes. Within-family prediction is typically weaker than population prediction. Say plainly that the honest version of this is "smaller, and more believable", not "fake".
  5. Who is actually doing it. At least one embryo-screening company states that it tests whether its scores correctly predict traits of adult siblings rather than relying only on unrelated biobank participants, and has published a 2025 medRxiv preprint describing its method. EPISTEMIC LABEL, ON SCREEN: preprint. Not peer-reviewed. Company-authored. Credit the method without endorsing the product, and do not name the company on air.
  6. The professional bodies. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and reproductive-medicine bodies have said the science of polygenic risk scores has not progressed enough to produce reliable estimates for this use. (Characterisation drawn from review and news coverage, not from the bodies' own documents, and labelled as such.)
  7. The honest close, and no product. A score is a statement about a distribution. A person is not a distribution. Within-family testing is the field holding itself to a harder standard, and the right response to a company doing a harder test is to say so, and then still ask what the number means for one actual child.

HONEST-LABEL BLOCK (on screen, 8 seconds): "Preprint, not peer-reviewed. Professional-body positions summarised from coverage. No source opened at its own domain."
