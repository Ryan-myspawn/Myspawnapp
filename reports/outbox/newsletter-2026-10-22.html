(c) THE LEAD

A cryogenic storage tank can be failing for five weeks before the thermometer notices.

That is not a slogan. It is the finding of a 2019 study in the Journal of Assisted Reproduction and Genetics, which compared two kinds of alarm on the same kind of tank. One watched the weight. One watched the temperature.

An intact tank lost about ten percent of its liquid nitrogen in roughly four to five days. Warming to minus 185 degrees Celsius took thirty-eight to forty-four days.

Read those side by side. The liquid starts going in days. The temperature reports it in weeks, and then it moves fast, because once only a few centimetres remain at the bottom there is nothing left holding the cold.

So the instrument most alarms use is the instrument that reports last.

Two things we want to say carefully. First, this is not an argument that fertility clinics are careless. We know these numbers because those fields investigate their failures and publish them. A sector that hides its failures produces no studies at all. Second, cryogenic storage exists because living cells genuinely need it. Eggs, embryos and cell lines are not durable dry. The cold is doing real work.

What travels is not a conclusion about temperature. It is one question, and it works on anyone holding anything of yours: what does your alarm measure, and how long is the lag between the thing going wrong and the alarm noticing?

Peer-reviewed, bench study, 2019. We read it through a search index rather than at the publisher, and we would rather tell you that than not.

(d) Three things worth knowing

Science. An institution that lost a large amount of frozen material over a holiday period published its own review. One line from it: the failure did not occur due to any single event or source of error, but was the result of multiple issues. No villain, no single fix. That sentence is harder to write than a denial, and it is the only kind you can learn from. Not a US institution, cited as engineering rather than law.

Culture. Everyone argues about the size of the great wealth transfer. Almost nobody talks about when it lands. One study, reported by a trade publication, puts about 52 percent of inheritances now arriving when the recipient is 55 or older, up from 41 percent in 2015. We could not confirm its publication date and are saying so rather than guessing. The money, if it comes, arrives after the decisions it would have changed.

Product. We published two pieces this week on a distinction almost nobody draws: who inherits from you and who decides about you are answered by completely different laws. In Texas, the right to decide what happens to your remains can pass down a statutory list if nobody acts within six days of notice. Nobody has to behave badly for that to happen. A delayed flight will do it.
