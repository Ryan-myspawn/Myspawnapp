Issue 8 · 8 October 2026
New Jersey kept newborns' blood for 23 years. Then it quietly stopped.
Almost every baby born in this country gets a heel prick in the first days of life. A few drops of blood on a card, sent to a state laboratory, screened for conditions that are treatable early and devastating late. It is one of the genuinely good public-health programmes of the last century and nothing here is an argument against it.
What happens to the leftover card is a separate decision, made under a different statute, and most parents are never told about it.
New Jersey used to keep those cards for 23 years. Parents largely found out from a 2023 federal lawsuit. We wrote about it, and the figure went round.
Then we checked it, and it has been wrong since June 2024. New Jersey now retains identified newborn screening blood spots for two years by default, with parents able to choose a longer or shorter period. The policy changed quietly, the old number kept circulating, and we were circulating it too.
We corrected our own post before it went out. The useful lesson is not about New Jersey. It is that a fact has a half-life, and nothing printed on the page tells you how much of it is left.
Retention is set state by state and runs from about 30 days to indefinitely. Texas destroys within two years unless you consent to longer. New York holds up to ten years. Virginia keeps normal results six months. Minnesota may hold indefinitely unless you direct otherwise. Search your state's health department plus "residual dried blood spot" and read the page itself.
THREE THINGS WORTH KNOWING
Science. The first cellular rejuvenation therapy using epigenetic reprogramming to reach human trials now has a full public timeline: FDA authorisation to proceed on 15 January 2026, announced 28 January, first participant dosed as announced on 9 June. It is a Phase 1 trial in eye disease, NCT07290244, and its primary purpose is safety and tolerability, because that is what first-in-human means. (Company announcements and coverage of them; we did not open FDA records.) Cleared to try something in people is not the same as it working. Both can be genuinely exciting.
Culture. A UCL analysis published this June found that about one in ten people born between 1997 and 2012 had had a child by 23, against roughly one in four millennials at the same age. Read it carefully: it measures when, not whether. A 23-year-old has not finished deciding anything, and the separate 15 percent who reported no clear interest is a minority rather than a generation.
Custody. Here is one that surprised us. HIPAA does not say how long your doctor has to keep your medical records. It requires six years for HIPAA's own paperwork; your actual file is governed by state law. Florida: at least two years after a practice closes. Massachusetts: seven years from your last visit. Both are the law. They are just different laws.
