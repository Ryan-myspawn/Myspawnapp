NEAREST NEIGHBOURS IN THE LOG, both opened: "How Many Prices Does It Have?" is about how many prices one service displays. "Year Thirty-One" is about what a payment structure transfers. Neither is about a promise concerning the FUTURE price, which is a third object: not the price, not the structure, but the guarantee wrapped around them.

Cold open, word for word (about 80 seconds)

Every long promise has a second promise hidden inside it, and almost nobody asks about the second one.
The first promise is the thing. We will keep this for you. We will look after it.
The second promise is about the price. Not what you pay now. What you will be asked to pay in ten years, or twenty, when the thing is still here and so are you.
Most sellers of long promises are quiet about the second one, because the honest answer is that they do not know. Costs move. Facilities move. Nobody can see thirty years out.
So when an organisation does make the second promise, it is worth looking at closely. Not to admire it. To ask what it charged you for it.
Because a guarantee about a future price is a product. It has a price of its own, and that price is never money. It is a condition. Do this, and the number stops moving for you. Do not, and it does not.
Today: two organisations that both sell a very long promise, both offer the second promise, and charge completely different things for it. One of them is us, and ours is not the generous one by default. It is just cheaper to qualify for.

Segment 1: The second promise, defined

Stance: the guarantee is a separate product and should be read as one.

Talking points.

  • A long-duration service has a recurring cost and therefore a repricing risk, and that risk sits with the customer unless something explicitly moves it.
  • Three ways a seller can move it: freeze the price for everyone, freeze it for a named group, or let the customer buy the freeze by paying everything up front.
  • Say on air: none of these is more honest than the others. They move the risk to different places.

CLIP MOMENT: "A guarantee about a future price is a product. It has its own price, and the price is usually a condition rather than a number."

Segment 2: The instance we found, and what we could not read

Source: the Cryonics Institute's own published material on membership and funding, at https://cryonics.org/membership/ and https://cryonics.org/funding-for-cryostasis/.

EPISTEMIC LABEL, AND IT IS SPOKEN ON AIR RATHER THAN FOOTNOTED: we did not open either page. cryonics.org returns 403 at our own outbound proxy. Everything below reached us through a search index, which means we are describing a position, not quoting a page, and no sentence of theirs is read aloud in this episode.

Talking points.

  • The organisation is reported to offer price protection to members who prepay in full: pay the whole thing now, and future increases do not reach you.
  • The condition is the entire point. The guarantee is not given for being a member. It is given for removing the organisation's exposure to you.
  • That is a coherent design and worth saying so out loud: the people who get the certainty are the people who gave certainty.
  • Our own version runs the other way and it is not more generous, it is differently priced. MySpawn is $99 a year, billed annually, and the first 1,000 members lock the founding rate. The condition is not a sum of money. It is being early.
  • State the cost of ours, on air: a lock earned by arriving early is worth nothing to somebody who arrives late, and we have a finite number of those places by construction.

CLIP MOMENT: "They ask for all the money. We ask for the date. Neither of those is free."

Segment 3: What a price promise is actually worth

Stance: a price guarantee inherits every weakness of the organisation making it.

Talking points.

  • Our own prior sentence, quoted verbatim because it is ours: "We are selling certainty, not a saving. The founding-rate lock is a promise that the number does not move for you, and it is worth exactly as much as our ability to keep that promise." Published by this show on 16 October 2026.
  • That sentence is the ceiling on this entire episode and it applies to the other organisation identically. A guarantee from an entity that stops existing is a guarantee that stopped existing.
  • The useful consumer question is therefore not "is the price locked", it is "locked by whom, and what happens to the lock if they change hands".
  • Say plainly: we do not publish an answer to the second half of that question yet. That is a gap, it is ours, and naming it here is cheaper than being asked.

CLIP MOMENT: "Ask who is holding the lock. Not whether there is one."

Segment 4: The habit to take away

Stance: practical, no fear, no competitor named as a villain.

Talking points.

  • Three questions for any long promise you are buying: does the price move, what do I have to do to stop it moving, and who is standing behind the promise that it will not.
  • The second question is the one that is never on the pricing page, because the answer is a condition and conditions do not fit in a price table.
  • Nobody is being accused of anything. The organisation described here publishes its terms, including unflattering ones, which is more than most.

Outro CTA

If you take one thing: the price you are quoted is a fact, and the price you will be quoted later is a promise. Those are different kinds of object and they deserve different kinds of attention.
MySpawn stores a DNA record for $99 a year, billed annually. The first 1,000 members lock the founding rate. That is the condition, stated plainly, and when those places are gone they are gone.

Three Shorts cut points

1. "A guarantee about a future price is a product." Segment 1's clip moment, standalone, 22 seconds.

2. "They ask for all the money. We ask for the date." Segment 2, 18 seconds. Must carry the on-air label that we did not open the page.

3. "Ask who is holding the lock." Segment 3, 20 seconds, ending on our own gap.
