Transfer on Death Deeds: What They Move, and What They Leave Behind

A transfer-on-death deed moves exactly one thing: the piece of real estate named in it, to the person named in it, at the moment of death, without probate. For that property it generally beats a conflicting will, even a will signed afterwards. It does not move your furniture, your car, your bank accounts, or any contract you are party to. In most states it does not put the property beyond your creditors either. Everything below is the detail behind those four sentences.

What a TOD deed is

A transfer-on-death deed, called a beneficiary deed in some states, is a document you record with the county while you are alive. It names who gets a specific property when you die. Until then nothing changes: you still own it, you can sell it, mortgage it, or revoke the deed, and the beneficiary has no interest in it at all.

The appeal is narrow and real. The named property passes outside probate, which for most estates is the slowest part of the process. If you want a sense of how slow, we wrote about that in How Long Does Probate Take?.

Two mechanical conditions decide whether it works, and both are unforgiving. The deed must be recorded before death. A deed signed and left in a drawer does nothing, and the same is true of a revocation. And if the named beneficiary dies first and no alternate is named, the deed lapses and the property falls back into the probate estate it was meant to avoid.

Which states allow them, and why the count keeps moving

This is where most guides sound more certain than the evidence supports, so here is the disagreement as it stands.

One October 2026 guide counts 34 states plus the District of Columbia. Another, citing statute texts as of 12 October 2026, counts 31 states plus D.C. A third says 33. We are not going to resolve that for you, and we are not going to average it, because an averaged count is a number nobody legislated.

What the sources agree on is the direction of travel and two recent dates. Maryland's law took effect on 1 October 2026, applying only where the owner dies on or after that date. Delaware enacted its law in September 2025, with county recording reported to have begun in December 2025. At least one state-by-state table lists Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Rhode Island and South Carolina as still lacking TOD deeds for real estate, with bills introduced in several states during 2026 that had not been enacted at the time of writing.

Label, and it applies to this whole section: these are commercial guides and law-firm pages, not official statute compilations. The one primary source in our research is Virginia's codified version of the Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act. If a count matters to your decision, the only answer worth having is your own state's current statute.

It beats the will. That is the point and the trap

For the property it covers, a recorded TOD deed generally supersedes a conflicting will, and this holds even where the will was signed after the deed was recorded. The same logic governs every beneficiary designation you have ever filled in: a named beneficiary on a policy, an account or a title takes that asset at the moment of death, and it never enters the probate estate for the will to govern.

This is the part that quietly undoes careful estate plans. A will drafted in 2026 that leaves "my house to my daughter" does not reach a house with a 2019 TOD deed naming someone else. The will is not wrong. It is simply not the document that controls that asset.

The practical instruction: before you redraft a will, list every beneficiary designation and recorded deed you have, and read them. The will governs what is left over.

Four things a TOD deed does not move

1. Personal property. A Texas guide states plainly that a TOD deed cannot transfer furniture, jewellery, china or clothing. The deed covers the real estate it describes and nothing else.

2. Accounts and vehicles. These need their own payable-on-death or transfer-on-death designations, or they go through probate. Each asset class has its own form, and nothing cascades from the deed.

3. Your creditors. More on this below, because it is the most commonly misunderstood part.

4. Contracts. A deed conveys title to land. It does not assign an agreement you are party to, and it does not name a successor to a custody arrangement. This includes any storage or service contract, which is where our own product sits and where we have an obvious interest, so we have put it in its own section rather than smuggling it into a list.

The creditor point, which is the one people get wrong

A TOD deed avoids probate. It does not, in most states, put the property beyond the reach of the people you owed money to.

Virginia's codified Uniform Act is explicit: property transferred at death by a TOD deed is subject to the transferor's creditors, administration costs, funeral expenses, and certain spousal and child allowances. A New Hampshire description shows the usual structure: if the probate estate cannot cover the debts, the estate can reach the TOD property. One guide lists Alaska, Hawaii, Maine, New York, Oregon, Utah, Virginia and Washington among the states carrying such a provision.

The windows vary widely and the reporting on them is thin. One guide cites claim limits ranging from eight months in Delaware to two years in Texas and Arizona, and says Colorado bars the owner's creditors one year after death. A figure for California, described as a 120-day waiting period, appears in one source only and we are not vouching for it. That is the same discipline we applied when a source handed us a wrong statute section for California's creditor rule earlier this month.

The general shape, stated at the confidence the evidence supports: TOD property usually stays reachable by the deceased owner's creditors, for a period your state defines.

Where a stored sample sits, and why a deed does not reach it

Our interest here is small and specific, so we will name it and keep it short.

A DNA sample held under a storage contract is not real estate, and a TOD deed does not touch it. It is also not quite like a bank account, because what exists is an agreement with a custodian, not a balance with a beneficiary line. The thing that decides what happens to it is the contract and whoever you have named inside that contract, not your deed and, often, not your will either.

The practical version, which costs you nothing and does not require buying anything from us: for every ongoing agreement you hold, find out whether it has a successor or authorised-contact field, and fill it in. Storage units, safe deposit boxes, domain registrars, digital accounts and biological storage all behave this way. A beneficiary designation is only as good as the form it is written on.

FAQ

Does a transfer on death deed avoid probate?

For the property named in it, yes. The property passes to the named beneficiary at death without going through probate, provided the deed was recorded before death.

Does a transfer on death deed override a will?

Generally yes, for the property it covers, and this holds even where the will was signed after the deed was recorded. The will governs assets that have no beneficiary designation.

Can creditors reach property transferred by a TOD deed?

In most states, yes. Virginia's Uniform Act version makes TOD property subject to the transferor's creditors, administration costs, funeral expenses and certain spousal and child allowances, and guides list Alaska, Hawaii, Maine, New York, Oregon, Utah, Virginia and Washington among states with such provisions.

How many states allow transfer on death deeds?

Sources disagree. Counts of 31 plus D.C., 33, and 34 plus D.C. all appear in October 2026 guides. Maryland's law took effect on 1 October 2026 and Delaware enacted in September 2025, so the count is still moving. Check your state's statute.

What happens if the beneficiary dies before I do?

If the deed names no alternate or contingent beneficiary, it lapses and the property falls into the probate estate.

Does a TOD deed cover my car or my bank account?

No. Those require their own transfer-on-death or payable-on-death designations, and without them they pass through probate.
