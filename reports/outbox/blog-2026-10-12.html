Primary audience: Men 25-55.

Trend-hook used: a federal ruling reported on or about 30 September 2026 in Hutcheson et al. v. Verogen, Inc., No. 3:24-cv-01977 (S.D. Cal.), in which a judge allowed plaintiffs to pursue the claim that a genealogy platform's law-enforcement opt-out could be circumvented.

Short answer: it depends almost entirely on which kind of company holds your DNA, and the deciding factor is not privacy policy but database architecture. If your DNA sits in a genealogy database that matches you against relatives, police can search it under certain conditions. If it sits in a testing company's own records, police generally need a court order, subpoena or warrant, and the large companies say they fight those. And in every case, a warrant is a key that opens doors a privacy setting cannot hold shut.

The two different things people mean by "police got my DNA"

One is a search of a matching database. Investigators upload a crime-scene profile to a genealogy service and look for partial matches, then build a family tree back down to a suspect. You are not the suspect. You are the cousin whose profile made the tree possible. This is forensic genetic genealogy, and it is how the Golden State Killer case was solved in 2018.

The other is a request to a company for a named person's records. That runs through legal process and looks like any other subpoena.

These get discussed as one thing and they are not. The first reaches you through your relatives. The second reaches you through your account.

What actually happened to the opt-out

After the 2018 Golden State Killer case, GEDmatch, the site most used for this work, changed its policy in May 2019 to require users to choose whether law enforcement could match against their profile, with the default set to out. Reported uptake afterwards: between 75 and 80 percent of new users opted in, against under 25 percent of existing users, and about 260,000 users had opted in by May 2020. A Cornell law journal analysis estimated that nearly 90 percent of profiles became unavailable to law-enforcement searching. [1] [2]

That change was triggered partly by a 2019 report that police had used the site for an aggravated assault case, outside the homicide and rape limits the site had set itself. [2]

Then came the part people miss. The Electronic Frontier Foundation noted that opting out did not prevent access where investigators obtained a warrant, citing an Orlando officer who did exactly that. [3] (That account is undated in our reading, which matters for a legal detail, so treat the mechanism as established and the particular case as unverified.)

And in 2026 the opt-out itself went to court. In Hutcheson et al. v. Verogen, Inc., No. 3:24-cv-01977 in the Southern District of California, filed 24 October 2024, a federal judge on or about 30 September 2026 allowed plaintiffs to pursue the claim that a technical feature of the site's PRO platform let law-enforcement users circumvent the opt-out setting. Reported judicial language described that access as, at the very least, a substantial privacy invasion. Claims reportedly survive under genetic privacy statutes in Illinois, Oregon and New Hampshire plus the California Invasion of Privacy Act. [4]

Epistemic label, because this one matters: that is a ruling on a motion to dismiss. It means a court accepted that the allegation, if true, states a claim. Nothing has been proven and the defendants have not had their evidence weighed.

What the big testing companies say they do

23andMe publishes a transparency report, last updated 30 April 2026, and says it updates it twice a year. It reports no National Security Letters and no FISA orders. Its stated policy is to scrutinise every request and comply only with court orders, subpoenas or warrants it judges legally valid. NPR reported in 2024 that authorities had sought genetic data on 15 individuals since 2015 and that the company had resisted and produced none. [5] (2024 figure. Not current, and labelled as such.)

Ancestry also publishes a transparency report, and TechCrunch reported in 2021 that it fought two police requests. [6] (Five-year-old reporting.)

Read what those statements actually promise. They are commitments to demand legal process and to litigate requests the company thinks are invalid. They are not a promise that a valid warrant will be refused, because no US company can promise that.

The laws, and there are fewer than you would expect

Maryland and Montana were the first states to restrict law-enforcement genetic genealogy, both in 2021.

Montana's is short: it requires a probable-cause search warrant for genealogy searches of government or consumer DNA databases, and for identification searches of consumer databases unless the consumer has waived privacy.

Maryland's is broader and closer to the Justice Department's interim policy: forensic genetic genealogy may not begin without judicial authorization, and only for specified serious crimes, with a certification to the court. It also restricts covert DNA collection from suspects and their relatives, which requires prior court approval. [7] [8]

A 2026 Maryland amendment, SB 678, would add a requirement that a short tandem repeat profile has already been developed from the forensic sample before a search begins. Its hearing was rescheduled to 10 March 2026 and its listed effective date is 1 October 2026. WE COULD NOT CONFIRM WHETHER IT WAS ENACTED, and we are not going to assume it was. [9]

The Justice Department's interim policy on forensic genetic genealogy exists, and the dates reported for it conflict: one source gives a September release, another an effective date of 1 November. We report the conflict rather than picking a side; check the DOJ document itself before relying on either. [1]

The headline fact is the count. Two states. Everywhere else, this is governed by company policy, a federal department's internal interim guidance, and whatever a judge decides. Our guide to [genetic privacy laws in 2026](/blog/genetic-privacy-laws) covers the wider statutory picture, and the same pattern holds there: the real movement is at state level and it is thin.

What this means for you, practically

One. Know which kind of service you used. A relative-matching genealogy database is a searchable network. A testing company's internal records are not the same thing, and a storage facility is different again.

Two. Your relatives' choices affect you. This is the part with no individual fix. You can opt out of everything and still be found through a cousin who opted in, because the search is looking for partial matches, not for you.

Three. Check whether your service has a transparency report, and read the date on it. Two major companies publish one. Most do not.

Four. Understand what an opt-out is. It is a setting in a piece of software. The 2026 case above is an allegation that one such setting could be bypassed, and whatever the outcome, the useful question it raises is who tested the setting and whether anyone outside the company ever checked.

Frequently asked

Can police search my 23andMe or Ancestry account?

Not freely. Both companies state they require valid legal process, a court order, subpoena or warrant, and both publish transparency reports. 23andMe's was last updated 30 April 2026 and reports no National Security Letters or FISA orders. NPR reported in 2024 that authorities had sought data on 15 individuals since 2015 and the company produced none; that is a 2024 figure. No US company can promise to refuse a valid warrant. [5] [6]

What is forensic genetic genealogy?

Investigators upload a crime-scene DNA profile to a genealogy matching service, look for partial matches to distant relatives, and build family trees down to a suspect. The people whose profiles make it work are usually not suspects and usually have no idea their data contributed. It became widely known through the 2018 Golden State Killer case.

Does opting out of law-enforcement matching protect me?

Partly, and not completely. GEDmatch moved to opt-in in May 2019 with the default set to out, and a Cornell analysis estimated nearly 90 percent of profiles became unavailable to such searches. But the EFF has noted that a warrant can reach data regardless of the setting, and a 2026 federal ruling let plaintiffs pursue a claim that one platform's opt-out could be technically circumvented. That is an allegation surviving a motion to dismiss, not a finding. [1] [3] [4]

Which states regulate law enforcement genetic genealogy?

Two: Maryland and Montana, both in 2021, the first in the nation. Montana requires a probable-cause warrant. Maryland requires judicial authorization, limits use to specified serious crimes and restricts covert DNA collection. A 2026 Maryland amendment, SB 678, is listed with an effective date of 1 October 2026, and we could not confirm it was enacted. Everywhere else, company policy and judicial discretion govern. [7] [8] [9]

Can police get DNA I stored with a preservation company?

A valid court order can reach a storage company like anyone else, and no honest company will tell you otherwise. The structural difference is that a storage facility holding a sample is not a searchable matching network, so there is no relative-matching function for an investigator to use. That is a fact about architecture, not a legal shield.

What is the single most useful thing I can do?

Find out whether the service you used matches you against relatives, and whether that matching can be turned off. Then accept the part you cannot control: a relative's decision can expose you regardless of yours, and no setting you own changes that.
