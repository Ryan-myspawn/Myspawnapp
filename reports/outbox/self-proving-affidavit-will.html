Primary audience: Men 25-55

Trend / hook used: the 2026 remote-notarization collision. Remote online notarization is now authorized in roughly 48 to 49 states plus the District of Columbia, and wills are specifically carved out of it in Texas and New Jersey. Missouri's electronic wills and trusts act took effect 1 January 2026 and Kentucky's Uniform Electronic Wills Act took effect 15 July 2026. Labelled honestly: that is a 2026 statutory wave, not an event from the last fourteen days. The hook is the collision it creates, which is live right now: the one document where notarization actually buys you something is the document most states will not let you notarize remotely.

A self-proving affidavit is a sworn statement, signed by your will's witnesses in front of a notary, that says the will was signed properly. Its only job is to let a probate court accept the will without tracking down those witnesses years later and asking them to remember. It does not make the will valid: a will without one is still a will. What it buys is that your executor does not have to locate two people who may have moved, died, or forgotten, before anything in the estate can move. Most states offer the instrument. Ohio and the District of Columbia are the two that every source agrees do not. And the states disagree enough that the useful question is not "do I need one" but "what does my state actually require, and can I attach it after the fact."

What the document actually is, and what it is not

Two instruments get confused constantly, and they do opposite things.

A self-proving affidavit is signed at or after your will signing, by the people who watched you sign, and sits attached to the will until you die. Its audience is a probate judge, and its message is: the execution was proper, here is our sworn word on it, you do not need to call us.

A small estate affidavit is signed after you die, by whoever wants to collect the property, to get an estate through without full probate. In Texas it is available only if there is no will at all (small estate affidavit vs probate). Same word, opposite precondition.

So this is a shortcut past one step inside probate, not past probate itself.

What the form makes people swear

The Uniform Probate Code supplies the model at section 2-504 and most UPC states carry it there. Maine uses 2-503, so check the number in your own state.

The testator's half has you declare that you signed the instrument as your will, willingly and as your free and voluntary act, that you were of legal age and sound mind, and under no constraint or undue influence. Those last two clauses are not filler. They are the elements a later contest would have to attack.

The witnesses' half has each witness swear that you declared the document to be your will, that you signed it in their presence, and that each of them signed in your presence and in the presence of the other. Under Florida Statutes section 732.503 the testator's declaration runs along the lines of "I, [name], declare to the officer taking my acknowledgment of this instrument, and to the subscribing witnesses, that I signed this instrument as my will." An officer authorized to administer oaths signs and seals it in an official capacity, and the certificate is attached to or follows the will.

One detail that has saved wills: in the UPC states carrying it, a signature on the affidavit counts as a signature on the will itself where that is needed to prove due execution.

Two routes, and the second one is the useful one

You can do it at the signing. Texas section 251.1045 is the version where the will is executed, attested and self-proved simultaneously, with the statutory language inside the will itself.

Or you can bolt it on afterwards. Texas section 251.104 is the attached-affidavit route: the will already exists, and the testator and witnesses later appear before a notary to swear it up. States reported to allow later attachment include Kansas, New Jersey, Oklahoma, Texas, Wisconsin and Wyoming. Texas also accepts an affidavit in substantial compliance with the statutory form, a lower bar than exact wording.

This is the practical finding for anyone who already has a will. If you signed one five years ago without an affidavit, in many states you are not stuck. You need the same witnesses, which is the thing that gets harder every year you wait.

Which states, and where the sources openly disagree

The secondary sources do not agree, and any clean fifty-state list overstates what the public summaries support.

Four reference sources, four different exception lists:

  • FreeWill: Ohio and the District of Columbia.
  • Cornell's Legal Information Institute: DC, Maryland, Ohio and Vermont. Its page carries a last-reviewed date of 2021, and that age is part of the finding.
  • eForms: Louisiana, Maryland, Ohio and DC.
  • Nolo: DC and Ohio.

The intersection of all four is Ohio and the District of Columbia. Maryland appears on three, Vermont on two, Louisiana on one and is directly contradicted: another source calls Louisiana the only state that requires notarization, which is a notarial-will system rather than an absence of self-proving. We cannot resolve that from secondary sources and will not pretend we did.

There is also a category error in those lists. California, Illinois and Maryland are described as states where a correctly signed and witnessed will is already effectively self-proved through the attestation clause. If so, Maryland is an exception for the opposite of the obvious reason: not because proof is harder, but because the extra document is redundant. California sources disagree on whether a court may still demand more.

The Ohio case, which is not what the headlines say

The summaries make Ohio sound punitive and the statute does not read that way.

A statute-citing summary of Ohio Revised Code sections 2107.16 and 2107.18 describes a probate court that may admit a will when proper execution appears from the face of the will, or may require witness testimony, with other satisfactory proof available when a witness is missing, incompetent, or unable to testify within a reasonable time. Two competent witnesses must attest under section 2107.03, and notarization is not required at all.

So Ohio did not remove a protection. It never adopted the instrument and gave the court discretion instead. That is the difference between a risk you can plan around and one you cannot.

One source contradicts itself here, calling Ohio's affidavit "not available" on one page and describing it as available by notarized acknowledgment on another. Named, not averaged.

What actually happens without one

The will is still valid. People get this wrong in both directions.

What changes is the proof step. Courts want evidence that execution was proper, and without a sworn affidavit on file that evidence comes from a person. Many courts will require a witness to appear, in person or by sworn statement. If a witness has died or cannot be found, the executor falls back on testimony from someone familiar with the testator's signature, or expert handwriting analysis. Pennsylvania has a named structure for this, an unavailable-witness affidavit and a non-subscribing-witness oath, which practitioners there describe as costing time, money and fees.

On delay, practitioner sources describe a few extra weeks in straightforward cases and months in complicated ones. One source gives six to eighteen months and cites nothing for it, so we are not repeating it as a number.

The shape of the cost is the point. It is not a fee you can budget. It is a dependency on two specific people staying findable for as long as it takes.

The 2026 trap: you can notarize almost anything online, except this

The collision is live and catches competent people.

Remote online notarization is now broadly authorized: one commercial count puts it at 49 states plus DC as of August 2026, the land-title industry's reported count at 48 plus DC.

Wills are the exception, in the states that carved them out. The Texas Secretary of State's own guidance states that wills, codicils and testamentary trusts are excluded from the Texas Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, so they cannot be signed electronically or notarized by videoconference, and points to Estates Code section 251.051 for in-person execution. New Jersey is reported to carry the same exception. Commentary is blunt that this is a document-type exclusion, not a platform limitation: adding remote witnesses or choosing a provider that advertises estate documents does not get round it.

Fully electronic wills are a narrower club. Vendor lists name roughly ten states with a Uniform Electronic Wills Act or equivalent, commonly Florida, Indiana, Arizona, Colorado, North Dakota, Utah, Washington, Illinois, Idaho and Nevada. The lists do not fully agree, which is itself the finding. Recent additions: Missouri, 1 January 2026, and Kentucky, 15 July 2026, for decedents dying on or after that date.

One claim we are flagging rather than reporting. A single remote-notarization industry source states that a 2026 Texas amendment, HB 4358, added a two-witness requirement for wills notarized under Government Code section 406.105, effective 1 September 2026. No other source in our sweep mentions it, the agency material we found predates it, and we did not open the enrolled bill. Single-sourced and unverified.

Frequently asked questions

Does a will have to be notarized?

In almost every state, no. A will typically needs your signature and two witnesses. The notary appears only for the self-proving affidavit, which is optional in most states. Louisiana is reported as the outlier that requires notarization, as part of its notarial-will system.

Is a will invalid without a self-proving affidavit?

No. The affidavit affects how the will is proved, not whether it is valid. Without one the court generally needs a witness to appear or swear a statement, and if none can be found the executor falls back on signature testimony or handwriting analysis.

Can I add a self-proving affidavit to a will I already signed?

In many states, yes. Texas allows it under Estates Code section 251.104, and states reported to permit later attachment include Kansas, New Jersey, Oklahoma, Wisconsin and Wyoming. You need the original witnesses, which is why it gets harder the longer you wait.

Which states do not allow self-proving affidavits?

Every source we compared agrees on Ohio and the District of Columbia. Maryland appears on three of four lists, Vermont on two, Louisiana on one and contradicted. Several sources describe California, Illinois and Maryland as states where a correctly witnessed will is already self-proved. Check your own state's current probate code rather than any list, including this one.

Can I sign my will with an online notary?

Often not, even where remote notarization is otherwise legal. Texas excludes wills, codicils and testamentary trusts from electronic signing and videoconference notarization, and New Jersey reportedly carries the same exception. A smaller group of states permits fully electronic wills under a Uniform Electronic Wills Act.
