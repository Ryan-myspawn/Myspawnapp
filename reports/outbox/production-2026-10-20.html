1. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM EXPLAINER (7 to 9 minutes)

"It Worked In Mice. Here Is The Denominator."

THE RE-SOURCING YESTERDAY DEMANDED WAS DONE, AND IT CHANGED THE ANSWER. THIS IS THE WHOLE REASON THE FLAG EXISTED.

Yesterday's watchlist banked a figure from a 2024 review: "only approximately 4 percent of transferred embryos derived from iPSC gamete procedures result in a live birth." It carried a written instruction: re-source against the primary literature before any on-screen use.

Re-sourced today. The 4 percent is a FIELD-WIDE ESTIMATE in a review, not a result. The primary figure is better, older and far more useful.

The number this script is built on

**Hikabe O, Hamazaki N, Nagamatsu G, et al., Nature 539, 299-303 (2016), DOI 10.1038/nature20104. As cited in a 2024 EMBO Journal paper: full-term development from two-cell embryos made from in vitro-derived MII oocytes was 0.9 percent, which is 26 out of 2,753. The control in the same comparison, embryos from superovulated mice, was 61.7 percent, 37 out of 60.**

FOUR LABELS, ALL SPOKEN ON AIR AND NOT ONLY WRITTEN HERE.

1. This is MICE. 2016. Ten years old.

2. The 0.9 percent figure reaches us through the EMBO paper CITING Hikabe. We did not open the Nature paper. Say so.

3. Hikabe used both embryonic stem cell and iPSC lines, so 0.9 percent covers pluripotent-stem-cell-derived oocytes generally rather than iPSC alone.

4. A Nature commentary notes the protocol appears much less efficient again when applied to primordial germ cell-like cells as in Morohaku and colleagues. So 0.9 percent is not a floor.

Full script

Same species. Same laboratory setting. One is roughly sixty-eight times the other."

2. EVERGREEN BANK

"The Speakerphone"

Format returning ZERO fleet-wide: speakerphone, phone menu, press one. Structurally unlike anything in the last seven days: there is no narrator for the first fifty seconds, no music, no demonstration and no hands doing a task. Nearest neighbours are all different: the 19 October "Laminator" was a machine being operated, the 19 October evening "Elevator" was a moving space with continuous voiceover, the 19 October "Ice Cube" was a single object changing.

Shot design: one fixed overhead shot of a phone lying face up on a bare table, speaker on. The screen shows a call timer counting. Nothing else is ever in frame. No person, no hands, no cutaway.

Hook (first two seconds, sound only): the three ascending tones of an automated phone menu.

Beat list.

1. Menu audio begins. ON-SCREEN CAPTION, typed as it is spoken: "For account services, press one." The call timer reads 0:02.

2. A second menu. "Please say the name of the account holder." A pause. Nothing is said. "I did not catch that."

3. Hold music. The timer passes four minutes. Hold on the unchanging frame for a genuinely uncomfortable eight seconds.

4. A transfer tone. A new menu. "For account services, press one." The same line as beat one. Timer reads 11:40.

5. More hold music. Timer passes nineteen minutes.

6. The audio stops dead. The timer resets to 0:00.

7. First and only voiceover, flat, over the silent frame: "That is what it sounds like when the person who knew the account number is the person who died."

8. ON-SCREEN, no voiceover: Write the list while you can. Not passwords. Institutions.

Why it earns a place: it is the only piece in the bank that makes the audience feel the problem instead of being told it, and it costs almost nothing to produce. Product mention: none at all. No price card, no URL.

Risk flags, three. (1) Use SYNTHESISED menu audio written for the shoot. Do NOT record a real company's phone system: that captures a real brand's audio and a real employee's voice. (2) No real account number, name or digit may appear on the phone screen at any moment. (3) The nineteen-minute hold must be genuinely edited down with the timer jumping visibly, not faked as continuous. The piece's whole power is that the timer is the only thing moving, and a dishonest timer would be the one lie in a script about honesty.
