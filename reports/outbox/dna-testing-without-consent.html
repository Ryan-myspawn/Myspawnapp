In several US states, testing another adult's DNA without their express consent is a crime, not merely a civil wrong. Florida is the sharpest example: its statute makes knowingly analysing another person's DNA without express consent a third-degree felony, and separately bars disclosing or selling someone's results without consent even when the sample was collected with it. But there is no federal ban, the states that have acted are a minority, and the rules are moving fast. The practical answer for most people is that the law is thinner than you expect, the testing companies have pushed the consent problem onto the person who buys the kit, and neither fact makes doing it a good idea.

There is no federal answer, and that is the first thing to understand

No federal statute makes it illegal to test another adult's DNA without their permission. GINA, the federal genetic non-discrimination law, covers health insurance and employment. It is not a consent law and it does not reach your brother-in-law.

What exists instead is a patchwork. More than ten states have enacted genetic privacy legislation, and both the entities they regulate and the duties they impose vary considerably. Some reach only companies. Some reach any person. If you want a real answer for your own situation, the state you are in is the whole question.

Florida: the version with teeth

Florida is the state most often cited, because its law is unusually direct.

Under Florida law, a person or entity may perform DNA analysis only with the express consent of the person tested, subject to exceptions, and the results may not be disclosed without express consent. Knowingly analysing another person's DNA without that consent is a third-degree felony.

Read the second half of that again, because it is the part people miss: the disclosure rule is separate from the collection rule. A sample lawfully collected with consent for one purpose does not come with permission to sell, publish or hand the results to somebody else. Consent to be tested is not consent to be disclosed.

Exceptions exist and they are the ordinary ones. A person with legal authority can consent on behalf of a minor, an incapacitated adult or a deceased person. In legal disputes such as child custody or support, a court order can override the need for individual consent.

What changed in 2026, and what is only proposed

This area is moving, and the difference between an enacted law and a proposed bill is the difference between a rule and a press release.

Enacted in 2026: South Dakota and Utah. Proposed or advancing: Virginia, Wisconsin and West Virginia, among others. West Virginia's proposal would make it a misdemeanour to sell or transfer genetic data without express consent.

(Source: a law-firm privacy tracker, read through a search index, reporting as of April 2026. A bill that was pending in April may have passed, failed or been amended since. Check before citing any of these as law.)

One structural quirk worth knowing, because it surprises people: in California, companies must get permission before storing or selling someone's DNA, but state agencies are exempt from that statute. A consent law that binds companies and exempts government is a common shape, and it means "my state has a genetic privacy law" does not tell you who it binds.

The companies already decided whose problem this is

Here is the part that has nothing to do with legislatures.

Read the terms of the consumer testing services and a consistent pattern appears: they require the person submitting a sample to have legal authority to do so, and they put the consent burden on the customer rather than carrying it themselves.

  • 23andMe's terms require that the submitter be legally authorised to submit a saliva sample on behalf of another person. (Read from a Canadian version of the terms; verify the current US version.)
  • Ancestry requires each adult who gives a sample to create their own account. A parent or guardian may submit a minor's sample, but must represent that they are the parent or guardian and that the child agreed. (Read from a 2019 version of the terms; verify the current one.)
  • One testing service's terms have the customer guarantee the sample's origin outright, confirming that the sample belongs to the person it is said to belong to.
  • A forensic laboratory's consumer-kit terms go further and disclaim responsibility for obtaining proper consent altogether, while noting its kits include no chain of custody and no verification of identity and are not intended for legal purposes. (A Canadian laboratory. Cited as an illustration of how these terms are written, not as a statement about US law.)

So the enforcement question answers itself. The company has contractually established that you told it you had the right to submit the sample. If you did not, the misrepresentation is yours. That is not a loophole closing; it is a liability allocation, and it was written by the party with lawyers.

The three situations people are actually asking about

"My relative tested themselves and now my information is exposed." This is the common case and it is not a consent violation by anybody. A relative testing their own DNA reveals information about you by inference, and no state consent statute prevents a person from testing their own sample. We cover the knock-on effects in who inherits your DNA testing account and police access to consumer DNA.

"I want to test a relative to confirm a family relationship." Get their consent in writing. In a consent state you may otherwise be committing a crime; in a non-consent state you may be fine legally and will still have to live with the person afterwards. And note the second rule: even with consent to test, you may not have consent to tell anyone.

"Someone may have tested a sample of mine." Your remedies depend entirely on your state, and the honest answer is that in most states they are thin. The practical first step is not legal; it is to find out which of your samples exist and who holds them. Our walkthrough is at how to make a records request.

FAQ

Is it illegal to DNA test someone without their consent in the US?

There is no federal prohibition. It depends on your state. In several states, including Florida, knowingly analysing another person's DNA without their express consent is a crime. More than ten states have genetic privacy laws, but they differ in who they bind and what they require, and several more are considering bills.

Is testing someone's DNA without consent a felony?

In Florida it is charged as a third-degree felony. Most states that regulate this do so through civil or administrative penalties rather than criminal ones, and some regulate only companies rather than individuals. Check your own state's current statute before relying on any general answer.

Can I legally test my child's DNA?

Generally yes. A person with legal authority can consent on behalf of a minor, and the main consumer testing services allow a parent or guardian to submit a minor's sample while requiring them to represent their parental status. Some terms also require that the child agreed to the testing.

Does consent to be tested include consent to share the results?

No, and this is the distinction most people miss. At least one state bars disclosing or selling someone's genetic results without express consent even when the sample itself was collected with consent. Treat collection and disclosure as two separate permissions.

What happens if someone tests my DNA without permission?

Your options depend on your state, and in many states they are limited. Where a criminal provision exists, it is enforced by prosecutors rather than by you. Where only a civil remedy exists, you may have to show harm. The first practical step is usually establishing what sample was used and who held it.

Do DNA testing companies verify consent?

Generally no. They require you to represent that you are authorised to submit the sample, and at least one laboratory's terms explicitly disclaim responsibility for obtaining proper consent. The representation is yours, which means so is the exposure.
