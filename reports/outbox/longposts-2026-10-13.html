#137 [X] To find out how much of your lifespan is genetic, they first had to subtract everyone who died for a reason.

ZERO PRODUCT MENTION.

For twenty years the field's own estimate of how heritable human lifespan is went DOWN. Twin studies put it at 20 to 25 percent. Recent large pedigree studies pushed it as low as 6 percent.

In January a paper in Science put it above 50 percent.

Nobody's genome changed. The method did.

The paper: Science, 29 January 2026, Shenhar et al., led by Ben Shenhar in Uri Alon's lab in the Weizmann Institute's Molecular Cell Biology Department, with co-authors at the Karolinska Institutet's Department of Medical Epidemiology and Biostatistics. Peer-reviewed. Its own title says the quiet part: heritability of intrinsic human lifespan is about 50 percent when confounding factors are addressed.

Here is the confounder. People do not only die of being old. They die in cars, in wars, of infections, of accidents at work. Those deaths are real, and from a genetics point of view they are noise: they tell you nothing about how long that person's body was built to run.

So the team separated intrinsic mortality from extrinsic mortality and re-estimated. Accounting for extrinsic mortality, the heritability estimate rises to about 55 percent. The tools were mathematical modelling plus analyses of twin cohorts raised together and raised apart.

Read what that means carefully, because it is the whole article. The genetic signal was always there. It was being diluted by everyone who died for a reason that had nothing to do with their genes. Previous estimates were not lying. They were answering a slightly different question and getting a smaller number for it.

Two limits, both load-bearing.

One: heritability is a statistic about variation in a population, not a forecast about a person. "Fifty percent heritable" does not mean half of your lifespan is already written. It means that across the population studied, about half of the variation between people traces to genetic differences. Nothing in that sentence is about you.

Two: the population matters, and the authors say so. The data come mostly from Scandinavian and European cohorts, and generalisability to other groups is untested. A heritability figure is partly a fact about the environment it was measured in. Change the environment and the number moves, which is exactly what this paper demonstrates by changing one assumption.

One figure we are deliberately holding at arm's length: secondary coverage reports roughly 70 percent heritability for dementia-related mortality up to age 80. We did not see it in the paper. Treat it as reported, not established, until somebody opens the article.

And one phrase worth guarding. Coverage of this paper ran headlines saying genes matter "way more than we thought." The paper says about 50 percent, and about 55 percent after the adjustment. "Up to" is doing real work in that sentence and it should not be quietly dropped.

What this changes for anyone reading it: nothing you can act on. That is not a criticism of the paper. It is the honest status of a population genetics result. You cannot take a heritability estimate to a doctor.

What it does change is the shape of the argument. For a decade the fashionable claim was that lifespan is mostly lifestyle and genes barely matter. That claim rested on numbers like 6 percent. Those numbers now have a competitor with a stated method, and the competitor is five times larger.

Hold both. One paper does not settle a field it just reopened.

#138 [FACEBOOK] A $99 DNA kit can start an inheritance claim. It cannot finish one.

A test you bought for curiosity is now one of the most common ways a closed family question gets reopened. And almost everything written about it skips the part that decides the outcome.

Here is the part: a consumer DNA result is treated as a lead, not as proof.

What courts generally want instead, per probate practitioners writing in 2025 and 2026: testing performed by an accredited forensic laboratory, documented chain of custody for the sample, and expert testimony to put it in evidence. A screenshot of a relative-match list is not any of those three things. It is the thing that makes you go and get them.

The case that made the category famous. Forbes, 4 December 2025, Matthew Erskine, reported on Carmen Thomas, who found two half-sisters through a consumer DNA test and pursued a claim touching roughly $28 million connected to their late father's malpractice award. One account says the matter resolved in favour of the existing heirs in part because the claim was brought too late, rather than because the biological connection failed. Sources we read disagree on whether that was a court ruling or a settlement, and we are not going to pick one. (Reported. Named outlet, named author, dated. Not a docket reading.)

The procedural lesson in that one sentence is the most useful thing on this page: the claim is reported to have failed on a CLOCK, not on biology. Being right about who your father was is not the same as being on time.

A case that went the other way, as reported. A man who learned through DNA testing that a business owner who died without a will was his biological father was held entitled to one third of the estate by the Utah Supreme Court, although another man had raised him. (Reported via practitioner commentary. We could not confirm the case name or the year, and we are not going to supply either from memory.)

And one still open. A woman in California claimed to be the biological daughter of the sculptor Fred Eversley on the basis of 23andMe results, in a Manhattan estate matter. As of the reporting we read there is no final ruling. (Reported, unresolved. Named as a live matter, characterised no further.)

Where the law is actually unforgiving

A valid will with clear intent usually controls distribution. If someone wrote a will and said who gets what, a biological surprise does not automatically rewrite it.

Without a will, it is the opposite. In most states, a person proven to be a child of the deceased has strong statutory rights under intestacy, and nobody has to have wanted that outcome for it to happen.

Courts can compel the test. In North Carolina, for example, a court can order DNA testing where inheritance turns on whether someone is a child of the deceased, and can enforce a valid order against a party who refuses. (Practitioner source, state-specific. Do not assume your state is the same.)

The three things worth doing with this

  1. If you have a will, check that it says what you mean and not just who you like. Silence is not a decision. It is a default, and the default is statutory.
  2. If you are the one with the test result, understand that you are at the start of an evidentiary process, not the end of one. Accredited lab, chain of custody, expert.
  3. Watch the clock. The reported $28 million matter is a timeliness story. Deadlines in probate are short and they do not care why you were late.

Where we fit, stated against our own interest. MySpawn stores a biological record, at $99 a year, billed annually, with samples held at an independently accredited third-party facility, and the first 1,000 members lock the founding rate. That record is not legal proof of parentage, and we are not a forensic chain-of-custody service. If a court is involved, you will need an accredited forensic lab and a lawyer, and we will tell you that rather than let you assume otherwise.

A DNA test never changes who your father was. It changes who can prove it, and when.

What would you rather your family found out from: a document you wrote, or a match notification?

#139 [X] The worry about insurers pricing you off an epigenetic clock is not new discourse. It is a 2019 paper. The tests still have no reference standard.

ZERO PRODUCT MENTION. This punches at an idea, and at a 2019 paper's own argument. No person is the target.

The received wisdom is that biological-age tests are a fun consumer novelty and the insurance question is speculative. Both halves of that are wrong, and the second half has a citation.

In 2019, Environmental Epigenetics (Oxford) published 5(3):dvz018 on the potential misuse of epigenetic age estimators by private companies and public agencies. Its stated concerns: actuarial and moral fairness, free and informed consent, data governance and privacy, and equity and non-discrimination. Peer-reviewed. Seven years old. That is not a tweet. That is the field flagging the pricing problem before most consumers knew the tests existed.

Now the state of the measurement, seven years later.

A 2026 real-time Delphi consultation published in Epigenetics Communications put 13 items on direct-to-consumer epigenetic testing to experts from the Francophone research community of France and Canada, analysed under a Reliability, Utility and LEgitimacy framework. Its conclusion: perceived consumer value exists, scientific and clinical reliability remains limited, utility is contested, and legitimacy is tied to equity and to how society perceives ageing. (Peer-reviewed. Expert-opinion method, which is a real limit: a Delphi panel measures what experts think, not what the assays do.)

A commercial-test review puts the margin of error on epigenetic age estimates at roughly two years. A 2026 review states that no dedicated peer-reviewed publication details the specific algorithm, CpG sites or accuracy metrics for the commercial Chronomics test.

And the direction problem, which is the newest thing here. The Insider, 6 October 2026: Horvath's clock may read the same person as YOUNGER than their chronological age while GrimAge reads them as OLDER. Not a difference in magnitude. A difference in sign.

So the honest summary is: a category with a two-year error bar, no cross-lab reference standard, and at least two mainstream clocks that can disagree about which way you are going, was identified in 2019 as a candidate input to pricing decisions about your life.

The idea we are punching at is not the science. It is the assumption that an unstandardised number becomes acceptable as an underwriting input once enough people have bought one.

Now the part most people get backwards, and we are going to label our own uncertainty rather than resolve it for you.

GINA, the federal statute everyone reaches for, covers health insurance and employment. It does not cover life insurance. So the instinct "GINA protects me" was never load-bearing here in the first place.

And whether a methylation score even counts as "genetic information" under GINA is a genuinely open question we are not going to answer. GINA is built around genetic tests that detect genotypes and mutations. Methylation is a chemical modification sitting on top of the sequence, not the sequence. Reasonable lawyers can read that both ways, and we have not read a case resolving it. If anyone has one, we want it.

A disclosure, because this operation keeps catching itself: our first search today attributed the insurance-underwriting concern to the 2026 Epigenetics Communications paper. Our second search said that attribution is not confirmed and traced the concern to the 2019 Oxford paper instead. We did not merge them, and we are telling you we nearly did.

Our own prior position on this, disclosed: we published an epigenetic-clock audit on 5 September arguing the readout is not reproducible enough to be called your biological age. This is a different argument. That one was about whether the number is reliable. This one is about what a number nobody can reproduce is already being considered for.

#140 [FACEBOOK] A paper says about half of how long I live was settled before I was born. I spent this week reading forms.

ZERO PRODUCT MENTION. Founder voice.

I run a company that stores DNA records. So you would expect me to enjoy a paper saying genes matter more than we thought.

I did not enjoy it. I found it clarifying in a way I did not want.

The paper is in Science, 29 January 2026, from Ben Shenhar in Uri Alon's lab at the Weizmann Institute with co-authors at the Karolinska Institutet. Peer-reviewed. It estimates the heritability of intrinsic human lifespan at about 50 percent, rising to about 55 percent once deaths from external causes are accounted for. The previous numbers were 20 to 25 percent from twin studies and, in recent large pedigree work, as low as 6 percent.

The method is the part that stayed with me. To see the genetic signal, they had to set aside everyone who died in a car, in a war, of an infection, in an accident. Those deaths were drowning out the thing being measured.

I am one of the people in that second category in waiting. So are you. Every one of us is a candidate for the noise term.

Two things are true at once and I want to hold both without flinching.

The first is that the number is not about me. Heritability describes variation across a population. It does not say half of my span is pre-written. The paper's own data are mostly Scandinavian and European, and the authors say generalisability is untested. Anyone who reads a population statistic as a personal forecast has made a category error, and the fact that it feels personal is not evidence.

The second is that even the honest version is humbling. Across a large population of people broadly like the ones studied, about half of the difference in how long people last traces to something decided at conception. Not to discipline. Not to foresight. Not to being the kind of man who reads papers.

So what is a man actually entitled to do with that?

Here is my answer, and it is deliberately small. The part of this you control is not the biology. It is the paperwork. And the paperwork is boring, which is precisely why it does not get done.

I spent this week reading application forms and fee schedules. A state vital records office will charge you about $15 and the fee buys a search, not a document: if the clerk finds nothing, you do not get the money back. A provider has 30 days to answer a request for your own medical records. A federal rule sets a ten-year floor on how long certain tissue records must be kept, which means a record can lawfully be destroyed before a person who needs it reaches adulthood.

None of that is inspiring. All of it is in your control, and roughly 55 percent of your lifespan is not.

That is the trade this paper put in front of me. The large thing is mostly not up to you. The small things are entirely up to you, and they are the only things anyone after you will actually be able to use.

I notice how much more appealing it is to read about longevity than to fill in a form. A protocol feels like agency. A form feels like admin. The paper suggests the protocol is working on the smaller half of the problem, and I do not think most of us act as though that is true.

I am not going to pretend storing a biological record fixes mortality. It does not. It is a filing decision, like naming a beneficiary or writing down where the deed is.

What I have stopped doing is treating the filing decisions as the unserious ones. They are the only part of this where effort converts into outcome at a rate anyone can measure.

One honest admission about this piece: the paper behind it is nine months old. It is not news. I am writing about it today because I read it this week, and because a result stops being new long before it stops being true.

If about half of it was decided before you were born: what have you actually written down about the half that wasn't?
