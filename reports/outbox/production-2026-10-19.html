1. PODCAST EPISODE PACK

Title options

1. "Eighty-Two Out Of Ninety-Three Will Not Tell You Who Decided"

2. "We Sell One Of These. Here Is The Audit Of Our Own Category."

3. "The Part That Is Easy To Check Is Not The Part That Matters"

Cold open, word for word, 60 to 90 seconds

A professor at MIT built a chatbot of her dead mother.
Her mother died when she was a teenager. The service she tried wanted text messages, and she did not have text messages from 1960-something, so she and a researcher fed her own memoir into ChatGPT instead and let it answer as her mother.
It told her about a serene afterlife. And when she pushed it on the hardest chapter of her family's history, it gave her an account her mother never gave.
Her name is Sherry Turkle. She has spent forty years studying what happens to people when they talk to machines. On the fourth of October she published a piece in the Washington Post called "AI claims it can resurrect the dead. I tried it." In the book it is adapted from, her line is: "When we play with chatbots of the dead, we are playing with fire."
Now, I want to be careful here, because there is a version of this episode where I use a grieving woman's experiment to sell you something, and that version is beneath this show.
So here is what we are actually doing. Six weeks ago we made an episode about this industry's money and its failures. This one is about a different question, and it is a question with a number attached. Two researchers at Lehigh University audited ninety-three of these services. Not their technology. Their paperwork. Specifically: do they tell you who is allowed to decide that a dead person gets turned into a chatbot?
Eighty-two of the ninety-three do not.
We sell a product in the neighbourhood of this one. So at the end of this, I am going to hold our own up to the same ruler, out loud, and I do not know yet whether we pass.

Segment 1: The audit, and what it actually measured

Verbatim quote, and note carefully whose words these are. From the preprint's abstract: the work is based on "a public-record audit of 93 systems."

Source: Shi, Hanjing and DiFranzo, Dominic, Beloved Afterlives: Governing AI Resurrection Beyond Consent, arXiv 2609.29544. https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29544

EPISTEMIC LABEL, SAID ON AIR AND NOT ONLY IN THE NOTES: this is a PREPRINT. It has not been through peer review. I have not opened it at arXiv, because arXiv does not resolve from where I work, so I am reading it through an index. Say both of those sentences out loud.

AND A DATE DISCREPANCY I AM REPORTING RATHER THAN SMOOTHING. A commercial grief-tech site gives the preprint's date as 28 August 2026. The arXiv identifier, 2609, normally indicates a September 2026 submission. I could not reconcile those from the primary source, so I am telling you the date is one of those two and I do not know which.

Talking points.

  • The three numbers, and they are the spine of this episode. Of 93 systems: consent or authority information was thin in 82. Objection or redress information was thin in 82. Deletion or export information was thin in 77.
  • The authors' framing is the useful part and it is not the numbers. They reframe AI resurrection from "a product authorized once" into an ACCOUNTABILITY CHAIN with five links: creation authority, source boundaries, circulation, contestation, and exit.
  • The finding that should make anyone in this business uncomfortable: creation was far easier to inspect than the conditions for contesting, preserving or leaving. In plain terms: the part where you start is documented. The parts where you object, keep, or get out are not.
  • The authors state their own limit and I am stating it with them: they measured only what users and affected parties can publicly inspect. Private implementation and lived outcomes remain open empirical questions. A company could be doing this well and documenting it badly. The audit cannot tell those apart and neither can I.

[CLIP MOMENT: "The part where you start is documented. The parts where you object, keep, or get out are not."]

Stance: agree and extend. Not pushback.

Talking points.

  • She is not making a technology argument. She is making a mourning argument, and it is the stronger one: the risk is not that the simulation is bad, it is that a good-enough simulation removes the pressure that grief uses to do its work.
  • Extend it with the audit: if 82 of 93 do not document who authorises a recreation, then the person most exposed to Turkle's risk is not the customer. It is the dead person, who cannot object, and the relative who did not agree and is not listed anywhere as someone who could.
  • Punch at the idea, never the person, and never at a user. Anyone who has built one of these was grieving. The target of this episode is a documentation failure, not a bereaved customer and not Sherry Turkle, with whom we agree.

Segment 3: Two more preprints, both labelled, because one audit is not a literature

Verbatim quote: the title itself, which is the argument: "His Name, Their Judgment: Expert Authority in Posthumous Persona AI."

Source: arXiv 2609.32989. https://arxiv.org/html/2609.32989

Talking points.

  • A second paper in the same window is asking who gets to be the authority on a dead person's persona, which is the contestation link in the Lehigh chain, examined on its own.
  • A third item, and it is the one with the best name: "Grief Rent: Platform Afterlives and the Price of Keeping the Dead Available," on Zenodo. LABEL: Zenodo is a repository, not a journal. Venue and peer-review status unestablished. Title cited, argument not asserted.
  • The honest shape of the field: three documents in roughly one month, none of them peer-reviewed, all pointing at governance rather than at capability. That is what an emerging consensus looks like before it is a consensus, and calling it more than that would be the exact overstatement this show exists to refuse.
  • What nobody has: an empirical study of what actually happens to the families. The Lehigh authors say so themselves. Anybody telling you the outcome data exists has not read the limitations.

Segment 4: Our own product, held to the same ruler, on air

This is the single product mention in the episode and it is not a sell.

  • The real distinction, stated first because it is genuine: ECHO points FORWARD, at a descendant who does not exist, not BACKWARD at a person who died. Nobody is grieving the subject of an ECHO conversation. Turkle's mourning argument does not reach it.

  • The against-interest line, said without softening: we are a company in a category that just got audited, and the audit found the category documents its front door and not its exits. We are not exempt from that finding because we think of ourselves as a storage company.

Outro CTA

Three questions, and they work on us and on everyone else in this business.
One: who is allowed to decide that a recreation of a specific person gets made?
Two: if you object, where does the objection go?
Three: how do you get your material out, and who can do it after you are gone?
If a service cannot answer those three in writing, that is the answer.
Ask us. Ask them. And if our own pages do not answer it by the time you hear this, say so publicly, because that is the only thing that makes a company fix it.

Shot design: one fixed camera, low and level with a desk. The laminator and two hands. No face, ever. No cutaways.

Hook (first two seconds, no words, sound only): the mechanical whine of a laminator warming up.

Beat list.

1. A single sheet of paper, handwritten, face down so nothing is readable. Two hands square it up.

2. The hands slide it into a laminating pouch. Voiceover, flat: "You get one pass at this."

3. The pouch goes into the rollers. The whine changes pitch. Nothing visible happens for four seconds, which is the whole tension of the shot.

4. It emerges sealed, warm, slightly curled. A hand presses it flat.

5. The turn, and it is physical rather than spoken. A hand picks up a pen and tries to add a line to the sealed sheet. The pen does not take. It skates off the plastic. The hand tries once more, harder. Nothing.

6. Voiceover: "Everything you wanted in there had to be in there before the heat."

7. The hands set the sealed sheet down, and beside it place a second, unsealed sheet, blank, with the pen on top.

8. Close, no price, no URL on screen: "The decision is not what you seal. The decision is what you write first."

Why it earns a place: it is a ninety-second argument for deciding content before committing to a container, which is the Memory Vault's actual job, and it never says Memory Vault. Product mention: none.

Risk flag: do NOT let the sealed sheet be legible at any point. A legible personal document on camera is a privacy problem and an unnecessary one. And do not shoot a real person's real document at all; use written-out filler text, face down.
