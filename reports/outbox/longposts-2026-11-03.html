#185 [X] The desk nobody is sitting at

Freshness check: laboratory developed and vacated return ZERO across every longpost. DECLARED ADJACENCY: #153 and #156 are both CLIA pieces, on sanction lists and on a proposed location rule. Neither touches the 2024 rule or its vacatur, and this article is about a REGULATOR THAT STOPPED EXISTING for a question rather than about CLIA's own machinery.

Primary audience: Men 25-55.

Trend-hook used: None. Two dated legal events, 2025, presented as history.

Two questions get asked about any genetic test.

Did the laboratory read the sample correctly. And does that reading predict anything about you.

The first question has a regulator. It is CLIA, the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments, administered by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, and it is concerned with whether a laboratory produces reliable measurements.

The second question, for a large share of genetic tests, has nobody.

Here is how that happened, with dates.

In May 2024 the FDA issued a final rule that would have treated laboratory developed tests as medical devices, pulling them into a framework that reviews clinical validity. That is the second question.

On 31 March 2025 a federal district court in Texas vacated the rule, holding that such tests are not "devices" under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and that the agency had exceeded its statutory authority. Reporting indicates the Department of Health and Human Services chose not to appeal (reported, not confirmed from a primary filing).

On 19 September 2025 the FDA formally removed the phrase "including when the manufacturer of these products is a laboratory" from 21 CFR 809.3(a), reverting the regulation to its pre-rule text. That is the Federal Register notice carrying RIN 0910-AJ05.

So the current position: question one is inspected. Question two largely is not, unless a specific test went through an FDA pathway, and some do.

This is not a scandal and I am not going to pretend it is. Nobody hid anything. A court read a statute, a government agency accepted the reading, and a regulation went back to its earlier words. It is simply that a desk people assume is occupied is empty, and the emptiness is a matter of public record.

What to do with that. When a company shows you a certification, read which question it answers. "CLIA certified" is a real and meaningful statement about a laboratory's measurement quality. It is not a finding that your result predicts anything. Treating the first as evidence of the second is the single most common error in this field, and it costs nothing to stop making it.

We sell storage, not tests, which is exactly why we can say this without it being a sales pitch.

EPISTEMIC LABELS: regulatory and legal documents, not scientific findings. Nothing here says any test is unsafe.

#186 [FACEBOOK] The answer a good company will give you, and almost nobody does

Freshness check: clinical utility, sensitivity, predictive value and reference range ALL return ZERO across every longpost. The ACCE framework has never been named in this lane.

Primary audience: Men 25-55, with 40-60 readers who have paid for a test.

Trend-hook used: None.

There is a question worth asking before you pay for any test, and in twenty years of this industry almost nobody has been trained to ask it.

Not "is it accurate." Not "is it approved." This one: if the result comes back positive, what would I actually do differently?

There is a formal name for that question and it has been sitting in the literature since the early 2000s. Between 2000 and 2004 the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Office of Public Health Genomics ran the ACCE Model Project, which produced the first publicly available process for evaluating genetic tests. It works through a standard set of 44 targeted questions, and it splits "is this test any good" into three separate things.

Analytic validity. Does the laboratory detect the variant it is looking for, reliably.

Clinical validity. Does that detection correlate with an actual outcome. This is measured with sensitivity, specificity and predictive values, which are not the same quantities as laboratory accuracy and often diverge from them sharply.

Clinical utility. Does testing produce a measurable improvement in health or care. The CDC's own model files effective interventions, natural history of the disorder, economic evaluation and quality assurance under this heading.

Here is why the third one matters to you personally. A result can be accurate, and predictive, and still not actionable, because no intervention exists that would change your outcome. Those are three different properties and we collapse all of them into one word: useful.

So "nothing, currently" is a respectable answer. It means the test is informative and not decision-changing, and a company that tells you so is being straight with you.

What should worry you is the company that cannot tell the difference, or that answers the third question with enthusiasm instead of specifics. Enthusiasm is not an intervention.

MySpawn does not run tests. We store a sample, at $99 a year, billed annually, and we generate no results at all. Which means I have no stake in whether any particular test is useful, and that is the only reason this is worth hearing from me.

So: before the next time you hand over a card for a panel, a screen or a kit, ask the one question. What would I do differently?

And if you have ever had a test come back with an answer that changed nothing, what did you wish somebody had told you first?

EPISTEMIC LABEL: ACCE is an established evaluation framework, not a new finding. The CDC's ACCE pages are hosted on an archive domain and may be outdated; the structure is widely reproduced in the peer-reviewed literature.

#187 [REDDIT] r/SideProject: how we date a source that has no date

Freshness check: monotonic returns ZERO across every longpost and every fleet file. ZERO PRODUCT MENTION, and this is the run's product-free article.

Primary audience: Men 25-55, builders.

Trend-hook used: None. Drawn from our own research log, 2 November.

Disclosed up front: I run a small company and I do a lot of competitor and standards research. No pitch here, no link, and the product is not mentioned anywhere in this post. I just hit a problem that has a neat solution and I had not seen it written down.

The problem. You find two sources giving different values for the same statistic about the same organisation. Neither source is dated. Which one do you trust?

Most people reach for authority. Which site looks more official. That is a weak signal and it fails constantly.

Here is a better move when the statistic has the right shape. Ask whether the number is a monotonic counter: a figure that can only go up, never down. Cumulative totals. People admitted and never removed. Units shipped. Papers published.

If it only goes up, the smaller number is the EARLIER source. Not the wrong one. The older one. You cannot date either document, and you can still put them in order, which is often all you actually needed.

The worked example, from last week. I had two figures for the same organisation's cumulative count, 263 and 248. One came from a listing we had already used; the other from an undated foreign-language profile. The count in question is intake with no removals, so 248 is a reading of the same counter from an earlier moment. No contradiction. No correction needed.

Now the part that makes this honest rather than clever. The monotonicity is an assumption, and it came from the same family of sources that gave me both numbers. If that assumption is wrong, the whole inference collapses and I am back to two unreconciled figures. So I logged it as a well-founded inference and explicitly not as a verified fact.

Three ways this goes wrong, so you do not have to find them yourself.

1. Restated baselines. Organisations sometimes restate historical totals. A counter that "only goes up" can still be rewritten downward by a methodology change.

2. Scope changes. The same label can cover a wider or narrower set over time. The number went up because the definition did.

3. Copy-paste decay. A low number may be an old figure republished yesterday. The SOURCE is earlier; the PAGE may not be. That distinction matters and I got it wrong the first time I thought about this.

What we do not know yet: whether the specific counter in my example is genuinely never revised downward. I have not seen the issuing organisation's own methodology note, only community-maintained listings, and until I do this stays an inference.

Useful generally: before you argue about which of two numbers is right, check whether you are actually looking at the same number at two different times.

#188 [X] Twenty-six states, or twenty-seven, depending who you ask

Freshness check: holographic and elective share return ZERO across every longpost. UTMA returns 2 and uniform act 2, so the adjacent lane is occupied: DECLARED ADJACENCY to #177, which was about FDIC coverage and minors as beneficiaries. This is about whether a document is a will at all.

Primary audience: Men 25-55, practical realism.

Trend-hook used: None.

A handwritten will, unwitnessed, in your own hand. Valid or worthless?

Depends on the state, and the sources that tell you which states disagree with each other.

The counts I found, and they do not match. One source says 27 states accept an unwitnessed handwritten will, with 2 more recognising one only for military service members, and the remaining 22 not at all. Another says 26 plus Louisiana. A third counts 26 with separate service-member rules in Maryland and New York. I am giving you the range rather than picking a favourite, because picking one would be inventing precision.

There are two different standards hiding inside "handwritten", and this is the part that actually catches people.

Standard one, the Uniform Probate Code model: the signature and the material portions must be in the testator's own hand. A printed form with the dispositive terms written in can qualify.

Standard two: the entire document must be handwritten. Sources name Arkansas, Kentucky, Mississippi, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Texas, Virginia, West Virginia and Wyoming, plus Louisiana's olographic testament, which must also be dated.

So a will that is valid in one of those states can fail in the other group for the opposite reason, and vice versa.

Then there is proof. Some states want the handwriting proven at probate. Reported requirements include three witnesses in Arkansas and North Carolina, and two in Tennessee and Virginia (secondary sources; verify against the statute).

And the states that do not recognise them at all do something specific that people underestimate: your handwritten will is treated as NO will, and the estate passes under intestacy. Not "mostly as you wanted". Under a statutory default that has never read your handwriting.

The sources openly conflict on individual states. Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and Florida are each described differently by different lists, and one of those disagreements is really about a technicality (Pennsylvania does not require witnesses for a signed will, so a signed handwritten one can be valid without being a "holographic will" in the strict sense).

The practical conclusion is boring and I am going to say it anyway: the list is not the authority. The statute is. Every one of those state-by-state tables is a secondary summary, they do not agree, and the one that matters is the one for the state you actually live in, as enacted today.

If you have written something by hand and put it in a drawer, that is not nothing. It is also not a plan until you know which of the two standards your state uses.

#189 [FACEBOOK] The quietest line in any study is the one that says who was allowed in

Freshness check: AMH, cut-off and eligibility return ZERO across every longpost. DECLARED CROSS-LANE ADJACENCY, stated not hidden: a production explainer written on 2 November uses the same criterion as its opening beat. That explainer is not published. If it ships first, this article should post at least five days later.

Primary audience: Men 25-55. The worked example is deliberately generalised to lab panels so this is not a women's-health piece.

Trend-hook used: None.

Every clinical trial has a short, dull section near the top that almost nobody reads. It is called eligibility, and it quietly decides who the answer applies to.

Here is a live one. The Phase 3 trial registered as NCT06858111 enrols women aged 18 to 35 with an AMH of at least 3 nanograms per millilitre. AMH is a blood marker used as a proxy for ovarian reserve. Registry updated 29 September 2026; primary completion estimated May 2027.

Read that threshold again. Not 2.9. Three.

Why would anyone draw a line there? Because to detect a difference between two treatments, you need people in whom the difference can actually show up. It is normal trial design and it is not a flaw. A trial that enrolled everybody would be larger, slower, more expensive and less likely to answer its own question.

But it has a consequence, and the consequence is the point of this post. When the result arrives, it will be a result about people who cleared that line. How well it transfers to someone below it is a separate question that the trial was not built to answer.

Now take that out of fertility entirely, because it is everywhere.

A reference range on your own blood test is built from a reference population. If you are not well represented in that population, the range is a worse guide for you than it looks, and nobody at the laboratory is doing anything wrong by printing it. A value just under a cut-off and a value just over it are nearly the same measurement. They get two different words attached. Normal. Low.

Insurance thresholds do it. Regulatory limits do it. Trial entry does it. Each line exists to turn something continuous into something decidable, for somebody's specific purpose, and that purpose is usually not you.

I am not telling you to ignore the lines. They are the best available way to make a smooth quantity into a yes or a no, and a world without them is worse. The error is not having a line. The error is thinking the line is a fact about your body.

So when somebody quotes you a study, ask the dull question. Who was allowed in?

We are a storage company, $99 a year, billed annually, and we run no trials and generate no results, so this costs us nothing to say. What is a number you were once given that turned out to mean less than it sounded?
