1. PODCAST EPISODE PACK

Titles (3 options)

1. "Who Is Holding It?" (recommended)

2. "The Capsule On The Mantelpiece"

3. "Possession Is Not Custody"

Length: 18 to 22 minutes.

Lane: category and taxonomy. NEW AXIS: not what a preservation product promises, but WHO physically has the thing afterwards. possession returns ZERO across the production log.

ADJACENCY DISCLOSED AND ARGUED: "Custody Without Access" (18 September) is about a platform's legacy-contact feature, where the question is permissions on an account nobody physically holds. This episode is about objects: who has them in their hands, and what happens to the hands.

EPISTEMIC LABEL SAID ON AIR: every fact about the competitor's product was read through a search index and not opened at its own source. Twenty-ninth consecutive day.

Cold open (word for word, 70 to 80 seconds)

There is a product in the funeral industry that I want to describe to you without judging it, because the mechanics are more interesting than any opinion I have about them.
A funeral home collects a sample from somebody who has died. It has to happen before cremation or embalming, which is real biology and not a sales tactic. The sample goes to a lab, the DNA is extracted and preserved, and then it comes back. In a sealed stainless steel capsule. To the family.
That last part is the whole thing. It comes back. The family takes it home.
Now hold that next to what we do, which is the opposite. A sample goes to a biorepository and it stays there, and you pay a fee every year for it to keep staying there.
Two products, same category on the shelf, completely different question underneath. One asks what you want to own. The other asks who you want to depend on.
And for two months I have been writing a price comparison between them that does not actually work. I will get to that.

[CLIP MOMENT: "One asks what you want to own. The other asks who you want to depend on."]

Segment 1 (5-6 min): The mechanics, told straight

Stance: describe the competing model accurately and without sneering, because the mechanics are the argument.

Talking points:

  • The funeral provider collects using supplies in the kit. Collection must happen BEFORE cremation or embalming.
  • The lab extracts, purifies and preserves the DNA, and returns it to the family inside the capsule.
  • The capsule is 316L surgical-grade stainless steel, triple-sealed, with a built-in humidity regulator. That is a serious object, not a trinket, and saying so costs us nothing.
  • The home-banking version of the product takes 10 to 14 business days to process. (The post-mortem timeline was not stated in what we could read.)
  • Epistemic label, said on air: all of this is from the company's own published pages, read through a search index. We did not open them.

[CLIP MOMENT: "Before cremation or embalming. That is real biology, and it is also the most emotionally loaded deadline in the industry."]

Segment 2 (5-6 min): Possession risk against custody risk

Stance: both models have a failure mode, and anyone selling you one while hiding the other is selling.

Talking points:

  • Possession risk: you are the single point of failure. Nobody is monitoring anything. The object moves house, gets inherited, gets put in a drawer, gets cleared out. There is no alarm, because there is no system.
  • Custody risk: you depend on an institution's continuity and on your family's ability to reach it. If the company fails, the question becomes what happens to the samples. If your family cannot prove authority, the sample is somewhere they cannot get to.
  • The receipt that makes custody risk concrete, and it is ours from two days ago: a cryogenic tank can lose ten percent of its liquid nitrogen in four to five days while taking thirty-eight to forty-four days to warm to minus 185 degrees. (2019 bench study, peer-reviewed.) An institution can be failing invisibly too. The difference is that an institution can be ASKED what it measures. A capsule in a drawer cannot.
  • The honest symmetry: a well-kept object in a careful family may outlast a badly run company. That is not a hypothetical and it should be said.

[CLIP MOMENT: "Nobody is monitoring the drawer."]

Segment 3 (4-5 min): The comparison we got wrong

Stance: own it in one beat and move on.

Talking points:

  • Since August our own competitor file has carried the line "$99 a year while you're alive beats $498 at your funeral." It reads well and it is not like-for-like.
  • $498 once for an object you keep is a different kind of transaction from $99 a year for somebody else keeping it. A comparison whose thesis depends on one-time against recurring is not a comparison, it is a slogan.
  • What replaces it is not a better price line. It is the question from segment 2: which failure mode do you want to own?
  • And one more admission, because it is in the same file: a price we recorded in August was contested in September and is still marked do-not-quote in our own records. We are not going to quote it here either.

[CLIP MOMENT: "A comparison whose thesis depends on one-time against recurring is not a comparison. It is a slogan."]

Segment 4 (3-4 min): What we are for

Stance: boundary, stated against our own interest.

Talking points:

  • MySpawn is custody. $99 a year, billed annually. Storage only: no testing, no analysis, no interpretation, no promise about the future.
  • The questions that apply to us are the ones from segment 2, and we would rather you ask them: what do you measure, who gets told, and what happens to the samples if the company does not exist.
  • If a reader concludes possession suits them better, that is a correct conclusion for some people and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

Outro CTA

Whatever you decide, decide which failure mode you are choosing, rather than which brochure you liked. If that leads you to a capsule on a shelf, fine. If it leads you to a storage fee, that is us, ninety-nine dollars a year, billed annually, storage and nothing else.

Three Shorts cut points

1. The cold open from "It comes back" to "who you want to depend on."

2. "Nobody is monitoring the drawer" plus the thirty seconds of possession-risk setup before it.

3. The slogan admission from segment 3, cut to end on "it is a slogan."

"Who Inherits, And When"

This is TIMING and DIRECTION: when it arrives, and from which generation. fertility rate returns zero and no explainer has touched inheritance timing.

ADJACENCY DISCLOSED: "We Published the Wrong Number" (9 September) involved a fertility-measure comparison error. Different lane, different measure, and nothing here revisits it.

Full script beats with B-roll directions:

[B-ROLL: a wall calendar, pages turning slowly. No face.]

Open on the assumption: that an inheritance is a thing that happens to you in your thirties or forties, from your parents, and changes what you can afford.

[B-ROLL: a plain card on a dark table, the figure typed on it.]

Receipt one, and it is about timing. A study reported by a trade publication finds about 52 percent of inheritances are now received when the recipient is 55 or older, up from 41 percent in 2015. ON-SCREEN LABEL, held for the full beat: publication date not confirmed by us. Say it out loud too.

[B-ROLL: two chairs at a table, then three.]

Receipt two, and it changes the shape rather than the date. In a survey of 600 high-net-worth individuals across Asia-Pacific, Europe and the United States, nearly half had inherited directly from their GRANDPARENTS.

TWO LABELS ON SCREEN AND SPOKEN, both binding: high-net-worth respondents only, so this is not a statement about ordinary families; and NOT a US-only sample. This is a shape, not a US statistic.

[B-ROLL: a single sheet of paper, three columns drawn by hand.]

What changes if both are true. The money arrives after the decisions it would have changed. And the person deciding may be a generation further away than you assumed, which means a different relationship, a different instrument, and usually a longer gap between the decision and the money.

[B-ROLL: the calendar again, now still.]

The honest limit, stated rather than hidden: one of these figures has no confirmed publication date and the other is not a US-only sample. Neither is strong enough to plan around on its own. What they are strong enough to do is break the default assumption, which was never sourced at all.

[B-ROLL: hands putting a document into a folder.]

Preserve-now thesis, without overpromising. If the transfer arrives later and from further away than people assume, then the things that do not depend on timing become the ones worth being deliberate about. A record exists or it does not. MySpawn stores one for $99 a year, billed annually. It is not an investment, it has no financial value, and it will not arrive in your fifties with a cheque attached.

[B-ROLL: the folder closing.]

Close: "You cannot choose when it arrives. You can choose what exists by then."
