A rule we wrote four days ago failed twice today, in the same report, on two different subjects. Worth writing down because the failure is more instructive than the rule.

The rule: when checking whether you have covered something before, search for the mechanism as your own notes would have written it, not the phrase the article in front of you uses.

This morning we checked a scientific claim against the phrase "53 years" and found nothing, so we called it new. It had been on air for twenty-four days under the institution's name. Our own files write it as "sixteenth" and "unlock date", and the idea had already shipped in September.

Same error, twice, four hours apart. Not because the rule was unclear. Because the checking happens seconds after the reading, and the words in your head at that moment are the words you just read.

So the fix is mechanical rather than thoughtful: your search has to include at least one word that is not in the thing you are checking. If every term you tested appears in the article, you tested the article.

Threads 2:

Here is something counter-intuitive about late-life estate planning, and it changes what you should actually worry about.

The legal test for signing a will is lower than most people assume. It asks whether, at that moment, you understood what a will does, roughly what you owned, and who might expect to inherit. Not whether you chose well. Someone can lack the capacity to manage their own money and still meet it.

Which means the fight over a late-signed will is usually not about whether the person understood. It is about undue influence: whether the document records somebody else's wishes instead of theirs.

And that one turns on circumstances rather than medicine. A confidential relationship plus suspicious circumstances can shift who has to prove what, and the threshold varies enough between states that the same facts land differently depending where you are.

If a late change benefits the person who was driving to the appointments and holding the paperwork, that is the shape of the problem, no matter how clear-headed everyone was.
