1. PODCAST EPISODE PACK (16-20 minutes)

Title options

  1. "Who Actually Checks the Ad?" (recommended: it is the question, and nobody in the audience knows the answer)

COLD OPEN (word for word, ~85 seconds)

If a genetics company puts a number on a billboard, who checks it?

 

Most people assume the answer is a government agency. Some assume nobody. Both of those are wrong, and the real answer is more interesting than either.

 

On the eleventh of September this year, a self-regulatory advertising body looked at the marketing for an embryo-screening product and recommended that the company modify or discontinue certain claims. The company said it would appeal.

 

I am not going to name the company and I am not going to spend this episode on them. I want to talk about the mechanism, because almost nobody in this audience knows it exists.

 

Here is why it matters to you rather than to them. There are three completely different systems that can look at a health-adjacent claim, they move at completely different speeds, and the one that moved first here is the one with no legal power at all.

 

And there is a reason I am the wrong person to tell you this and the right person to tell you this at the same time. We advertise. Everything in this episode applies to us.

SEGMENT 1 (~5 min): The thing that happened, told without a villain

Receipts.

  • 11 September 2026: a self-regulatory advertising body recommended that a genetics company modify or discontinue certain claims for an embryo-screening product. The company said it would appeal (trade release; not opened at its own domain; the company is NOT named on air).
  • What was challenged, described and not quoted at length: promotional material that assigned individual embryos specific values across a list of traits, including disease risks, physical characteristics and IQ.
  • STANCE, and it is a rail rather than an opinion: we do not name them. An ad built on another company's regulatory trouble is an ad about them, and we do not need a villain. A recommendation is also not a finding of wrongdoing, and an appeal is pending.

CLIP MOMENT: "I am not going to name them. If our argument needs somebody else to be in trouble, it is not an argument."

SEGMENT 2 (~5 min): Three systems, three speeds

Talking points, with the honest labels on.

  • Self-regulation moved first here. It has no legal power. It reviews advertising claims, issues recommendations, and its leverage is publication and referral rather than penalty.
  • Federal consumer-protection enforcement is slower, carries real penalties, and is a different threshold.
  • Medical and professional guidance is a third thing entirely and binds nobody who is not a clinician. On this specific product class, medical societies either have no guidelines for polygenic embryo screening or do not recommend it for estimating risks of complex disease, and the absence of regulation has left no standardised scoring method (statement about a field, not about a company).
  • STANCE: the gap between those three is where consumer genetics actually lives. A claim can be simultaneously legal, unendorsed by any professional body, and successfully challenged as advertising. That is not a loophole. It is three systems answering three different questions.

CLIP MOMENT: "A claim can be legal, unendorsed and still indefensible as an advertisement. Those are three different tests and the public thinks there is one."

SEGMENT 3 (~4 min): What this means when you are the buyer

Talking points.

  • The practical heuristic: ask which test a claim has passed. "FDA" means one thing, and we have a whole piece on what it does and does not mean. "Peer-reviewed" means another. "Nobody has made us take it down" means almost nothing.
  • Numbers on a chart are the easiest thing in the world to print and the hardest thing to substantiate. The presence of a decimal point is not evidence.
  • A pending appeal means nothing is settled, and we say so.

CLIP MOMENT: "The presence of a decimal point is not evidence."

SEGMENT 4 (~4 min): This applies to us, and here is where we are exposed

Talking points, against interest and specific rather than performative.

  • We advertise. The body that issued this recommendation reviews advertising. We are inside the same system.
  • What we would be judged on: our price, our storage claims, and our accreditation language. We say $99 a year, billed annually. We say samples are held at an independently accredited third-party facility, and the accreditations belong to that facility rather than to us. If we ever blur that, somebody should challenge it.
  • What we deliberately do not claim: no score, no prediction, no trait language, no health benefit. We are storage. That is a narrow claim and narrow claims are defensible.
  • THE ADMISSION: our own published pricing was wrong for forty-five days earlier this year, and the correction was applied to one log and missed another. A company that got its own price wrong does not get to lecture anybody about claim accuracy. It gets to show the correction.

OUTRO CTA

Ask which test a claim has passed. Then ask who would hear your complaint if it turned out to be wrong. Dates and the mechanism in the description.

2. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM EXPLAINER (7-9 minutes): "How a Maybe Becomes a Yes"

The explainer promised on 5 October for 8 October or later, re-briefed onto the mechanism. Delivered on the date.

Thesis: a variant of uncertain significance is not a medical finding. It is a bookkeeping state, and four separate things decide how long you stay in it.

Script with B-roll

[0:00-0:45] COLD OPEN. (B-roll: a printed report, one line highlighted, nothing else in frame.)

"Uncertain significance. It is the single most common thing a genetic test can tell you that is not an answer. It does not mean the lab found something worrying. It means the evidence is insufficient or conflicting, and the honest move was to say so. What almost nobody explains is what has to happen for that to change, so that is the whole of this video."

[0:45-2:15] THE FRAMEWORK. (B-roll: a five-column diagram drawn on paper, filled in as he speaks.)

"Classification runs on a framework published by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics with the Association for Molecular Pathology in 2015. Twenty-eight codified criteria, covering things like how common the variant is in the population, whether it tracks with disease in families, and what laboratory experiments say about the protein. Those criteria combine into five tiers: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain, likely benign, benign. Reclassification means new or re-weighed evidence has moved it between tiers. (Peer-reviewed framework, 2015, with later gene-specific specifications.)"

[2:15-3:45] WHAT ACTUALLY TRIGGERS A CHANGE. (B-roll: four cards laid down in sequence.)

"Four triggers. New population frequency data. New case reports or family segregation data. New functional studies. Or a revised gene-specific specification, which is the one people never mention. Expert panels write criteria tailored to individual genes, and when they do, the backlog moves. One study re-evaluated sixty-nine uncertain variants after a cardiomyopathy panel adapted the criteria for eight genes. In a paediatric arrhythmia cohort, twelve of twenty-three uncertain variants, fifty-two percent, were reclassified under the 2015 criteria. In one BRCA1 and BRCA2 series, thirty of ninety-three, about thirty-two percent. (Peer-reviewed studies, specific cohorts, not general rates; the numbers differ by gene and by method and I am not going to pretend otherwise.)"

[3:45-5:15] WHY IT TAKES LONGER FOR SOME PEOPLE. (B-roll: he writes four words on a card: DATA, PANEL, INTERVAL, CONTACT.)

"One: reference data. A variant gets resolved when enough other people carrying it have been catalogued, and most large genetic studies were done in people of European ancestry, so a variant in somebody from an under-represented group has less to be compared against. That gap is bookkeeping, not biology, and it is read as medicine.

Two: whether an expert panel has reached your gene yet. Some genes have tailored criteria. Most do not.

Three: your laboratory's reanalysis interval. And here is the part that should stop you. There are no guidelines for reinterpretation of prior genetic test results. Laboratories set their own intervals. (Stated in the literature; there is no national schedule to cite because there is not one.)

Four: whether anybody tells you. Re-contact is, in practice, usually something the patient has to initiate."

[5:15-6:30] WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO. (B-roll: a phone, a note being written.)

"Keep the laboratory's variant identifier. Not the summary sentence, the identifier. It is the only thing that lets anyone look it up later.

Ask your lab, in writing, two questions: do you reanalyse, and on what interval. If the answer is no interval, that is an answer and it is worth having.

Diarise a re-ask. Annually is more than enough.

And if a result matters to relatives, that is a genetic counsellor's job, not a group chat's."

[6:30-7:30] CLOSE, preserve-now thesis, no overpromising. (B-roll: the report again, put into a folder, folder closed.)

"Here is the honest version of why I care. The criteria get better. The reference databases get bigger. The expert panels work through the genes. The one thing that does not improve on its own is a sample that no longer exists to be re-examined, and a record nobody can find the identifier for. I am not telling you that your uncertain result will resolve. Most do not, and when they do, the overwhelming majority are downgraded to benign, which is good news told badly. I am telling you that the thing which decides whether you benefit from the next ten years of this is clerical. Keep the identifier. Ask about the interval. That is the whole ask."

HONEST-LABEL BLOCK, on screen at the end: 2015 ACMG/AMP framework, peer-reviewed. Cohort reclassification rates are from specific studies and are not general rates. The absence of a national reanalysis schedule is a statement about what does not exist and is sourced as such. The four-step process description is general practice and is labelled as such on screen.
