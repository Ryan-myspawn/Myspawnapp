#118 [FACEBOOK] "Your DNA test said 'no increased risk.' It probably means 'we didn't look.'" (~640 words)

Lane: practical legacy guide. Ends with a question.

There is a sentence in most consumer DNA reports that does more damage than any scary result ever has.

"No increased risk detected."

Here is what is usually underneath it.

Most consumer DNA tests do not read your genome. They check a list.

The technology is a genotyping array, and a typical consumer array reads on the order of 700,000 specific positions (vendor technical documentation; uncontroversial and consistent across sources).

Your genome is about 3.1 to 3.2 billion bases.

So a consumer array reads roughly two hundredths of one percent of it, and only the positions it was built to read.

That is not a flaw. It is the design. Arrays are cheap, fast and excellent at the things they were built for: relatedness, ancestry estimates, a defined set of known variants. The problem is the reporting language, because "we checked 700,000 addresses and your name wasn't on any of them" gets printed as "no increased risk."

The industry itself is moving. In October 2025, MyHeritage announced a move to whole genome sequencing, described in trade coverage as the first mainstream consumer company to offer it widely (company announcement plus trade reporting, not peer review; twelve months old, so a receipt rather than news).

Three practical consequences.

First: a negative consumer result is not a clinical negative. If there is real family history behind your worry, the test that answers it is one ordered by a clinician and run in a CLIA-certified laboratory against your actual question.

Second: an old array result cannot be upgraded. A 2018 result is a 2018 list. You cannot go back and ask it something new. The data file is finished. The only thing that can be asked a new question is the physical sample, if one still exists.

Third: the question list itself keeps moving. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics maintains the list of genes where an unexpected finding is worth telling you about. Three genes (ABCD1, CYP27A1 and PLN) were added effective 12 January 2026 (laboratory product update; we did not establish the list's total gene count and are not stating one). A sample analyzed in December 2025 was analyzed against a shorter list than the same sample would be today.

What to actually do with this.

Go find your report. Look for the word "array" or "genotyping" or a variant count. If it says 700,000-odd markers, you have a list check, not a genome.

Then ask the question almost nobody asks: does the physical sample still exist, and who has it? Many companies destroy it by default. Others retain it by default. Deleting your results online does not destroy the sample, and those are two different requests.

That is the one part of this that is still in your control. At MySpawn we store the physical sample at an accredited biorepository rather than selling you an interpretation, which is a deliberately narrow job: the sample stays readable by whatever exists later, and we make no claim about what will be found in it.

Here's my question for you: have you ever actually read what your DNA company says it does with the sample after the test is finished? Not the results. The sample.

#119 [X] "The most useful genetic test in medicine has had an FDA boxed warning on it since 2010. Most people who need it never get it." (~610 words)

Lane: contrarian discourse take. Punches at an idea, names no person.

Genomics sells itself on prophecy. What it is actually good at is dosing.

In March 2010 the FDA added a boxed warning to clopidogrel, the antiplatelet sold as Plavix, because the drug is less effective in people who poorly metabolize it through the liver enzyme CYP2C19 (FDA regulatory action, March 2010; reported contemporaneously in medical trade press).

A boxed warning is the strongest warning the FDA puts on a label.

How many people are affected: the FDA's own figure is roughly 2 to 14 percent of the population, varying substantially by ancestry (FDA, as reported in 2010 coverage). The relevant variants are principally CYP2C19 \2 and \3, with \4, \5, \6, \7 and \*8 associated with little or no metabolism but occurring less often.

Now the part that should bother you.

In the same action, the FDA notified physicians that testing was available and stopped short of recommending that patients be tested before starting the drug (FDA, March 2010, as reported).

Sixteen years later that is still the shape of pharmacogenomics: the knowledge is settled, the infrastructure exists, and the test is optional.

The infrastructure is not hypothetical. The FDA maintains a public Table of Pharmacogenetic Associations listing gene-drug pairs where it considers the evidence sufficient. The Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium publishes prescribing guidance for defined gene-drug pairs. Both exist, both are free, both are specific (we did not establish current counts for either and are deliberately not stating one; a "106 gene-drug pairs" figure circulates but traces to July 2020 reporting).

Here is the contrarian part, and it is an argument about priorities rather than about anybody's character.

The public conversation about genetics is dominated by prediction: what will I get, what will my child be like, what is my risk score. That conversation is scientifically the weakest part of the field. Polygenic scores explain a fraction of population-level variation and are not individual predictions.

Meanwhile the part that is strong, specific, actionable today and cheap is the boring part: whether a drug you are about to be prescribed will work in your body at the standard dose.

Prophecy gets the funding and the headlines. Dosing gets a footnote in a package insert.

I think that is backwards, and I think the reason is narrative rather than evidence. "Your genome predicts your future" is a story. "Your genome adjusts your milligrams" is a chore. Stories raise money.

Two honest limits, because this argument does not need to be overstated.

One: testing before prescribing is not free of cost or friction, and a regulator declining to mandate a test is not automatically a scandal. Reasonable people disagree about population-wide testing.

Two: pharmacogenomics is not preventive medicine. It tells you how to take a drug you already need. It does not keep you from needing it.

But those limits do not rescue the imbalance. One side of this field has an FDA boxed warning and a published prescribing table. The other side has a marketing deck.

#120 [FACEBOOK] "New Jersey kept newborns' blood for 23 years. Parents found out from a lawsuit." (~660 words)

Lane: story-driven, trust and custody. Ends with a question. ZERO PRODUCT: no MySpawn mention in this article.

Almost every baby born in the United States gets a heel prick in the first days of life. The blood goes onto a card, the card gets screened for a panel of serious treatable conditions, and that program is one of the genuinely great public-health successes of the last century.

Then there is the part nobody mentions. What happens to the card afterward.

New Jersey retained residual newborn blood spots for 23 years (as reported in state and legal coverage). Parents were not meaningfully told.

The Institute for Justice, a public-interest law firm, sued the state in November 2023 on behalf of parents who said they had no idea.

In June 2024, New Jersey Attorney General Matt Platkin issued a directive shortening the retention period, restricting how the spots could be used, and requiring clearer communication with parents (reported; the directive itself is the primary document).

In October 2025 the parents filed an amended complaint after a federal judge partially dismissed the case. The judge's reasoning is the part worth sitting with: the Fourteenth Amendment protects a parent's right to direct the care of their child, and the court held it does not extend to controlling the state's retention of residual dried blood spots (court ruling, as reported).

This is not one strange state.

Michigan officials agreed to destroy more than 3 million blood spots as a partial settlement in litigation brought by parents who argued they had not been given enough information to consent to research use (reported; the month was given in coverage without a year we could confirm, so we are not stating one). The Michigan case is Kanuszewski et al v Michigan Department of Health and Human Services.

Texas has previously destroyed over 5 million spots and Minnesota 1.1 million, both following similar legal challenges (reported; years not independently confirmed).

Add those three up and it is more than nine million samples destroyed because of how consent was handled.

Three things are true at once here, and the honest version keeps all three.

One: newborn screening saves lives. This is not an anti-screening argument and nobody should refuse the heel prick over it.

Two: screening and retention are different decisions. The screen takes days. The retention lasts decades. They were bundled, and the bundling is what people object to.

Three: a court has now said the constitutional route may not be available. Which means the answer, if there is one, is legislative and state by state. That is slow and unglamorous and it is where this actually gets decided.

What this is really about.

Most people believe that if something of theirs is being kept, somebody told them. That belief is doing a lot of unearned work. Here is a case where the sample is biological, the holder is a government, the period is measured in decades, and the notification was a line in paperwork handed to someone who had just given birth.

If that is the standard for a state, it is worth asking what the standard is for a company.

My question for you: do you know what happened to your own newborn screening card? Most people my age have never once thought about it, and depending on where you were born, it may still exist.

#121 [REDDIT r/SideProject] "Our automated price check passed for 40 days. It was checking the wrong folder." (~580 words)

Lane: story-driven ops, founder voice disclosed, ZERO PITCH, no links, first-party.

ADJACENCY DISCLOSED AND ARGUED, because this is the third ops-failure story in five days. #110 (1 October, r/SideProject) was a tool whose limits we had not mapped. #115 (3 October, r/Entrepreneur) was a search returning a 2009 bankruptcy as breaking news. This one is different in kind: the check was correct, the logic was correct, and it ran over the wrong set of files. If a fourth ops story comes up before 12 October it does not run.

I run a small DNA-storage product. We changed our pricing in August from one-time to annual. Standard stuff.

We added the obvious guardrail: an automated sweep for the retired phrases. "$99, once." "One-time." "No subscription." It runs before anything ships.

It has passed every single day since August.

Yesterday I found the old price in three of our published blog articles. Five separate instances. Forty days after the correction.

The sweep was not broken. The sweep was scoped wrong.

It ran over our logs, our changelogs, our internal production records. Everywhere we write about the content.

It did not run over the directory containing the content. The articles. The only files a customer ever reads.

And the reason it took forty days is the part I think is actually generalizable: the check kept returning green, and green is unreadable. A passing check carries no information about what it looked at. Nobody opens a green result and asks "yes, but over what?"

The morning before I found it, I had actually found the same error somewhere else and then swept "everywhere else" to confirm it was isolated. I swept the same wrong set, got a clean result, and wrote down that it was isolated. That is worse than the original bug, because now there was a confident sentence attached to it.

Three things I changed.

1. The sweep now runs over the whole repository, not a named list of directories. The named list was an optimization that saved maybe a second.

2. A passing check now prints what it covered. File count and path count, in the output. A green result that cannot tell you its own scope is a green result you cannot trust.

3. Anything customer-facing gets checked first, not last. We had the ordering exactly inverted: the most carefully monitored files were the ones nobody outside the company reads.

The uncomfortable bit. This is not an exotic failure. It is the most common one there is. The check that passes is the check nobody audits, and it will sit there passing for as long as you let it, because success is quiet.

If you have an automated guardrail you have not opened in a month, go check what it actually covers. Not whether it passes. What it passes over.

Mine covered everything except the product.

Founder here, writing about my own thing. No link, nothing to sign up for: the lesson is the post.
