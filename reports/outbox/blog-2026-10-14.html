What Belongs in a Safe Deposit Box, and What Does Not

A safe deposit box is a locked room you rent, and two things about it surprise almost everyone. Its contents are not FDIC insured and the bank's own rental agreement usually disclaims responsibility for them, so the only coverage is whatever your own policy provides. And the box does not open on your family's schedule after you die: depending on the state, access runs through a court petition, a sworn affidavit, or a limited search conducted in front of witnesses. That access rule, not the lock, is what decides whether the box helps anyone.

Nobody insures what is in it, including the bank

Under federal law a safe deposit box is not a deposit account. It is secured storage space rented to a customer, which means FDIC insurance does not reach the contents. [1] Cash sitting in a box is not protected the way cash in an account is.

The bank usually disclaims liability too, in the rental agreement you signed and did not read. [1] Flood, fire, employee error: read the contract, because it is the only thing standing between you and a loss.

So the coverage has to come from you. A homeowner's or renter's policy may extend to off-premises property, often at a reduced limit, and a scheduled personal articles policy covers named items properly. Currency is usually the hardest thing to insure and the easiest thing to lose. [1]

One correction worth making, because a search will hand you both versions. You will see it written that the FDIC insures the box but not the contents. That is wrong in a way that matters: the box is not an insured product at all. The distinction sounds pedantic until you are filing a claim.

The access problem is the whole problem

A box is excellent at keeping things safe and poor at making them available. It opens during banking hours, to an authorised person, with a key.

If the box is jointly rented, the surviving renter can generally open it with a death certificate and photo identification. [2] That is the cheap fix, and it is the one most people skip.

If it is rented in one name, access runs through the estate. Banks generally want the court's own paperwork, letters testamentary or letters of administration, naming the executor or administrator. [2] Which means the box is sealed during exactly the window when a family is looking for the will that tells them who the executor is. Our piece on [who keeps the family archive](/blog/who-keeps-the-family-archive) describes the same failure in a different form: the person who knows where everything is, is the person who died.

What the states actually do, and they do not agree

This is the part no general article will give you, and it is the part that decides your plan.

  • Nebraska: no court order. An executor, surviving spouse or heirs can gain access on an affidavit. [2]
  • North Dakota: similar, on a death certificate and affidavit. [2]
  • New York: a petition. You apply to the Surrogate's Court in the county where the decedent died for permission to open the box. [2]
  • Michigan: a petition too. Any party with an interest in the estate petitions the probate court. [2]
  • Missouri: a limited search, with witnesses. An executor may open the box to look for a will, trust documents or burial deeds, and it must be done in the presence of two employees of the financial institution. [2]
  • Florida: an initial opening and inventory, conducted in the presence of two of the following three: an employee of the institution, the personal representative, or the personal representative's attorney of record. [2]
  • Texas: two routes, one through a court order and one without, available to a family member or named executor. [2]

Epistemic label: these are practitioner summaries from probate and estate law firms, not statute text, and several of our sources disagree at the edges, including on whether New York requires a court appointment before any access at all. Treat the state examples as a map of how different the rules are, not as advice for your state. Check your state's probate code or the bank's estate services department. [2]

The pattern across all of them is worth more than any single rule: the limited-access states let someone look for a document and nothing else, under supervision. That tells you what the legislatures expect to be in the box, and what they expect families to be hunting for at the worst possible moment.

What belongs in it

Things that are hard to replace, rarely needed in a hurry, and not required to open the box itself.

  • Property deeds, vehicle titles, and other ownership documents.
  • Certified copies of birth, marriage and death certificates.
  • Military discharge papers.
  • An inventory of valuables, with photographs, for an insurance claim.
  • Physical items of real value you do not handle: heirloom jewellery, certificates, rare documents.

What does not

Anything your family will need before they can get into the box, and anything that loses money by sitting there.

  • The original will, and this is the big one. If the will names the executor and the executor's authority is what opens the box, the will has locked itself in. Some states allow a supervised search precisely because this happens so often.
  • Burial and funeral instructions. Decisions get made in days. Court access does not move in days.
  • Powers of attorney. A power of attorney is useless if it cannot be reached, and in most cases it dies with the principal anyway.
  • Passports and anything you may need outside banking hours. [1]
  • Cash. Not insured, not earning, and awkward to explain later. [1]
  • The only copy of anything. A box is a good second location and a poor single point of failure.

The rule underneath all of that: a document that authorises action should never be stored somewhere that requires authorisation to open. Keep the originals of those with your attorney or in a fireproof home safe, and tell one person where they are.

Two things to do this week

  1. Add a joint renter or a deputy, if your bank offers one. It is the single cheapest fix in this article and it converts a court petition into a death certificate.
  2. Write down what is in the box and where the keys are, and put that list somewhere that is not the box. A list in a box is a joke the bank has heard before.

Frequently asked

Who can open a safe deposit box after the owner dies?

It depends on the state and on whether the box was jointly rented. A surviving joint renter can generally open it with a death certificate and photo ID. For a sole-rented box, access usually runs through the estate, with the bank asking for letters testamentary or letters of administration. Some states, including Nebraska and North Dakota, allow access on an affidavit with no court order; others, including New York and Michigan, require a petition to the court.

Can the bank open the box to look for the will?

In several states, yes, as a limited search and nothing more. Missouri permits an executor to open the box to look for a will, trust documents or burial deeds, in the presence of two employees of the financial institution. Florida provides for an initial opening and inventory in the presence of two of three named people. Check your own state, because the limited-search route does not exist everywhere.

Are safe deposit box contents FDIC insured?

No. Under federal law a safe deposit box is not a deposit account, so FDIC insurance does not cover what is inside it, and the bank's rental agreement usually disclaims liability as well. Coverage has to come from a homeowner's, renter's or scheduled personal articles policy. You will see it claimed that the FDIC insures the box but not the contents; that is wrong.

Should I keep my will in a safe deposit box?

Generally no, and it is the most common mistake in this area. If the will names the executor, and the executor's authority is what persuades the bank to open the box, the document has locked itself in. Keep the original with your attorney or in a fireproof home safe and tell one person where it is.

What is the cheapest thing I can do to fix this?

Add a joint renter or a deputy to the box, if your bank offers it, and keep a written inventory of the contents somewhere other than the box. The first converts a court petition into a death certificate. The second means somebody knows the box is worth opening.
