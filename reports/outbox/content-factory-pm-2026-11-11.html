2 THREADS POSTS

Threads 1:

There is no regulator for the thing you are probably most worried about.
Investigative genetic genealogy is the technique where a crime-scene profile is uploaded to a consumer-style database, matched to distant relatives, and worked back down a family tree to a name. It has closed cases nobody else could.
The ISOGG wiki states there is currently no legal or regulatory oversight of it in the United States or elsewhere. It also notes an accreditation programme launched in August 2026, and I could not confirm that date, so treat it as reported.
A bill introduced in New Jersey this year would build an accreditation framework for genealogy centres and define what counts as a searchable database. Introduced is not passed.
So the honest state of it: a powerful technique, a few state statutes, one federal interim policy, and an accreditation scheme that is new. Not a vacuum. Not a system either.

Threads 2:

A study asked people whether they would opt in to letting police search their DNA profile, and the result that stuck with me was not the overall number.
It was that women were less likely to opt in than men.
I do not think that is squeamishness, and I would be careful about any explanation offered quickly. The useful observation is narrower: an opt-in rate is not a fact about a technology. It is a fact about who is being asked and what they have reason to expect.
Which is worth holding onto whenever a company tells you most users agreed to something. Most of which users.
Source is a discrete choice experiment published in Genetics in Medicine Open. Peer-reviewed, and a stated preference rather than a recorded behaviour, which is a real limit.
