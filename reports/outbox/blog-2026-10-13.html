What a Records Request Actually Looks Like

Requesting your own records has three moving parts: who holds them, what they may charge you, and how long they have to answer. The most useful thing to know before you start is that a vital records fee usually buys a search rather than a document, and is usually not refunded when the search finds nothing. Under HIPAA a provider has 30 days to respond to your request for your own records, with one 30-day extension on written notice. The fee cap on that right applies when the records come to you, and a 2020 federal ruling removed it when you direct them to a third party. Those facts determine how you word the request.

You are buying a search, not a document

Virginia's vital records FAQ describes its charge as $15.00 per certificate or search, and says there is no refund if the certificate is not found. [1] That is the standard structure, not a Virginia quirk. You are paying a clerk to look, and "no record exists under that name on that date" is a delivered service. Our guide to [legacy planning when your family health history is a blank](/blog/legacy-planning-no-family-health-history) covers which states release what; this is what the counter asks you for once you get there.

So every detail you supply narrows the search, and a narrow search is the only kind that succeeds. Full name at birth, date and city or county of birth, both parents' full names including a mother's name before marriage. If you are guessing at the year, say so: a clerk searching a range is a different request from a clerk searching a date.

The vital records office: the form, and the second fee

Take Virginia, because its law is the newest. Adult adoptees whose original certificate was sealed have been able to request a copy from the State Registrar without a court order since 1 July 2026. [1] The procedure, per the state's FAQ: submit the application for certification of a birth record, include a legible photo ID copy, pay the issuance fee, and file by mail or in person at the Office of Vital Records in Richmond. The current application carries the designation VS6B, revised 07/2026. [1]

A separate birth parent contact preference form is a different transaction with its own fee. One datapoint on scale, from a local broadcaster rather than the agency: more than 150 adoptees requested their original certificates in the law's first two weeks. [2] Directional only.

Now the second fee, where budgets go wrong. Most states contract remote ordering to one vendor, whose fee sits on top of the certificate fee:

  • North Carolina: $13.95, nonrefundable, and the state says VitalChek is the only authorized vendor for remote certified orders. [3]
  • San Diego County, California: $12.95 on top of a $34 certificate. [3]
  • Indiana: $12.95, said to cover online ordering, identity checks and expediting. [3]
  • Idaho: $10.50, nonrefundable on all orders. [3]
  • Massachusetts: a $12 vendor fee folded into a $54 first-copy online price. [3]

Two things follow. A remote order really costs the certificate fee plus roughly ten to fourteen dollars, and both parts can be nonrefundable. And ordering in person at the state office avoids the vendor fee entirely, with Virginia in-person requests often fulfilled the same day. [1] Within driving distance of the capital, driving is cheaper and faster.

The provider: a 30-day clock and one ruling that matters

If you need a medical chart rather than a certificate, you are using the HIPAA right of access, and it has a clock.

A covered entity must act within 30 calendar days of receiving your request, and may take one 30-day extension if it tells you so in writing inside the original window. [4] A shorter state deadline controls where one exists, and partial production inside the window followed by the rest later does not satisfy the requirement. [4] The clock governs the answer, not the record's survival: as [getting a parent's DNA results after death](/blog/parents-dna-results-after-death) covered, HIPAA sets no retention period at all, which is left to state law.

On fees, the rule limits the charge to reasonable cost-based items: labor for copying, supplies, postage, and a summary you agreed to in advance. Retrieval, verification and general administrative overhead are not chargeable. [4] Worth quoting back in a letter, because a bill for "record retrieval" is a bill for something the rule excludes.

Here is the part almost nobody is told. In January 2020 the federal district court in Washington, D.C. vacated the 2016 guidance that had extended the patient fee limit to records sent to third parties, in Ciox Health, LLC v. Azar, No. 18-cv-0040 (D.D.C.). [5] The Office for Civil Rights then confirmed the limit applies to a request for access to your own records and not to a request to transmit records to a third party. [5]

So: ask for the records to be sent to you, then forward them yourself. The same chart, routed to your lawyer or new clinic at your direction, can be priced without the cap.

The sperm bank: two stages, and a hard limit

A donor records request is not one form. At The Sperm Bank of California, a donor-conceived adult submits an initial request form; the bank matches it against its records; staff then email the remaining forms and an explanation of the process. [6] The identity-release undertaking covers the donor's name, date and place of birth, and last known contact details. [6]

The limit is what to plan around. That program is a program, with enrolled donors. Policies vary widely between banks, and a bank can release only what it holds and what the donor agreed to. [6] So ask two questions in writing, because they have different answers: what identifying information will you release, and what medical and genetic screening information. A bank may answer yes to the second and no to the first.

When the custodian no longer exists

If the practice closed, the records did not necessarily vanish, and what you want has a name: the custodian of record. Records are generally transferred to a successor provider, a facility, or a third party retained to hold them for the rest of the retention period.

The most useful call you can make is to the state medical board, asking "who was designated custodian of record for this practice." Boards often hold that designation because the closure had to be reported to them. The same logic runs through [how long labs keep your DNA sample](/blog/how-long-labs-keep-dna-sample) and [whether hospitals keep newborn blood samples](/blog/do-hospitals-keep-newborn-blood-samples): the question is never only how long, it is who holds it now.

The letter

I am requesting a copy of my complete medical record under my HIPAA right of access. Please send it to me at the address above, in electronic form if available. Please confirm in writing the date you received this request, and if you intend to take the 30-day extension, the reason and the expected date. If there is a fee, please itemise it before processing.

Short, dated, specific, and asking for a receipt. Two deliberate choices: delivery to you rather than a third party, for the fee reason above, and the fee itemised in advance, which is what shows whether you are being billed for retrieval.

Frequently asked

How long does a provider have to give me my medical records?

30 calendar days from receiving your request, with one 30-day extension if the provider notifies you in writing within the original window. A shorter state deadline applies where one exists. Producing part of the record inside the window and the rest later does not satisfy the rule.

Can I be charged for a records request?

Yes, but the HIPAA right of access limits the charge to reasonable cost-based labor for copying, supplies, postage, and an agreed summary. Retrieval, verification and general overhead are not chargeable, so ask for the fee itemised before processing. The cap does not survive a third-party directive: a 2020 federal ruling vacated the guidance extending it to records sent elsewhere at your request, so have them sent to you and forward them yourself.

Which form do I use to request an original birth certificate?

It is a state form from the state where you were born, filed with that state's vital records office. In Virginia the application for certification of a birth record carries the designation VS6B with a 07/2026 revision, the fee is $15 and a legible photo ID copy is required. Confirm the current form on the state's own site before mailing.

What if the clinic that holds my records has closed?

Ask the state medical board who was designated custodian of record for that practice. Records are generally transferred to a successor, a facility or a retained third party rather than destroyed at closure.

Will a sperm bank tell me my donor's medical history?

Ask it as a separate question from identity. Programs differ sharply between banks, and a bank can release only what it holds and what the donor agreed to. At The Sperm Bank of California the process starts with an initial request form, after which staff email the remaining forms.
