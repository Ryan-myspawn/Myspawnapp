How Long Does Probate Actually Take?

Short answer: for a straightforward estate with a valid will, roughly six to twelve months in most states, with national averages quoted anywhere between sixteen and twenty months once complicated cases are included. But the honest answer is more useful than the number. The published ranges disagree with each other, sometimes about the same state, and almost none of them come from court data. What follows is what the ranges actually say, where they conflict, and the four variables that move your case.

The ranges, and the fact that they fight

Here is what a sweep of current guides produces. Read the disagreements, not just the midpoints.

| Tier | States commonly listed | Range quoted |

|---|---|---|

| Fastest | Colorado, Wisconsin | 3 to 6 months |

| Fast | Texas, Utah, Wyoming | 4 to 8 months |

| Typical | most states, simple estate, valid will | 6 to 12 months |

| Slow | New York, Pennsylvania | 12 to 24+ months |

| Slowest | California | 9 to 18 months, or 18 to 24+ on a backed-up docket |

National averages quoted: about 16 months in one source, 20 months in another, and 6 to 12 months in a third for an uncontested estate with modest assets. Those are not three measurements of the same thing. The first two include contested and complex estates; the third excludes them.

The clearest conflict is Florida, and it is worth naming because it shows what these guides are. Some list Florida at 6 to 12 months. Others list the same state at 9 to 18 months. Nothing in between explains the gap.

Label, stated plainly: almost every source in this category is a marketing blog for a probate service, not a court statistics office. None of the figures above is an official average. If you need a reliable number for one state, the state court system or a probate attorney in that state is the source, and this article is not a substitute for either.

Variable one: the creditor claim period, which is a floor rather than a delay

This is the single most load-bearing number in the whole process, and most people planning around probate have never heard of it.

After someone dies, creditors get a window to make claims against the estate. Across sources that window typically adds three to six months to any case. It is not a queue or a backlog. It is a statutory waiting period that exists so the estate can close knowing what it owes, and an executor who distributes before it expires can be personally exposed.

California's rule is the one the research could state precisely: a creditor's window is generally the later of four months after the first publication of the notice to creditors, or sixty days after actual notice is mailed or delivered to that creditor. Claims after that are at the court's discretion. There is also an outer limit of roughly one year from death, after which recovery generally ends, though at least one source describes courts allowing a later claim where equity demands it.

A citation caution, because this is exactly the kind of thing that gets copied wrong. One source attributes that four-month rule to California Probate Code section 19100. That section sits in the division governing claims against TRUSTS, not against a decedent's estate administered in probate. The rule as described is consistent with California practice, but this article does not vouch for that pinpoint section number, and neither should anything that cites us.

New York could not be pinned down at all. One source gives seven months from the date letters were issued to the representative. Another gives six months after publication of notice. These are different clocks starting at different events, and one of the two sources is an AI-generated answer site. Reported as unresolved rather than averaged.

Texas and Florida creditor windows: not found. The sweep returned nothing authoritative for either. That gap is stated rather than filled with a plausible number.

Variable two: the court's docket, which has nothing to do with you

California appears at the slow end of every list, and the reason given is consistently volume rather than complexity. The same estate, with the same documents and the same heirs, closes faster in a county with a shorter calendar. This is the part of the timeline that no amount of preparation changes.

Variable three: what the estate owns, not what it is worth

Two things reliably extend a case regardless of dollar value.

Real property. Selling a house inside probate adds a sale timeline to a court timeline, and the two do not overlap neatly.

Property in another state. A second, separate proceeding in that state typically follows. This is why an estate that looks simple on paper can take twice as long as a larger estate whose assets all sit in one jurisdiction.

Variable four: whether anyone objects

A contested will is the difference between the twelve-month figure and the twenty-four-month-plus figure. The national averages near sixteen and twenty months are pulled upward by exactly this category, which is why they sit so far above the six-to-twelve figure quoted for uncontested estates. If nobody objects, the higher averages are not describing your case.

The shortcut that is not probate

Many estates never go through full probate at all. Small estate procedures can close in 30 to 90 days where the estate qualifies. That is a different process with its own dollar ceilings and its own waiting period, and we have written about it separately rather than squeezing it in here.

What to actually do with this

1. Find your state's creditor claim period first. It is the floor under every other estimate, and it is the number most planning ignores.

2. Ask whether property sits in more than one state. If it does, assume a second proceeding and plan the calendar around that rather than around the headline range.

3. Treat any single published figure with suspicion, including the ones in the table above. When two guides disagree about the same state by nine months, at least one of them is wrong.

4. Separate the question "when does the estate close" from "when can people get at specific things." They are not the same date, and conflating them is where most frustration comes from.

FAQ

How long does probate take on average?

Published figures disagree. Guides quote national averages of about sixteen months and about twenty months when complex and contested estates are included, and six to twelve months for a straightforward uncontested estate with a valid will. None of these are official court statistics.

Which state has the slowest probate?

California appears at the slow end of every comparison, quoted at nine to eighteen months for a typical estate and eighteen to twenty-four months or more where the court's docket is backed up. The stated reason is case volume rather than legal complexity.

Why does probate take months even when there is no dispute?

Because of the creditor claim period. Creditors get a statutory window to bring claims, commonly adding three to six months, and an executor who distributes before it closes can be personally exposed. It is a waiting period by design, not a backlog.

Can probate be faster than six months?

Yes, in two situations. Some states are quoted at three to six months for straightforward cases, and small estate procedures can close in roughly thirty to ninety days where the estate qualifies under that state's dollar ceiling.

Does owning property in two states make probate longer?

Usually yes. Property in another state typically requires a separate proceeding there, which runs on its own timeline. An otherwise simple estate can take substantially longer than a larger estate whose assets sit in one jurisdiction.

Is a stored DNA sample part of probate?

Not if it is held under a storage contract. A sample held by a third-party biorepository under an agreement is governed by that agreement rather than distributed as an estate asset, so its status does not wait on the probate calendar.
