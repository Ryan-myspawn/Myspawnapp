COLD OPEN (word for word, ~80 seconds)

For about forty years, a man could walk into a clinic, donate, sign a form promising he would never be identified, and walk out. That promise was the product. It is how the whole system recruited.

 

That promise stopped being deliverable in about 2005, and almost nobody told the men who had already signed.

 

In late 2004 a fourteen-year-old who had been conceived with donor sperm sent his own DNA to a consumer testing company. He used the Y-chromosome matches, some public records and a search engine. He had his biological father's name in nine days. He was fourteen.

 

Nothing had been leaked. No clinic had broken a confidence. The donor had not taken a test. A relative of his had, which was enough.

 

And here is the part that matters for anybody who has ever handed over biological material for any reason: the promise did not get broken. It got obsoleted. The people who made it in good faith had no idea that a technology arriving twenty years later would quietly reach back and undo it.

 

In June 2022, one state finally wrote down what was already true. I want to talk about what it wrote, what it did not, and why the date on the form matters more than anything anybody promised you.

SEGMENT 1 (~5 min): A promise that a later technology reached back and cancelled

Receipts.

  • The mechanism is the one people still misunderstand: the donor does not have to test. A relative testing is enough, which is why no individual decision can restore the promise.

SEGMENT 2 (~5 min): What Colorado actually wrote, with every date attached

Receipts.

  • Colorado SB 22-224, the Donor-Conceived Persons and Families of Donor-Conceived Persons Protection Act, signed June 2022. The first US law abolishing anonymous gamete donation.
  • Three dates and they get muddled constantly: signed June 2022, took effect 1 July 2024, and applies to donations made on or after 1 January 2025.
  • What it does: a donor-conceived person may learn their genetic parent's name at 18; identifying information stays private until then; access to the donor's personal and family medical history; a worldwide limit of 25 families from any one donor with no cap on children per family; gamete banks and clinics must hold a Colorado licence and periodically ask donors to update medical history and contact details.
  • And the line nobody is discussing: donor records are subject to PERMANENT RETENTION.
  • STANCE: almost every genetic-privacy argument of the last five years treats indefinite retention as the harm. Colorado legislated the opposite, for a group whose injury was deletion. Both positions are right about different people, and the real question is not how fast data is destroyed. It is who decides, and whether the person the record is about gets a vote before the clock runs out.

CLIP MOMENT: "A retention period set by a company's convenience is not a privacy policy. A destruction schedule the subject cannot override is not protection either."

SEGMENT 3 (~4 min): The boundary the law draws through living people

Receipts.

  • The act is not retroactive. It applies to donations made on or after 1 January 2025.
  • So a person conceived from a donation made on 2 January 2025 acquires, at 18, a statutory right to their genetic parent's name. A person conceived from a donation made on 30 December 2024 does not, and nothing in this act gives it to them. Same state, same clinics, same need.
  • STANCE: this is not a criticism of the drafting. Non-retroactivity is standard, and the alternative, reopening promises of anonymity already made to past donors, is its own serious problem. The point is that every retention rule has a cohort it was written too late for, and those are the people who go looking and find the record was never kept, or cannot be opened.
  • The broader point, said once and not laboured: the rules here move one state at a time, and what protects you in the meantime is a record somebody is obliged to keep.

CLIP MOMENT: "Two people conceived four days apart. One of them has a right. The other has a story about a form."
