Cold open, word for word (about 85 seconds)

"I want to talk about a failure that does not look like one.

Not a made-up number. Not a press release dressed as a study. A real number, properly measured, published in a real journal, by people who did the work carefully. And still useless to you.

SEGMENT 1: The figure with a passport (about 5 minutes)

The case: a strong study, correctly reported, wrongly generalised.

The facts, all labelled. A study led by Dr Charlotte Booth of the UCL Centre for Longitudinal Studies, published February 2026, finding that one in ten of Gen Z had had a child by age 23, against one in four millennials at a similar age. The sample is the Millennium Cohort Study: almost 10,000 people born across the UK in 2000 to 2002. [^1][^2]

Talking points with receipts.

  • A birth cohort is the strong kind of evidence, not the weak kind. These are the same people, followed from birth. It is better than a survey and that is exactly why the mistake is dangerous: the quality of the method makes you less likely to check the population.
  • The second finding from the same study, which almost nobody quotes: more than two thirds are living with their parents at 23, around three times the rate for millennials a decade earlier. [^3] That is a claim about when adulthood starts, not about wanting children. The study also reports that the vast majority still plan to have children.
  • The headline version says young people are not having children. The study says they are having them later and still intend to.
  • One number disagrees with itself across retellings: one secondary source gives the millennial comparison as 24 percent by age 25, where UCL's own pages say one in four by 23. An age cutoff that moves two years in transit is its own small lesson.

CLIP MOMENT: "It is a better study than most of what you read, and that is the reason it fooled us. Good method makes you forget to ask who."

SEGMENT 2: The number that answers a different question (about 6 minutes)

The case: a company's own document, correctly reported, measuring something other than the thing being sold.

What we can say, and every word of it is labelled. A company in embryo screening markets a model as selecting for a large gain on an intelligence measure. Its own technical whitepaper is reported to contain within-family accuracy figures and an expected SPREAD between the highest and lowest scoring of five embryos of about 10.8 points. An independent analysis puts the GAIN against an average embryo at roughly half that, near 5. [^4]

THE DISTINCTION IS THE WHOLE SEGMENT. A SPREAD IS NOT A GAIN. Top minus bottom is not top minus average. If you pick the best of five, you are not beating the worst of five. You are beating the middle.

And the floor, which is the strongest source in this episode. **Karavani and colleagues, Cell, 2019, "Screening Human Embryos for Polygenic Traits Has Limited Utility", estimated an average gain from screening of about 2.5 points with the scores available then.** [^5]

Talking points with receipts.

  • Four numbers, one subject: a marketing figure, a company-reported spread, a derived gain, and a peer-reviewed estimate. They descend in that order, and they are not describing the same quantity.
  • Every one of them is a model estimate rather than a measured outcome, INCLUDING the peer-reviewed one. Say that on air.
  • The company is not named in the audio. Not for legal caution: because naming it turns a method episode into a hit piece, and because our own file still carries conditions on the figures.
  • The whitepaper itself could not be opened. We know its address. We could not read it. That limit is spoken in the segment, not hidden in a show note.

CLIP MOMENT: "A spread is not a gain. Top minus bottom is not top minus average. One of those is a sales figure and one of them is the answer."

SEGMENT 3: Turn it on ourselves, again (about 4 minutes)

Yesterday's episode scored us on evidence. This one asks the provenance question about our own claims, which is a different and less comfortable exercise.

  • "$99 a year, billed annually." Measured on: our own price list. Provenance: perfect. There is no sampling problem in a price.
  • "An independent, CAP-accredited, ISO 9001 and ISO 20387, FDA-registered biorepository in New Jersey." Measured on: GenVault's accreditations, which are GenVault's and not ours. We state them and we have still not published the certificates. That remains a partial, and it was a partial yesterday too.
  • Every preservation statistic we have ever quoted. Dried blood spots, cryogenic studies, cord blood excursions. Provenance: laboratories and biobanks, not MySpawn. We have never run a long-term study on our own samples, because we have not existed long enough to have one. Anyone who tells you their consumer storage product has decades of outcome data on its own customers is describing a company older than this category.

CLIP MOMENT: "We have no long-term data on our own samples. We have been open for months. The honest version of our claim is that we have copied an established method, not that we have proved it."

SEGMENT 4: The method, in four questions (about 3 minutes)

Who was counted. How many. Where. And is the thing being measured the thing being sold?

That fourth one is the new addition and it is what segment 2 is for. The first three catch a population problem. The fourth catches a quantity problem, which is subtler and more common in marketing.

Outro CTA (about 40 seconds)

"Four questions. Who, how many, where, and is the measured thing the sold thing.

Use them on us. We store a physical DNA record for ninety-nine dollars a year, billed annually, with a named independent biorepository, and we have no long-term outcome data of our own because we have not been open long enough to have any. That is the whole claim, and its provenance is the only part of it we can fully vouch for."
