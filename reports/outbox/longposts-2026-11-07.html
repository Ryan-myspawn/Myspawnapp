#195 [X] The number everyone quotes is the one that matters least

Zero-product: no. One mention at the end. 40-60 reading: anyone who has written a will assuming it decides where things go.

In most of the United States a surviving spouse who is left out of a will can refuse the will and claim a statutory share instead. It is called an elective share, sometimes a forced share. Roughly forty states have one.

The number you will read is one third to one half. That number is the least useful thing about it.

Here is what actually decides the case: a percentage of what.

Some states apply the share to the probate estate only, meaning property that passes under the will. Others apply it to an augmented estate, a larger pool built by statute that reaches specified non-probate transfers: certain joint accounts, payable-on-death designations, some trusts, some lifetime gifts. (Cornell Legal Information Institute, spousal share: https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/spousal_share, via search index: verify before posting.)

That difference is usually the whole case. A person whose assets mostly pass outside the will leaves a small probate estate. In a probate-only state, a percentage of a small number is a small number. In an augmented-estate state, the same arrangement gets pulled back in and counted.

Now the four figures that do not reconcile, which is the real finding.

The commonly cited range is one third to one half. The Uniform Law Commission has suggested fifty per cent. Florida's statute is quoted at thirty per cent of the elective estate. West Virginia uses a sliding scale keyed to the length of the marriage, with one reform scheme's included share starting at three per cent for a marriage of under a year. (Ward and Smith summary and the West Virginia Code, both via search index: verify before posting.)

A single number, a model number, a different fixed number, and a schedule starting at three per cent are four different answers to "how much".

So I am not going to give you a percentage for your state, and the reason is not caution. A percentage quoted without its base, its state and its version year is wrong in a predictable direction: too high for someone in a probate-only state after a short marriage, too low for someone in an augmented-estate state.

Three exceptions that most coverage omits, and they are not small.

At least one state is reported to have no elective share statute at all. Community property states generally do not recognise elective rights, because a spouse already owns a half interest during the marriage: a protection that arrives earlier is not the absence of one. And the right can usually be waived by prenuptial or postnuptial agreement, which is often why an expected claim goes nowhere.

Notice the shape of that. The general rule and its exceptions appear on different pages. The exceptions are usually the newer information.

One more thing, and it has a clock on it. Elective share rights have deadlines tied to probate or to a notice. I am not naming a period, because they differ by state and a missed window is the commonest way this right disappears. How much comes after by when.

We store a DNA record for $99 a year, billed annually. It is not an estate asset, no spouse would elect into it, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

#196 [FACEBOOK] Being named executor is a job, and the first thing it hands you is a clock

Zero-product: no. One mention at the end. 40-60 reading: the man who has just been told he is named in a parent's will and assumes it means signing something.

Somebody names you executor because they trust you. What they have actually done is hand you a set of deadlines, a duty you can breach, and personal exposure if you get the order wrong.

The deadlines are real and they are not the same anywhere. One guide puts the Texas estate inventory at within 90 days. Florida guidance says within 60 days. California says within four months of appointment, naming Forms DE-160 and DE-161. North Carolina sources contradict each other in front of you: one says three months after qualification unless extended, another says 90 days from Letters. (State guides and law-firm pages, via search index: verify before posting.)

Read that last one again, because it is the lesson. Two sources describing the same state give two different answers. If you are an executor, the probate court's own instructions are the only thing worth relying on, and they are usually free.

The notices come before the distributions, and that order is the part people get wrong. Florida guidance requires notifying beneficiaries of the administration and publishing a creditor notice. Minnesota requires formal notice to all named beneficiaries and heirs-at-law. California guidance lists a 30-day creditor notice mailing among its key deadlines.

One source calls the creditor notice the most missed step and says skipping it can create personal liability. Label, and it matters: that particular page is marketing material, not a law firm or a court. Treat the claim as unverified and ask the court.

Here is where personal liability actually comes from. Distributing assets before paying taxes, or before a legitimate creditor's window closes, is the textbook example. So is mixing money: one source describes depositing estate funds into your personal account, even temporarily, as a serious breach of fiduciary duty.

And liability does end. In North Carolina the clerk issues an order closing the estate and releasing the executor. That document is the point of the whole exercise and almost nobody mentions it.

Four things to do in order, if this is you.

Get the court's own checklist before you read anything else. Open a separate estate account on day one and never touch it with personal money. Do the notices before the distributions, every time, even when the family is impatient. Write to the beneficiaries on a schedule, which one source recommends quarterly as best practice rather than law, because most executor disputes are about silence rather than money.

MySpawn stores a DNA record for $99 a year, billed annually, and none of the above applies to it. What does apply: account access is governed by the account, not the will, and an executor who assumes otherwise loses time.

If you have been named, do you know which court, and have you read its instructions?

#197 [X] Check the window before you check the arithmetic

ZERO PRODUCT MENTION. No link, no price, nothing to click. This is the per-run zero-product article. 40-60 reading: anyone who has ever found two different numbers for the same thing and assumed somebody was lying.

Something useful happened in our own files this week and the useful part is the method, not the subject.

A search turned up two different percentages sitting in our notes for what looked like the same quantity: 51.6 per cent and 52.4 per cent. For about a minute it looked like we had contradicted ourselves in writing, in a file we use to check other people's claims.

Then we opened the lines, and the contradiction evaporated in one step.

52.4 per cent is from a 2017 meta-analysis covering samples from 1973 to 2011. 51.6 per cent is from the 2022 update covering 1973 to 2018. Different papers. Different windows. Both correctly labelled where they appeared. (Both figures concern declines in sperm concentration and are reported in Human Reproduction Update; read through a search index: verify before posting.)

The search had manufactured a contradiction and the dates dissolved it.

That is worth a rule, because the failure mode is extremely common and extremely confident. When two numbers for the same quantity turn up in the same place, check the window before you check the arithmetic. Most apparent contradictions in published statistics are two honest measurements of two different periods, two different populations, or two different definitions.

Now the harder half, because the same week produced the opposite case and we are not going to only tell you the flattering one.

Our files state, in three separate entries, that the 2022 analysis found an annual decline of 2.64 per cent post-2000. A source this week gave that steeper period as after 2020, not 2000, and flagged it as something to check against the primary paper.

If the break year were 2020 rather than 2000, the acceleration story changes materially: a twenty-six-year trend and a six-year trend are not the same claim about the world.

We have not corrected anything, and here is the reasoning. One secondary source against three of our own entries is not a correction, it is a question. And none of the parties, including us, has read the paper. So it goes on the open-questions list and stays there until a second source raises the same date or somebody opens the paper.

Three things to take from a week of looking at our own numbers.

A contradiction is a hypothesis, not a finding. Open the lines before you write the correction. We nearly published one that did not exist.

A number without its window is not a number. Both of ours were fine. The search result that put them side by side was not.

And an unresolved date is allowed to stay unresolved. The instinct to pick the version you already published is strong, and it is the whole mechanism by which confident archives go quietly wrong.

#198 [FACEBOOK] The machine in the headlines does not do the thing it is named after

Zero-product: no. One mention at the end, and it is a disclaimer. 40-60 reading: anyone who has read "artificial womb" and filed it under things that are nearly here.

The devices reported as artificial wombs do not gestate anybody.

They support an infant who has already been delivered, usually by caesarean, by taking over one job the placenta does: gas exchange. A more honest name for the current technology is an artificial placenta.

Three design features tell you exactly what it is for.

There is no pump. The infant's own heart drives blood through the umbilical vessels into an external oxygenator and back, because adding a pump would risk overloading a fetal heart.

The infant stays in fluid, because lungs built to develop in liquid are injured by mechanical ventilation.

Access is through the umbilical cord, which is why caesarean delivery is a requirement rather than a preference.

What it does not replace is the reason it will not scale backwards. The placenta also runs endocrine functions and an immune interface between two genetically different bodies. Those are not features postponed to version two. They are why full ectogenesis is a different project, not a longer version of this one.

Now the part that made me write this.

An FDA advisory committee met in September 2023 to discuss human trials. To test in humans the developer needs an investigational device exemption. The technology holds FDA breakthrough device designation, which is a procedural status that speeds review and is not an approval and not evidence that anything works in humans. Animal testing is extensive: 2023 coverage puts it at roughly 300 lamb fetuses. (Scientific American and MIT Technology Review, 2023, via search index: verify before posting.)

And as of November 2026, I could not confirm from any retrievable source whether that exemption was granted or whether any human has been placed in such a system. Everything I can read is from 2023 or early 2024.

A three-year-old "trials could start soon" is not a current status. It is an old sentence being recirculated as a new one, and it is most of what you will find.

Two constraints that never reach a headline. Delivery must be by caesarean, and the infant would need anticoagulation to prevent clots where the tubes enter the body. Those are not footnotes. They define who this could ever be for.

And two things in circulation that are not real. "EctoLife" is a 2022 concept video, not a company, a prototype or a facility. The viral Chinese "pregnancy robot" is a fabrication assessed as AI-generated. If a source cites either as evidence of progress, that tells you what the source is worth.

We store DNA records and we are not in this picture at all. Storage is not a reproductive technology, we do not benefit from this field advancing, and we would be lying if we implied otherwise.

When a technology is named after what it resembles, what would you have to ask to find out what it actually replaces?

#199 [X] Seventy is a checkpoint, not a threshold

Zero-product: no. One mention at the end. 40-60 reading: this one is written for the oldest readers in the band and for the children reading on their behalf.

There is a myth that estate planning gets harder after seventy because of age. It does not. It gets harder because of evidence.

A capacity challenge does not turn on how old someone was. It turns on the facts at the moment of signing, and the single strongest protection is documentation created at that moment rather than reconstructed afterwards.

One firm puts it directly: a contemporaneous medical assessment done at the time of estate planning is far more persuasive than a retrospective opinion. (Law-firm commentary, Canadian and US, via search index: verify before posting. Jurisdiction matters here more than usual.)

Four practical consequences, and none of them is about age.

Timing the signing. One firm suggests scheduling it at the time of day when mental acuity is sharpest. That is not a legal rule. It is an observation that capacity is not constant within a day, and the signing is the moment that gets litigated.

Who witnesses it. Practice notes suggest the lawyer should review the document in person and watch for changes in capacity, and that a second attorney may witness the signing. Some advisers go further and suggest a capacity declaration from a geriatric psychiatrist, or a certificate of independent review, where a document is likely to be contested.

Who else is in the room. One source flags a specific and common pattern: a caregiver who becomes agent, fiduciary and beneficiary under the same person's documents. That combination is what later disputes are built from, regardless of anyone's intentions.

What the lawyer is supposed to be doing. Practice guidance expects capacity to be checked twice: when the document is drafted and again when it is signed. Those can be weeks apart, and a document signed by someone who was assessed only at the first of them has a gap in it.

And the audit nobody does. Beneficiary designations are the fastest thing to change and the easiest to leave stale. One source also recommends reviewing successor fiduciary and agent appointments across every document, plus the terms of any continuing trusts, which is the part people skip because nothing about it feels urgent. One checklist recommends a yearly beneficiary review plus an immediate one after any major life event, naming 401(k) and 403(b) plans, payable-on-death bank accounts and 529 plans. It also flags formula clauses tied to the federal estate-tax exemption as needing review, listing the exemption as $15 million in 2026. Label: that figure comes from an advisory checklist, not from the tax code, and should be confirmed before anyone relies on it.

Here is the thing worth carrying. Older clients often need more frequent reviews, not fewer, because more changes in their lives than births and deaths. The planning that fails is almost never the planning that was wrong when it was written. It is the planning that was right in 2014.

Seventy is a useful date in the diary. It is not a cliff, and treating it as one is how people put off the review that would have mattered.
