## #112 [X] DNA does not die of old age. It dies of water.

**ZERO PRODUCT. Science-explainer.**

Two numbers that look like they cannot both be true.

**In 2012, researchers measured the half-life of DNA in bone at 521 years.** They did it properly: 158 radiocarbon-dated moa fossils from New Zealand, specimens between roughly 600 and 8,000 years old, published in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*. **That 521-year figure is for a 242 base-pair stretch of mitochondrial DNA at an effective burial temperature of 13.18 degrees Celsius.** (Peer-reviewed, 2012.)

**In 2022, a team published DNA that is two million years old.** Environmental DNA from the Kap Kobenhavn Formation in North Greenland, in *Nature*, identifying poplar, birch and thuja, plus hare, reindeer, rodents, geese and mastodon. (Peer-reviewed, December 2022.)

Half-life of 521 years. Readable material at 2,000,000 years. **Both are correct, and the gap between them is the most useful thing in this post.**

**The half-life is not a shelf life.**

Decay in that study followed first-order kinetics, which means it is a rate, not an expiry date. **A half-life of 521 years does not mean DNA is gone at 1,042 years.** It means half the bonds in that particular fragment length have broken. Short fragments survive vastly longer than long ones, because there is less of them to break. Ancient genomics does not read intact chromosomes. It reads confetti and reassembles it.

**The second number has a mechanism, and the mechanism is the lesson.** The Greenland authors attribute that survival to **DNA binding to mineral surfaces**, which physically protects it from the chemistry that would otherwise take it apart.

**So what actually destroys a genetic record?**

Not the calendar. **Water, heat and pH.** Hydrolysis needs water. Depurination accelerates with temperature and acidity. The moa study found decay roughly **400 times slower than in-vitro depurination kinetics had predicted**, which tells you the lab model was missing how much the surrounding environment matters.

This is why "how long does DNA last" is the wrong question and **"under what conditions" is the right one.** A sample in a warm, damp, acidic environment is on a fast clock. The same material, dry and chemically stable and bound to something inert, is on a clock so slow that the Pleistocene is inside the error bars.

**The 2012 team also projected where it stops.**

This is the part the headlines dropped. At an **ideal preservation temperature of minus 5 degrees Celsius**, the authors calculated that effectively **every bond would be destroyed after a maximum of about 6.8 million years**. More usefully, the material would **stop being readable long before that, at roughly 1.5 million years**, because what is left is too short to carry meaning. (Reported from the 2012 paper, contemporaneous coverage.)

That projection is why the same study was widely reported as closing the door on dinosaur DNA. **Sixty-six million years is an order of magnitude past the point where there is anything left to read**, under any temperature anyone has proposed.

**The honest limit, stated plainly:** none of this says a readable modern genome will still be readable in two million years. The Greenland material is fragmentary environmental DNA, not a usable individual genome, and the researchers are reconstructing an ecosystem rather than a person. **Anyone who tells you the 2-million-year number proves anything about personal DNA storage is selling you the headline and skipping the method.**

What it does prove is narrower and more useful: **the thing that kills a genetic record is its environment, and the environment is the one variable anybody can control.**

## #113 [X] The law that protects your genetic results stops exactly where your family's money starts.

**Trust and custody.**

Most people who have taken a DNA test believe there is a federal law stopping that result being used against them. **There is. It is narrower than almost anyone thinks.**

**The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 covers two things: health insurance and employment.** Title I handles health insurers. Title II handles employers. That is the whole federal shield.

**It does not cover life insurance. It does not cover disability insurance. It does not cover long-term care insurance.** (Institutional sources, current.)

Read that list again, because it is the list of policies people buy specifically to protect a family after something goes wrong. **The three products most tied to mortality are the three GINA leaves out.**

**What the gap actually permits**

In the states that have not legislated further, a life insurer is generally free to **ask about genetic test results, use them in underwriting, rate your premium upward, or decline to offer a policy at all.** The exclusion was not an oversight. It was the compromise that got the Act passed, and it has been argued over in the bioethics literature ever since: a 2018 analysis in the peer-reviewed literature is titled, flatly, "Beyond the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act: ethical and economic implications of the exclusion of disability, long-term care and life insurance."

**And the employment half has holes of its own**

Title II is the part people are most confident about, and it is also narrower than its reputation.

**It does not apply to employers with fewer than 15 employees.** That is the same threshold used across federal employment law, and it leaves a large share of American small businesses outside the statute entirely.

**The US military is permitted to use genetic information in employment decisions.** And because TRICARE eligibility follows from military employment, a genetic result can reach health coverage through the back door that GINA closed at the front.

**GINA also does not protect against discrimination based on a disease that has already manifested.** The protection attaches to the risk, not to the diagnosis. Once a condition is present, you are in the ordinary world of health and disability law. (Institutional and legal-practice sources, current.)

**The states that moved**

**Florida went first.** HB 1189 passed the Florida House on **29 January 2020** and was signed by Governor Ron DeSantis in **July 2020**, making Florida the first state to prohibit life and long-term care insurers from using genetic information. The statute removes the old carve-outs for life, disability and long-term care, **while leaving carve-outs in place for accident-only, hospital indemnity and fixed indemnity, dental and vision policies.** (Law-firm analysis and legislative summary, 2020.)

**California went wider rather than deeper.** CalGINA, passed in 2011, extended genetic non-discrimination beyond insurance into emergency medical services, housing, mortgage lending and education.

**The practical consequence, stated without drama**

If you are considering a genetic test and you also intend to buy term life or long-term care cover, **the order in which you do those two things can matter, and it matters differently depending on which state you live in.** That is not legal advice and it is not a reason to avoid testing. It is a reason to find out what your state actually says before you assume a federal law has you covered.

MySpawn sits outside this entirely by design: we store a physical DNA record and run no test, generate no result and produce no report that an underwriter could ask you about. **That is a structural fact about the product, not a loophole we are recommending.**

## #114 [FACEBOOK] The will is in the box. The box is sealed. This happens every single week.

**Practical legacy guide.**

There is a mistake in estate planning so common that lawyers have a weary shorthand for it, and almost nobody hears about it until they are the one standing at the counter.

**You put the will in the safe deposit box. Then you die. And in a lot of states, the box is sealed the moment the bank learns about it.**

**What sealing actually means**

In many states, the box of a sole renter is closed on death and stays closed until somebody has legal authority to open it. **The authority that opens it is Letters Testamentary, the court document naming the executor.** Banks treat that as definitive.

**And the document that gets you Letters Testamentary is the will.**

That is the loop. The will appoints the executor. The court appoints the executor from the will. The bank wants the court's paper before it opens the box. The will is inside the box.

**How states break the loop**

They do break it, but narrowly, and the narrowness is the point.

**Massachusetts** allows access after death **for the sole purpose of searching for a will or trust document.** **Virginia** allows limited access on the death of a sole lessee to a spouse, next of kin, a court clerk or another interested person, **solely to look for a will or testamentary instrument, under the supervision of a designated bank officer, with nothing removable except that document.**

The general pattern across states that provide for this: you bring a **certified copy of the death certificate** and an **affidavit** stating who you are and that the will or burial instructions are in the box. **A court may permit the will and burial instructions to be removed, then order the box resealed until an executor is appointed.** (Legal-practice sources, current. Rules differ by state and this is not legal advice.)

**Note what that timeline does to a funeral.** Burial instructions are frequently kept with the will. **The box can take days to open. A funeral does not wait days.** Families regularly make irreversible decisions about a burial while the instructions sit twenty minutes away behind a locked hinge.

**The fix takes one afternoon**

1. **The original will does not live in the safe deposit box.** Give it to the attorney who drafted it, or lodge it with the probate court if your state allows, or use a fireproof box at home that your executor knows the location and combination of.
2. **Burial and cremation wishes live separately from the will entirely**, because they are needed in 48 hours and a will is read in weeks.
3. **Write down what is actually in the box.** Most executors do not know, and an inventory beats a guess.
4. **Add a co-renter or check whether your state recognizes a deputy**, if you want the box openable without a court.
5. **Tell one person where everything is.** Being findable is worth more than being organized.

The same logic runs through everything we store: a record nobody can reach is indistinguishable from a record that was never made. That is why we treat **who can get it, and how fast** as part of the product rather than a footnote.

**Has anyone in your family ever had to open one of these? What did the bank ask you for?**

## #115 [REDDIT r/Entrepreneur] Our research sweep returned a bankruptcy as breaking news. It happened in 2009.

**Story-driven ops. Founder voice, disclosed. Zero pitch.**

I run a small company and a lot of our research is automated. Last night the evening sweep went looking for anything that had broken in the previous ten hours in our space. It came back with this, cleanly summarised: **a genetics company had filed for Chapter 11, and customer DNA records were set to pass to a venture group.**

That is a real story. It would have been the best thing we published this week.

**It is from November 2009.**

The filing listed assets just under $70 million against debts of more than $313 million. The subsidiary and drug programs went to a vehicle called Saga Investments. **The company was then acquired by Amgen in 2012.** None of which is a criticism of anyone involved. The events are seventeen years old and the company's later history is public.

**The failure was not the summary being wrong. Every fact in it was true.**

**The failure was that it carried no date, and I almost did not ask for one.**

**What actually caught it**

Not scepticism. I did not feel anything was off, because nothing was off. The summary was clean, specific, internally consistent and factually accurate. **It read exactly like a correct answer, because it was one, to a question about a different decade.**

What caught it was a mechanical habit: before anything goes into a draft, ask what year it happened. Not "is this true". **"When."**

**Why this is an ops problem and not an AI problem**

Every research pipeline I have built has the same shape: a retrieval step that is good at relevance and indifferent to recency, then a summarisation step that will faithfully compress whatever it was handed. **Neither step has any reason to volunteer a date, because neither step was asked for one.** The gap is in the spec, not the model.

Three things we changed, and they are boring on purpose:

1. **Date is a required field, not a nice-to-have.** If a claim has no date attached, it is not a claim yet. It is a lead.
2. **The question is asked before the claim is evaluated**, because once you have assessed something as true you stop interrogating it.
3. **We wrote down the near-miss with the actual example**, in the repo, the same night. A lesson with a story attached survives. A lesson phrased as a principle gets reworded into nothing within a month.

**What we still do not know:** whether this is a systematic property of how these summaries are generated, or whether we got an unlucky result on one query. **One incident is an anecdote.** We have started logging the date-presence of every research hit so that in a month we will have a rate instead of a feeling.

If you run automated research for your business, the cheapest possible test is to take your last five research outputs and ask, for each one, when the thing happened. **I would genuinely like to know if anyone finds a clean five.**

## #116 [FACEBOOK] The world's most famous backup took water through the front door, and the lesson everybody draws from it is the wrong one.

**Contrarian take. Punching at an idea, not at people.**

The Svalbard Global Seed Vault is the most photographed backup on Earth. A concrete wedge in a frozen Norwegian mountain, holding duplicate seed samples from gene banks around the world. **It is almost always described as a doomsday vault, built for the end of the world.**

Two things have actually happened there, and neither of them is the end of the world. **Both are more instructive than the apocalypse ever was.**

**Thing one: water got in.**

In **October 2016**, Svalbard had extreme weather, unusually high temperatures and heavy rain. **Meltwater entered the access tunnel**, travelled roughly **15 metres into a tunnel of about 100 metres**, and then froze solid.

The seeds were never at risk. The story is still the interesting one, because of what the operators concluded. **They did not conclude that the vault had held. They concluded that the design was wrong.** The Norwegian government allocated **100 million kroner** to an upgrade, reported in English-language coverage at roughly **9 to 13 million dollars**: a new concrete access tunnel, **heat-generating equipment removed from the tunnel entirely** and rehoused in a separate service building, waterproofing, and drainage channels cut into the mountainside.

Read that middle item again. The heat-producing machinery was **inside the cold tunnel**. The facility was warming its own entrance. **The most famous backup in the world had a design flaw that was obvious only once weather found it.**

**Thing two: somebody actually made a withdrawal, and it was mundane.**

The first withdrawal in the vault's history came in **2015**. Not a famine. Not a collapse. **ICARDA, an agricultural research centre headquartered near Aleppo, had deposited most of its collection at Svalbard before the conflict in Syria made its own site inaccessible.** So it drew on the backup, the way anyone draws on a backup: because the primary copy was out of reach. **The number of samples withdrawn is not established in the sources available to us, so we are not quoting one.**

**Here is the contrarian part.**

The doomsday framing is not just melodramatic. **It is actively misleading, because it teaches the wrong lesson about what a backup is for.**

Doomsday framing implies a backup is for a catastrophe so large that everything else has failed. That makes it feel optional, exotic, and somebody else's problem. **The record says the opposite.** The vault has been used exactly once, for an ordinary institutional reason, and it has been seriously upgraded exactly once, because of rain.

**Backups do not get used on the worst day. They get used on a Tuesday, because the original became inconvenient to reach.** And they fail, when they fail, at the join between the thing and the world: the tunnel, the entrance, the contact details, the payment method, the person who was supposed to know.

We think about our own storage the same way. **The interesting failure mode is never the mountain. It is the door.**

**What is the backup in your life that you have never actually tested opening?**
