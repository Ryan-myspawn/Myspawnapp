## #97 [X] Congress introduced a bill to stop this. In May 2025. It is still in committee.

**Word count: 540**

When 23andMe filed for Chapter 11 in March 2025, a lot of people said the same reassuring thing: Congress is on it.

Congress is, technically, on it. Here is exactly how much that is worth.

**The bill is real and it is specific.** The **Don't Sell My DNA Act, S. 1916** in the 119th Congress, introduced by **Senator Chuck Grassley (R-Iowa)** with **Senator John Cornyn (R-Texas)** and **Senator Amy Klobuchar (D-Minnesota)**. A House companion was introduced separately by **Representatives Zoe Lofgren and Ben Cline**.

It does three things, and they are the right three things.

**One.** It amends the Bankruptcy Code so that **genetic information counts as "personally identifiable information."** Right now it does not clearly, which is the whole gap.

**Two.** It requires **written notice and affirmative consent from each affected person** before genetic information can be used, sold or leased during a bankruptcy.

**Three.** It requires the trustee to **permanently delete** any genetic data not covered by a sale.

**Now the date.** S. 1916 was **introduced on 22 May 2025**. It was read twice and **referred to the Committee on the Judiciary** the same day.

**That is where it still is. Sixteen months later. No floor action. Four cosponsors.**

### Why the gap exists in the first place

The Bankruptcy Code already restricts selling personally identifiable information: **11 U.S.C. §363(b)(1)** limits a sale where the company's own privacy policy said the data would not be transferred to non-affiliated parties. **11 U.S.C. §332** lets the court appoint a **consumer privacy ombudsman** to weigh in.

Both of those protections are real. Both hinge on definitions written before consumer genomics existed.

In the 23andMe case the process ran: Regeneron was declared the successful bidder at **$256 million**, bidding reopened, and **TTAM Research Institute**, a nonprofit founded by 23andMe's co-founder, acquired the company at **$305 million**, completing on **14 July 2025**. Several state attorneys general objected. Some remained opposed at the close.

### The part nobody says out loud

Even if S. 1916 passed tomorrow, **it only governs bankruptcy.**

A company that is doing fine and simply sells itself is not in bankruptcy court. There is no trustee, no ombudsman, no judge, no objection window. The transfer happens under an assignment clause that was in the terms of service you accepted, and the first you hear of it is a cheerful email about an exciting new chapter.

**Bankruptcy is the loud failure mode. Acquisition is the quiet one, and it is far more common.**

### What to actually do with this

Two things, neither of which requires a bill.

**Read the assignment clause before you hand over a sample.** It is usually one sentence, usually near the end, and it usually says the data goes with the company in a merger or sale. That sentence is the whole promise.

**Stop treating "a bill was introduced" as news that a problem is solved.** Introduced is not reported. Reported is not passed. Passed is not signed. Sixteen months in committee is the normal outcome, not a scandal, and it is exactly why the sentence "Congress is on it" should never end a conversation about your own data.

**EPISTEMIC STATUS:** bill text, sponsors and referral date are **official congressional records**. The 23andMe bid figures are **reported and widely corroborated**. Nothing here is a prediction about whether the bill will pass, because nobody knows.

## #98 [REDDIT r/SideProject] I ran 12 stock photo candidates this week for two ads. Zero were usable. The pattern is not bad luck.

**Word count: 590**

Founder here, disclosing that up front. I run a small DNA-storage startup and I make our own ad creative, which means I spend an unreasonable amount of time in stock photo search results.

This week I failed twice in a row, completely, and the failures turned out to be the same failure. Posting it because if you make your own creative, this will save you a day.

**The two briefs were ordinary.** One wanted a hairbrush. One wanted a cast iron pan. Both are objects sitting in most homes.

**Hairbrush family: eight candidates, eight kills.** Three were brand product shoots, identifiable not by a logo in frame but by the photographer's own description reading like marketing copy with product hashtags. One was a beauty company's own shoot of an anti-aging device. One had an identifiable person and a named salon with a URL in the description. **One was approved into the platform's own 3D-renders topic. It was not a photograph at all.**

**Cast iron family: six candidates, six kills.** Two had food in frame, which the brief forbade. **Two said "3D render made in Blender" in their own descriptions.** One was shot by a cookware brand with a portfolio URL in its bio.

**Hair strand macro, the fallback: four candidates, four kills.** Two of the four were **approved into the 3D-renders topic.** One was tinsel. One was a painting.

### The diagnosis, which is the useful part

Two supply categories are effectively closed, and for structural reasons rather than random ones.

**Consumer objects are photographed overwhelmingly by the people who sell them.** If a thing has a retail price, the dense, well-lit, high-resolution supply of images of it was made to sell it. That brand rides along even with no logo visible, because the photographer's account, description and hashtags carry it.

**Macro abstracts are generated overwhelmingly by the people who render them.** Anything tiny, perfect and evenly lit is a strong candidate for CGI, and renders are indexed alongside photographs in the same search results.

### What actually worked

Every image that shipped this week was **weathered infrastructure nobody sells**: a rusted chain on a harbor embankment, a ladder leaning on a concrete wall, vinyl grooves in raking light, an architectural concrete corner.

**Nobody's marketing department owns a rusted chain.** That is the entire trick.

### Three habits that came out of it

**Read the full description, not the thumbnail.** Provenance and medium both live in the text and neither is visible in the grid.

**Search your own asset log before you download anything.** This cost me seconds and killed three near-duplicates I would otherwise have shipped, including one that collided with a previous ad on its *argument* rather than its image.

**When a brief names a product, change the brief, not the query.** Three families and twelve candidates is enough evidence. I killed that concept rather than trying a fourth.

### What I don't know yet

I have tested this on one library. **I do not know whether the same two categories are closed on other stock platforms**, and I would genuinely like to hear from anyone who has run the same wall somewhere else.

I also don't know how much of the render problem is platform policy versus platform indexing. The renders I hit were openly labeled by their creators. They were just labeled in a place the search grid does not show you.

## #99 [FACEBOOK] We printed a competitor's price wrong for five weeks. The mistake was not the number. It was the explanation we invented for it.

**Word count: 595**

I want to tell you about a small error, because the shape of it is more interesting than the size of it.

We publish a pricing guide comparing ways to preserve biological material. One row covers a competitor who sells a sealed capsule you keep at home: pay once, no annual fee, the object is yours.

For five weeks that row carried two numbers from our own notes. One note from August said the capsule cost 498. A later note said 389.

**Neither note recorded a currency.**

Here is the part I keep thinking about. When we wrote the article, we noticed the two numbers disagreed. We did not flag it. **We explained it.** We decided that 498 must be the at-need price sold through funeral homes, and 389 must be the pre-need price you pay in advance. It was plausible. It fit how that industry works. We printed it as fact, in five places, including the structured data that search engines quote directly.

Yesterday we checked again. The US site shows **580 dollars**. The Canadian site shows **389 Canadian dollars**. There is a third option at 920 that nobody explains anywhere. And the one figure that matched our notes exactly, 250 dollars for each additional capsule, was the one nobody had ever disputed.

**So the gap between 498 and 389 was probably never pre-need versus at-need. It was probably two countries.**

A missing currency does not announce itself. It sits there looking like a number. And when two of them disagree, the mind does not reach for "we forgot to write down the unit." It reaches for a story.

**That is the actual failure. Not the wrong price. The confident explanation of the wrong price.**

Here is what we did about it, and what we deliberately did not do.

**We did not swap 389 for 580.** Every one of that company's web pages is unreachable from our network, so today's figures are second-hand too, and replacing one unverified number with another unverified number is not a correction. It is the same mistake wearing a fresh coat.

**We corrected it to a range, with the currency caveat attached, and a dated note saying our previous explanation may have been wrong.** We changed it in the structured data as well, because that is the copy that actually gets quoted.

Now the uncomfortable part, and it is the reason this is worth writing down.

This is the **second** pricing correction we have made in a week. The first one, five days ago, was also about a competitor. Both corrections moved a competitor's price **upward**. Both, in other words, made us look better than we had been claiming.

Two in a row that flatter you is not luck. It is a pattern, and the honest reading of it is unflattering: **we recorded competitor prices quickly, without units, in a direction nobody on our side had any motive to challenge.** A number that makes your rival look good gets checked by nobody. Not by them, because they are winning. Not by you, because checking it costs you the comparison.

So we are going back through the rest of that file on the assumption that the remaining unchecked prices lean the same way.

None of this required expertise. It required writing down a dollar sign's nationality.

**What is the number in your own work that everybody repeats and nobody has ever checked the units on?**

## #100 [X] A company that sells grief services published a survey. It is better documented than most academic work I read.

**Word count: 618**

I spend a lot of time taking other people's statistics apart. So when one holds up, it is worth saying so, especially when the source has an obvious commercial interest.

**Empathy**, a company selling bereavement support, published research called **"Grief in the Age of AI"**, announced **30 June 2026**.

Here is what it discloses, in its own materials, before anyone asks.

**Who ran it:** Censuswide, a named third-party research firm.
**Sample size:** 6,002 adults.
**Geography:** United States, Canada and the United Kingdom.
**Screening criterion:** everyone surveyed had experienced grief and loss.
**Fieldwork window:** 7 to 18 May 2026. Eleven days, both dates given.

**That is five things.** Most statistics that cross your feed disclose none of them. The number arrives alone, with no sample, no provider, no window and no screen, and it is repeated for years by people who could not tell you what year it was collected.

### The findings, stated as what the report says

Only **31 percent** of respondents said they received the help they needed in their grief.

Nearly **one in four** AI users reported turning to AI during grief. Of those users, **97 percent** reported a positive impact.

**Gen Z** reported the highest rate of using AI during grief at **39 percent**, while **Millennials at 50 percent** and **Gen X at 45 percent** were the most open to AI's role overall.

**56 percent** said they were comfortable with AI handling practical tasks such as paperwork.

**EPISTEMIC STATUS: company-commissioned survey, third-party fieldwork, method disclosed. Not peer-reviewed.**

### The two cautions that are mine to add, not theirs to hide

**The company sells bereavement services.** A finding that seven in ten people are underserved by existing support is a finding that expands its market. That is a disclosure, not an accusation, and the disclosure is necessary precisely because the method is otherwise good.

**The 97 percent is a percentage of a subgroup of a screened sample.** It is not 97 percent of everyone, or of everyone grieving. It is 97 percent of the roughly one in four AI users who turned to AI while grieving, within a sample that had already been filtered to people who had experienced loss. Quote it with that chain attached or do not quote it.

### The actual takeaway

Ask five questions of any statistic before you repeat it. **Who ran the fieldwork. How many people. Where. Who was screened in. What dates.**

A number that can answer all five is usable with its caveats. A number that cannot answer any of them is a rumor with a decimal point.

**And notice which kind you encounter more often.** The best-documented figure I have read this month came from a company with something to sell, and the worst-documented ones came from sources with no commercial interest at all. **Interest is not the same as sloppiness, and we tend to assume it is.**

### One more limit, because a disclosed screen is also a constraint

The screening criterion is the most useful thing in that report and the easiest to lose.

**Everyone surveyed had already experienced grief and loss.** That is the right sample for the question being asked, and it means the findings say nothing whatever about the general population. The 31 percent who felt adequately supported are 31 percent of the bereaved, not 31 percent of adults.

Stated, it is a strength. Dropped, it silently converts a careful finding about a specific group into a sweeping claim about everybody, and nothing in the number itself resists that conversion.

**That is how most good statistics go bad. Not by being wrong. By being repeated one clause shorter each time.**

## #101 [X] "Functional eggs" made the headlines. Look at what the word functional was doing.

**Word count: 591**

A year ago this week, a real scientific milestone was reported with a word that was carrying an enormous amount of weight.

**On 30 September 2025**, researchers at **Oregon Health and Science University** published in ***Nature Communications*** the first artificial reduction of a human body cell's chromosome count. They called the technique **mitomeiosis**. **Label: peer-reviewed.** The coverage said scientists had created **functional eggs from human skin cells**.

That is not false. It is also not what most readers took from it.

**Here are the numbers from the study itself.**

The team produced **82 eggs**.

After fertilization, roughly **9 percent** were still developing at **day six**.

**Every single resulting embryo carried chromosomal abnormalities.**

The senior researcher's own public characterization was that it **"partially works, and partially doesn't."**

### What "functional" actually meant

In that sentence, "functional" meant: **this cell did the specific thing we were testing, which was accept a sperm and begin dividing.**

It did not mean viable. It did not mean usable in a clinic. It did not mean healthy. Every embryo was chromosomally abnormal, which in ordinary reproductive terms is the definition of not working.

**The team's own estimate is roughly ten more years of research before this could even be tested in people**, and then only with permission for a clinical trial.

### The bit that gets left out of the headline entirely

The technique starts with **a donated human egg**. The skin-cell nucleus is placed into an egg that has had its own nucleus removed.

So the phrase "eggs from skin cells" is describing a process that **requires an egg to begin with.** That is a completely different proposition from the field's actual goal, which is building a gamete from a body cell and nothing else.

**Where that goal stands: in mice, done.** Full reconstitution of both egg and sperm development from stem cells, producing live and fertile offspring. **In humans, no laboratory has ever produced a mature, usable egg or sperm cell from a body cell.** The furthest anyone has gone is early precursor cells.

### Why humans lag mice, and it is not funding

Two reasons, and the second one is rarely mentioned.

**Mouse germ cell development is short and you can run the experiment all the way to a live birth.** That is the whole loop, closed, repeatedly.

**In humans the loop cannot be closed, by rule.** Human primordial germ cells are specified at roughly **two weeks** of embryonic development, and most jurisdictions cap human embryo culture at **14 days**. The developmental window you most need to observe is the one you are least permitted to reach.

So the human bottleneck is partly biology and partly a line drawn in research ethics that most people writing headlines about this field have never heard of.

### Why I am writing this a year late on purpose

Because a year is exactly how long it takes for the caveats to fall off.

The study is still true. The numbers are still 82, 9 percent and all-abnormal. **But the sentence that survives into 2026 is "scientists made eggs from skin cells", and the sentence that died is "nine percent of them were still dividing on day six."**

When a technical word appears in a headline about a field you do not work in, **go and find what that word was measuring.** Functional, effective, successful and promising are all doing specific, narrow, defined jobs inside the paper, and general, enormous jobs outside it.

**EPISTEMIC STATUS: the study is peer-reviewed; the numbers and the ten-year estimate are from the institution's own announcement and the researcher's reported remarks. No claim here about whether the technique will ever work.**
