## #172 [X] Science explainer, ZERO PRODUCT MENTION

**Freshness check:** `LINE-1` returns one longpost hit and it is a kill note, not an article. No longpost has ever been built on this study. The biology was covered by a 10 October video explainer under a written scope limit that forbade mentioning the study at all.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend-hook used:** **None claimed. This is a dated-document piece and its whole subject is that the date is the story.**

**A study about IVF and DNA is circulating as new. Check the date before you repeat it.**

The finding: IVF-conceived offspring showed higher global LINE-1 content than naturally conceived controls, **P=0.04**. The team reported **11 genomic loci** with differing L1 insertion frequencies and **14 loci** with differing deletion frequencies, clustering near genes associated with metabolic, cardiovascular, neuropsychiatric and neoplastic disease. Conventional IVF and ICSI showed no clear difference from each other.

**Before that paragraph travels any further, here is what its own authors say about it.** The evidence is described as **preliminary**. The sample is called **relatively modest**, particularly for subgroup analyses. The sibling confirmation rests on **three families**. And the disease link is **inferred from gene proximity, not measured in any person**. The paper does not establish that IVF caused anything.

**Those caveats are stated first here on purpose.** A claim like this one gets quoted without them in about two clicks, and the quoting is where the damage happens.

**Now the date, which is the actual subject.** The paper appears in the *American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology*, 2026. **The preprint it grew from was posted to medRxiv on 21 October 2025.** Coverage of it was circulating again in October 2026. So depending on which artefact somebody saw, this is either a 2026 peer-reviewed paper, a 2025 preprint, or a 2026 news cycle about a 2025 document.

**All three descriptions are true and they imply different things.** A preprint is not peer-reviewed. A paper is. A news cycle is neither. **If you repeat the finding, say which one you read.**

**LINE-1, placed properly.** Long Interspersed Nuclear Element 1 is the most studied human mobile element and one of the few still capable of moving. Insertion inside a functional gene is an established disease mechanism and a rare one. This is ancient biology being measured now because sequencing got cheap, not biology that started happening recently.

**One more thing that stops this being a clean story.** An earlier study in *Human Reproduction* looked at a related question and found **lower** methylation of H19/IGF2, KCNQ1OT1, LINE-1Hs and ERVFRD-1 in IVF and ICSI placentas, with no difference in cord blood. **It measured methylation rather than insertion counts, so it neither confirms nor refutes the new paper.** Two studies, two measurements, one topic, no resolution.

**What nobody should take from this:** that IVF-conceived people are damaged, or that naturally conceived DNA is cleaner. **Neither claim is in the paper and both are easy to build from a headline.**

## #173 [FACEBOOK] Science explainer, warm, ends with a question

**Freshness check:** `in vitro maturation` returns **ZERO** across every longpost ever written. The fleet has two IVG articles and had never explained the clinical category next door.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55, written to be read by anyone weighing fertility options
**Trend-hook used:** **The Ferring agreement to acquire Gameto for up to $1.3825 billion, announced 27 October 2026.**

A Swiss pharmaceutical company agreed this week to buy a New York fertility biotech for **up to $1.3825 billion**. Most of the coverage described the technology as lab-grown eggs. It is not that, and the difference is worth four minutes.

**The product matures a patient's own immature eggs outside her body**, using ovarian support cells engineered from stem cells. The stem cells are in the support system. The egg was always hers. That is a category called **in vitro maturation**, and it has been in clinical use for years under other people's brand names.

**Here is the detail that settles it, and it comes from the trial design rather than from anybody's marketing.** The pivotal US study, registered as **NCT06858111**, compares the new product against **MediCult IVM, a standard maturation medium**. The trial is not asking whether maturing eggs outside the body works. It is asking whether this way beats the existing way.

**So how well does the existing way work?** The American Society for Reproductive Medicine's committee opinion says IVM is **no longer considered experimental**. It also says it is not for everyone, pointing to patients with a high antral follicle count, and it asks clinicians to have specific training and to counsel patients about expected results.

**The numbers explain why that counselling is required.** In a retrospective study of **919 women** with high antral follicle counts, cumulative live birth after one cycle was **239 of 608, or 39.3 per cent, for IVM, against 155 of 311, or 49.8 per cent, for conventional IVF**. An older comparison reported clinical pregnancy and live birth per retrieval of **26.2 and 15.9 per cent for IVM against 38.3 and 26.2 per cent for IVF**, with the source noting those differences were not statistically significant.

**And the number that explains the attrition: the chance of a single immature egg resulting in a live birth was 1.1 per cent.** Cycles retrieve many eggs, so that figure is not a scandal. It is the reason nobody should hear "matured in a lab" and picture a sure thing.

**Two limits that matter if preservation rather than treatment is your interest.** Vitrified-thawed IVM eggs did worse than fresh ones in a study the ASRM cites, with **59.8 per cent survival** and pregnancy rates of **10.7 against 36.1 per cent**. And the ASRM states that **vitrification of immature oocytes is not recommended**.

**Status, plainly: the product in the news has no FDA marketing approval, its US trial is still recruiting, and the interim readout guided to for late 2026 has not been published.** Reported clearances exist in Australia and parts of Latin America.

We store DNA samples for $99 a year, billed annually. That is a different product from anything on this page and it does not preserve fertility. We are saying so because the week's headlines make it easy to blur.

**Which did you think the deal was about before you read this: lab-grown eggs, or a better dish?**

## #175 [X] Trust and custody, self-audit

**Freshness check:** `21 CFR 807.39` returns **ZERO** fleet-wide. **Adjacency declared rather than hidden: longpost #142, on 15 October, unpacked what ISO 20387 actually requires. That piece explained a standard. This one audits a phrase we use, against the rule that governs its use, and names the regulation that could make us wrong.**
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend-hook used:** **None. A company checking its own boilerplate does not need a hook.**

**"FDA-registered" is in our own copy. It is a weaker phrase than most readers assume, and there is a regulation about saying it the wrong way.**

**Registration is administrative.** It is a notice of a facility's location, its activities and its products. **It is not a review of anything.** The FDA's own registration and listing database carries the point: an entry **does not denote approval, clearance or authorisation** of that facility or its devices.

**Now the part with teeth.** **21 CFR 807.39** provides that references to establishment registration and to a registration number which create an impression of official **approval, clearance, authorisation, certification or endorsement** are misleading and constitute **misbranding**.

**The FDA has also said it does not issue registration certificates**, in a notice dated **March 2021**, and that firms displaying such certificates next to product marketing to imply review are misbranding their products.

**And disclaimers do not rescue it.** In a warning letter to a KN95 seller, the agency said small-print disclaimers **could be easily overlooked and do not limit or otherwise mitigate the misleading impression.** That is the sentence worth remembering, because the usual industry fix is a footnote.

**So here is our own audit, out loud.** We describe our storage partner as an **FDA-registered biorepository with CAP accreditation and ISO 9001 and ISO 20387 certification**, at ambient temperature. **Registered is the correct word for the first item and it is the weakest of the four.** It means the facility is on a list. It does not mean the FDA assessed how your sample is handled. **Anyone reading our copy as "the FDA checked this" is reading something we did not write and should not imply.**

**What would make the other three worth more than the first: the certificates themselves, with numbers and expiry dates, published.** We have not published them. **That has now been raised internally six times, and saying it in public is the version that costs something.**

**One more mechanical fact worth carrying.** Registration is **annual**, and drug and biologic establishment registration sits under **21 CFR Part 207** rather than the device rule quoted above. **So a registration is not only weak evidence, it is also a statement about a moment in time that has to be renewed.** A company that was registered is not necessarily a company that is registered.

**A test you can run on anybody, including us.** Ask which of the four words applies: registered, listed, cleared, approved. **They are four different things and only two involve the agency looking at the product.** If the answer changes when you ask twice, you have learned something.

**Label on this piece: the regulation citation and the warning letter are primary-source anchors; the surrounding explanations come from compliance-vendor pages, and the exact CFR wording should be read at its source before anyone quotes it.** We did not open the CFR text directly.
