### Six Hundred and One Days From True to Known

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. No prior longpost has been built on the publication-lag of a single paper, and this paper has never appeared in any MySpawn asset.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** The Mayo Clinic senescence paper (Martini et al., *Nature*) hit the consumer science press on **September 11, 2026**, four days ago, after being online at the journal since July 29, 2026 and sitting as a preprint since December 5, 2024.

---

On December 5, 2024, a team posted a preprint to Research Square.

On July 29, 2026, the peer-reviewed version went online at *Nature*.

On September 11, 2026, it finally reached your feed.

**That is 601 days from preprint to journal, just under twenty months, and another 44 days from journal to headline.** Your biology did not change once in that window. Only the paperwork did.

**What the paper actually found**

The study is "Mitochondrial metabolism and epigenetic crosstalk drive SASP," Martini et al., *Nature*, 2026 (DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10791-2), **peer-reviewed**, with authors from Mayo Clinic, the MRC London Institute of Medical Sciences and Albert Einstein College of Medicine.

Start with the thing it is about. As you age, some of your cells stop dividing but refuse to die. Biologists call them senescent cells; the press calls them zombie cells. They stay metabolically busy and they leak a chemical cocktail called the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, or SASP. The SASP is a large part of why chronic inflammation rises with age.

The known part was that zombie cells inflame you. The new part is the wiring diagram.

**The mechanism, in one paragraph.** In senescent cells the mitochondrial pyruvate-citrate-acetyl-CoA axis is upregulated. That raises the supply of acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA fuels histone acetylation at the SASP gene loci, which is the molecular equivalent of unlocking those genes so they can be read. Mitochondrial DNA-driven signalling switches on the inflammatory transcription factors at the same time. Two locks, one key each.

The team then pulled the key. Blocking **SLC25A1**, the mitochondrial citrate carrier, reduced chromatin accessibility at SASP loci, dampened systemic inflammation, and improved healthspan.

**In aged mice.** Say that part out loud, because most coverage buries it. There is no human trial of an SLC25A1 inhibitor for aging. There is a target, a mechanism and a mouse.

**Why the delay is the actual story**

Longevity science has a strange relationship with time. Several longevity candidates are in human trials right now, and not one of them has a published result. No reprogramming candidate has human data at all. Meanwhile the results that do exist take the better part of two years to clear review.

So a man deciding what to do with his forties is always reading a map drawn from a photograph taken 600 days ago.

**The practical consequence: the interpretation moves, the material does not.**

A biological-age readout you bought in 2024 was scored against 2024's understanding of what inflammation means. This paper is part of why that scoring will be redone. The number will change. The blood it came from is long gone.

That asymmetry is the entire argument for keeping the sample rather than the summary. Analyses get revised on a two-year cycle. A stored DNA record does not get revised at all; it just waits for a better reader.

That is the whole of what we sell, and I will not dress it up further: MySpawn stores a DNA record and sealed video messages at $99/year, billed annually, at an ISO/CAP-accredited, FDA-registered biorepository in New Jersey run by GenVault. Those are GenVault's credentials rather than ours, and FDA registration means the facility is registered with the agency, not that the FDA has approved or endorsed anything. We do not score you, and we do not tell you your biological age.

**What I would do with this paper if I were 42**

Nothing, medically. There is no intervention to take.

What I would take from it is calibration. The distance between "true in a lab" and "known in a headline" is measured in years, and the distance between "known in a headline" and "available to you" is measured in more. Plan on the clock, not the press release.

## ARTICLE #68, [FACEBOOK]
### On October 1, Your Saliva Becomes Property in One American State

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. No MySpawn asset has covered state genetic-privacy statutes; #59 covered beneficiary designations and #27 covered RUFADAA, both different bodies of law with different mechanics.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** Connecticut's genetic-privacy provisions take effect **October 1, 2026**, sixteen days from today.

---

Sixteen days from now, a law comes into force in Connecticut that does something no federal law does.

It says that the spit you mailed to a DNA testing company, and the results they generated from it, are **your property**.

Not your data. Your property.

**What the Connecticut law actually gives you**

Connecticut's SB 4 creates a property right for consumers in the biological sample given to a direct-to-consumer genetic testing company and in the results of testing done on that sample. Its provisions take effect **October 1, 2026**.

Four concrete rights come with it:

- The right to **access** your genetic data.
- The right to **delete** it.
- The right to require the **destruction of your biological sample**, the physical tube, not just the file.
- The right to **revoke consent** for the use and storage of that data.

Companies also have to disclose how they handle genetic data and obtain express consent for collection, use, disclosure and retention, including secondary uses, third-party transfers, and holding the sample after the test is done.

Enforcement is where it gets narrower. Violations are treated as unfair or deceptive trade practices, enforceable **solely by the Connecticut Attorney General**. There is no private lawsuit in it. You get a right; the state gets the hammer.

**Connecticut is not alone, and the map is uneven**

South Dakota enacted SB 49 regulating direct-to-consumer genetic testing companies, effective **July 1, 2026**.

By one 2026 legal-industry count, thirteen states had enacted regulation of direct-to-consumer genetic testing: Alabama, Arizona, California, Florida, Kentucky, Maryland, Montana, Nebraska, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Virginia and Wyoming. *(Reported count from a law-firm survey, not an official registry; treat it as a good estimate rather than a census.)*

Which means the answer to "do I have the right to make them destroy my sample" is currently: depends what state you live in.

**Congress has had two bills for sixteen months**

Two federal bills exist. Neither has become law.

The **Genomic Data Protection Act** (S.863), from Senators Bill Cassidy and Gary Peters, would require DTC genomic testing companies to let a consumer access their genomic data, delete it, and have their biological sample destroyed, with the FTC enforcing.

The **Don't Sell My DNA Act** (S.1916), from Senators John Cornyn, Amy Klobuchar and Judiciary Chairman Chuck Grassley, would amend the bankruptcy code so genetic information cannot be sold, leased or used in a bankruptcy unless every affected person consents in writing after the case has begun. It was introduced and referred to the Judiciary Committee on **May 22, 2025**. A House companion followed on **July 17, 2025**. Both remain in committee.

That is roughly sixteen months in the first stage of the legislative process.

**Why any of this exists**

23andMe filed for Chapter 11 on **March 23, 2025**. Its business was sold through a court-approved process to the TTAM Research Institute, a nonprofit led by co-founder Anne Wojcicki, for **$305 million**, approved in July 2025. Along the way, state attorneys general in California, Nevada, Utah and Massachusetts issued consumer advisories telling their residents to go delete their data.

An executive later said **nearly two million** customers had requested deletion. *(Reported statement via trade press, not a filed figure; I am giving you the number and its weak sourcing in the same breath.)*

That is what produced this legislative wave: not a scandal about what a company did with DNA, but a bankruptcy proving that custody can change hands while you are not looking.

**Three questions, whoever holds your DNA**

Worth asking of any company, including mine:

1. **Can I make you destroy the physical sample, not just delete the file?**
2. **What happens to it in a bankruptcy?**
3. **Who else already has a copy?**

Here are our answers, for the record. MySpawn stores a DNA record and sealed video messages for **$99/year, billed annually**, at a New Jersey biorepository operated by GenVault, which is ISO and CAP accredited and FDA-registered. Those are GenVault's credentials rather than ours, and FDA registration means the facility is registered with the agency, not that the FDA has approved or endorsed anything. We do not sell or license genetic data, and we are storage only: we do not sequence you, score you, or offer anything reproductive.

And on question two, the honest answer for every company our size is the same one: a written policy is a promise, and a bankruptcy court is a venue where promises get tested. The Don't Sell My DNA Act exists precisely because that test has already happened once.

Which of the three questions would you actually want answered before you mailed anyone a tube?

### The People Attacking AI Afterlives Are Right About Three Things

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. #57 (Sept 11) was a company obituary about HereAfter's wind-down and #65 (Sept 13) was about California's rules for AI companions protecting only the living. Neither engaged the ethics critique on its own terms, and neither conceded anything.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** Honest answer, this is not a dated news event. The academic critique of "deadbots" and "digital necromancy" has been consolidating through 2026 across several journals rather than breaking on a single day, and our own Sept 11 radar flagged it as a slow current. I am saying so rather than dressing it up as a headline.

---

I run a company that stores DNA records and sealed video messages. So when critics come for the business of digital afterlives, I am not a neutral party. I am close enough to be one of the targets.

They are right about three things, and the people in this industry who wave that away are selling something.

**One: the dead cannot consent, and there is no fixing that.**

This is the hard one. A person who has died is, in the language of the ethics literature, permanently and structurally unable to consent, object, or withdraw. Every corrective mechanism we normally rely on in data ethics, revoke permission, sue, complain, walk away, requires a living person to operate it. None of them exist here.

You can get consent in advance. You cannot get consent to the specific thing the model eventually says, because nobody knows what that will be.

**Two: the product being sold is a person's identity.**

Not their data. Their voice, their manner of expression, their relational patterns, the things that made them recognisable to the people who loved them. A deadbot turns those into the raw material of a commercial service. Research reviewing this field in 2026 names the services doing it, including Character.AI, Replika and Myloves.ai, and notes Project December charging **$10** for what it calls fictionalised séances.

Ten dollars. That is the price at which a simulated conversation with your father became a consumer product.

**Three: the customer is a grieving person.**

Grief is an acutely vulnerable psychological state, and the critique's sharpest point is that it is also an unusually profitable one. The asymmetry between a platform and someone three weeks into a loss is not a normal customer relationship.

There is a fourth item the critics raise that is purely technical, and it may be the most damning. Generative models hallucinate. A deadbot can therefore produce statements entirely out of character, things the person never said and would never have said, delivered in their voice to someone who is in no condition to discount it.

**What I will not concede**

Here is where I get off the train, and it is a narrower place than the critics usually allow.

**A recording repeats what someone actually said. A model eventually says something they never said.**

That distinction is not a technicality. It is the entire line. One is an archive. The other is an author writing new material under a dead person's name.

A twenty-year-old voicemail from your mother is not digital necromancy. A letter, a home video, a sealed message recorded on purpose for a date you chose, none of those generate anything. They transmit. The person who decided what would be said is the person who said it.

**Where we stop, in plain terms**

MySpawn stores records: a DNA sample and sealed video messages you record yourself. We do not generate speech in anyone's voice. We do not build a model of a real person. Our ECHO widget speaks as an explicitly fictional descendant writing from 2126, a character, announced as a character, never a simulation of anyone who lived.

If we ever cross that, the criticism above should be aimed at us, and I would rather have written this paragraph now than been asked about it later.

**One thing the critique gets wrong**

It sometimes slides into contempt for the users. That part I reject. Someone talking to a simulation of their wife at 2am is not foolish; they are bereaved, and the design decisions that put that product in front of them were made by people who were not.

Aim at the incentives. Aim at the designers. Not at the widower.

Where would you draw the line?

### The Splenda Study Everyone Shared This Week Was Published in 2023

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. We have run one prior debunk (#3, Aug 19, on two fake artificial-womb videos) and two effect-size takedowns (#52, #62). This is neither: the study here is real, competently done, and simply three years old. The hook is the date, not the science.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** A wave of "artificial sweetener damages your DNA" coverage ran on **September 12, 2026**, three days ago.

---

A story went around this week saying that the sweetener in Splenda produces a chemical in your gut that breaks your DNA.

The study behind it is real. It is also from 2023.

**What the study did**

The paper is Schiffman et al., *Journal of Toxicology and Environmental Health, Part B*, **2023** (DOI 10.1080/10937404.2023.2213903), out of North Carolina State University and the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. It is **peer-reviewed**.

Its subject is not sucralose exactly. It is **sucralose-6-acetate**, a related compound that appears both as a manufacturing impurity and as something the body produces after sucralose is consumed. The researchers reported finding up to **0.67%** sucralose-6-acetate in commercial sucralose samples they tested.

Two standard tests were run. A MultiFlow assay and a micronucleus test both indicated the compound is **genotoxic**, and it was described as clastogenic, meaning it causes breaks in DNA strands.

Then they went after the gut. Using lab-grown human intestinal tissue, both sucralose and sucralose-6-acetate lowered transepithelial electrical resistance, which is a measurement of how tight the gut barrier is. A lower number means a leakier barrier letting larger molecules through. Exposure also raised expression of genes tied to inflammation and oxidative stress.

That is a genuinely unsettling set of results. I am not going to pretend otherwise.

**What the study did not do**

It was not done in people.

Every result above came from human cells and lab-grown tissue in a dish. There is no arm of this study where humans consumed sucralose and were measured. The finding is that a compound damages DNA in that setting at those doses, not that your afternoon Diet Coke is doing it to you.

The manufacturer also contests it. Splenda maintains a public response page specifically about the sucralose-6-acetate research, disputing the levels reported in its product. That is an interested party defending its product, and it should be read as one, but a reader who has only seen the scary headline has not seen it at all.

**Why the date matters more than the finding**

Nothing about this study changed in the last three years. No new trial ran. No regulator moved.

What happened is that a science news outlet ran a fresh write-up on September 12, 2026, and the internet treated a fresh write-up as a fresh result. That is the most common way misinformation gets made in health coverage, and almost nobody involved is lying. The article is accurate. The study is accurate. The impression left on the reader, that something new was discovered this week, is false.

**Three questions, ten seconds each**

I use these on every health headline now, and they cost nothing:

1. **Was it in people?** Cells in a dish, mice, and humans are three different claims.
2. **When was the underlying study published?** Not the article, the study. They are often years apart.
3. **Who disagrees, and what do they say?** If a manufacturer or a scientific body has published a rebuttal, read it before you decide.

That is not cynicism. It is the difference between being informed and being moved.

**The honest summary**

A 2023 peer-reviewed study found that a compound associated with sucralose damaged DNA and weakened the gut barrier in laboratory conditions, the manufacturer disputes elements of it, and no human feeding study has settled it either way. If you want to cut artificial sweeteners, that is a reasonable choice and this paper is a reasonable input.

But nothing was discovered this week.

What is the last health headline you actually checked the date on?

## ARTICLE #71, [REDDIT: r/transhumanism]
### Some animals delete a fifth of their own genome on purpose, and only one cell lineage keeps the complete copy

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. Programmed DNA elimination has never appeared in any MySpawn asset. The nearest prior item is #51 (Sept 7, egg dormancy as low-power mode), which was about metabolism, not about genome content.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** *Nature Reviews Genetics* published a review titled "The mechanistic and evolutionary diversity of programmed DNA elimination" on **September 10, 2026**, five days ago.

---

Disclosure first: I am the founder of a small company that stores DNA records and sealed video messages. This post is not about that, and I have put the one relevant paragraph at the bottom where you can skip it. I am here because this is the best thing I read this month and this sub is where I would want to argue about it.

**The phenomenon**

In a number of animals, the embryo builds two kinds of cells: the ones that will become the body, and the ones that will become eggs or sperm. In some lineages, at a specific moment in early development, the body-destined cells **physically throw away part of the genome**. Chunks of chromosome. Sometimes whole chromosomes. Sometimes most of the genome.

This is not damage. It is scheduled, reproducible, and happens in the same cells at the same stage every generation. Biologists call it programmed DNA elimination, and a new review in *Nature Reviews Genetics* on September 10, 2026 covers the range of it: chromosome breakage, whole-chromosome elimination and whole-genome elimination as separate mechanisms that evolved more than once ([journal page](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41576-026-01004-7); the review is also described by the Duharcourt lab at Institut Jacques Monod [here](https://www.ijm.fr/duharcourt-lab-the-mechanistic-and-evolutionary-diversity-of-programmed-dna-elimination/?lang=en)).

**The numbers, which are the fun part**

- **Sea lamprey:** roughly **20%** of the genome is physically removed from somatic progenitor cells. What gets discarded includes germline-specific chromosomes packed with repetitive elements, plus hundreds of protein-coding genes whose functions are germline-specific ([*Cell Reports*, 2023 germline genome assembly](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/fulltext/S2211-1247(23)00274-7)).
- **Songbirds:** most of them eliminate an entire germline-restricted chromosome from the somatic lineage during early embryogenesis. In the zebra finch that chromosome is more than **10%** of the genome: over **150 megabases** ([review in *Current Opinion in Genetics & Development*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959437X2300093X)).
- **Some ciliates:** over **90%** of the genome is eliminated from the somatic lineage, including the transposable elements ([*Current Biology* primer](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(24)01074-1)).
- **Ascaris roundworms:** this was first observed by **Theodor Boveri in the 1880s**, watching chromosome fragments get discarded under a light microscope, decades before anyone knew what DNA was.

**Why I think this belongs in this sub**

We spend a lot of time here on copies: uploads, backups, substrate independence, what it would mean to instantiate a person twice. And it turns out evolution has been running a version of that architecture for hundreds of millions of years, in an unexpected direction.

The organism maintains **one complete copy of its own information**, sequestered in a cell lineage that does nothing else, and it deliberately ships a **reduced copy** to the part of itself that actually lives, eats, and dies.

The body is the lossy render. The germline is the master file.

That is an archive/working-copy split, arrived at independently in lampreys, songbirds, roundworms and ciliates. It suggests the distinction is not a human metaphor imported onto biology. It is a solution biology keeps rediscovering, presumably because keeping one pristine copy and letting the working copies degrade is cheaper than protecting everything.

**What we don't know yet**

A lot, and I want to be straight about it because this is the part the pop-science version drops.

Nobody has settled **why** elimination evolved repeatedly. There are three main candidate explanations, and all three are hypotheses: silencing transposable elements; resolving genetic conflict between the germline and the body; or protecting somatic tissue from genes that are only safe to express in the germline. The review treats the mechanistic diversity itself as evidence that different lineages may have arrived at elimination for different reasons.

Also, and this matters: **mammals do not do this.** Your somatic cells carry the same genome as your germ cells. So nothing above is a fact about your body. It is a fact about how information gets partitioned across the tree of life, which is a different and more interesting claim.

**The one paragraph about what I build**

I store DNA records and sealed video messages, a time capsule with your genome in it, kept as a physical record at a third-party biorepository, $99/year billed annually. We are storage only: no sequencing, no screening, nothing reproductive, and no promise about anything downstream. I bring it up because the germline/soma split above is the closest thing I have found to a natural precedent for what an archive is actually for, and because it would be dishonest to write 800 words on the subject without saying who I am.

Curious what this sub makes of the conflict hypothesis specifically. If the germline-restricted chromosome in songbirds really is a genetic-conflict artifact rather than a feature, does that change how you read the archive framing at all?

**What we don't know yet (one-line version for the top comment if asked):** the function of programmed DNA elimination is unresolved, the review describes mechanistic diversity rather than a single explanation, and none of it occurs in mammals.
