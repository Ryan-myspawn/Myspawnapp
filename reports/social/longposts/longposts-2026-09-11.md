## #57 [FACEBOOK] — "The Company That Held Your Father's Voice Stopped Answering the Phone"
**Freshness check:** HereAfter has appeared as a line item in #4 (23andMe custody, Aug 19) and as one bullet inside an explainer, but never as a longpost, and the endgame details are new since August. Distinct hook structure (company-obituary open).
**Primary audience:** 35-60 FB voice; men-25-55 rules respected (no sentimentality, facts do the work).
**Trend-hook used:** the shutdown's documented completion, August 2026.
**Lane:** trust/custody, story-driven. ~620 words.

In 2019 a journalist named James Vlahos started a company called HereAfter AI, and the idea was genuinely good.

It interviewed you about your life. It kept your **actual voice** answering actual questions — not a synthesized imitation, your real recordings. Later your family could ask you something and hear you answer. Some people used it because they had time. Some used it because they were running out.

**In July 2026, HereAfter announced it was shutting down.**

**As of August, the homepage does not load.** The iOS app has not been updated since **September 2023**, which in hindsight was the first warning anyone could have read. And if you want your father's voice back, the reported route is to email a company that is winding down and hope someone is still reading that inbox.

**Here is the part I cannot stop turning over.** Nothing was lost to a fire. No hard drive failed. No format went obsolete. The audio files were fine the entire time, and they are probably still fine right now, sitting on storage somebody is paying for until they stop.

What failed was the institution.

**Meanwhile the category is booming.** Digital-afterlife services are projected at roughly **$5 billion in 2026** — griefbots, AI voice memorials, holographic avatars, VR reconstructions. Adoption is significant in China and South Korea, where a televised VR reconstruction of a deceased child was watched by millions.

So the demand is real. Grief is the oldest market on earth. What is new is that it ships software updates, and software companies have a mortality rate.

**Three things this should change about how you keep anything.**

**1. Keep your own copy of anything irreplaceable.** If a service holds the only version of a recording, you have a subscription, not an archive. Export it. Put it on something you control. Do it this weekend for anything with a voice in it.

**2. Read the wind-down clause before you need it.** Every long-term service has one, or conspicuously doesn't. What happens to your content if the company is acquired, or folds? Is retrieval a contractual obligation or a courtesy? Ask in writing.

**3. Treat an un-updated app as a countdown.** Three years without an update is not a stable product. It is a product with nobody left on it.

I run a preservation company, so I will say the uncomfortable version out loud: every one of those questions applies to us exactly as hard as it applies to HereAfter. That is why we tell you the name of the independent facility that physically holds our members' samples rather than just saying "secure storage," and it is why I would rather you ask us the awkward question now than email us about it in 2033.

Nobody at HereAfter was a villain. They built something kind, and the business underneath it did not last. That is the ordinary, unglamorous way most archives actually die.

What is the one recording in your family that exists in only one place right now?

## #58 [X] — "Two Dishes, One Word, Two Completely Different Futures"
**Freshness check:** the iPSC-vs-testicular-stem-cell distinction has never been a longpost. Distinct from #12 (JQ1 meiosis switch, Aug 23) and #39 (meiosis-wall zero-euploid explainer, Sep 3) in mechanism, claim and structure.
**Primary audience: Men 25-55.**
**Trend-hook used:** a May 13, 2026 company announcement and the vocabulary problem it exposes.
**Lane:** science-explainer. **ZERO PRODUCT (no MySpawn mention).** ~570 words.

Two labs. Two different starting cells. The press calls both of them the same thing, and that word is going to be used to sell people hope.

**Dish one: IVG, strictly defined.** In vitro gametogenesis means taking an ordinary cell — skin, blood, whatever — reprogramming it backwards into an induced pluripotent stem cell, then pushing it forward into an egg or a sperm.

Status, unchanged as of 2026: researchers have made **human primordial germ cell-like cells** from iPSCs. Nobody has finished the journey. **No lab has produced a mature, functional human gamete from an iPSC.** That sentence is precise on purpose, and I will come back to why.

**Dish two: in vitro spermatogenesis.** Some men produce no mature sperm but still have the spermatogonial stem cells that are supposed to make them. Nothing is reprogrammed here. The goal is to finish, in a dish, a maturation the body did not finish.

**On May 13, 2026, a company called Paterna Biosciences announced it had done exactly that** — functional human sperm grown outside the body from a testicular biopsy, used to create early test embryos as what it described as preliminary safety validation.

**How much weight to put on that, stated flatly:** it is a **company announcement**. It has **not been published in a peer-reviewed journal**. **No independent lab has replicated it.** It is **not cleared for clinical use.** That is not an accusation — it is the epistemic status, and the coverage I read said the same. If it survives peer review and replication it is a major result for male-factor infertility. Until then the honest verb is "claims."

**Why be this pedantic?** Because the two dishes imply different worlds.

Reprogramming any cell into a gamete would eventually decouple reproduction from having usable gametes at all, with everything that follows — for older parents, for same-sex couples, for people sterilized by cancer treatment, and for a set of ethical problems nobody has solved.

Maturing a man's own existing stem cells is a **treatment for a specific medical condition**. Enormous for the men it helps. Not a civilizational hinge.

Same headline word. Wildly different futures.

**The two questions that survive any version of this story:**
1. **From what starting cell?**
2. **Published where?**

Peer-reviewed journal, preprint, or press release is the whole second answer, and the gap between them is where most science reporting goes wrong.

I'll add one admission, because it is the reason I wrote this. Our own standing line used to be a looser version of "IVG has produced no mature human gametes," and someone quoting the Paterna announcement could have called that sloppy. It has been corrected in our public notes. The vocabulary matters even when — especially when — you are the one using it carelessly.

## #59 [X] — "The Estate Document That Beats Your Will, and Takes Twenty Minutes"
**Freshness check:** beneficiary designations have never been a longpost. Distinct from #27 (RUFADAA digital-account access, Aug 29) and #48 (the checklist-bug essay, Sep 9) — different instrument, different failure, different hook.
**Primary audience: Men 25-55.**
**Trend-hook used:** Trust & Will's 2026 report and this week's estate-planning research.
**Lane:** practical legacy guide. ~560 words.

There is one estate document that overrides your will, that most people have never deliberately reviewed, and that takes about twenty minutes to fix.

It is the beneficiary designation on your accounts.

**How it actually works.** Retirement accounts, life insurance policies, annuities, payable-on-death bank accounts and transfer-on-death brokerage accounts pass **directly to the named beneficiary**, outside your will and outside probate. Your will governs what is left over. It does not govern these.

**The failure mode, which estate attorneys describe as the most common one they see:** someone divorces, carefully updates their will, and never touches the 401(k) or the life insurance. The ex-spouse is still named. Courts have consistently held that the designation controls, **even when the deceased person's intent was obvious**.

Read that twice if you have been divorced, remarried, widowed, or simply opened a 401(k) at a job you left in 2014.

**The upside is real too.** With correct designations, the money moves to the people you chose in days, with no legal fees and no public court file. Without them, the same assets can spend months in probate at meaningful cost.

**The twenty-minute version, tonight:**
1. List every account that has a beneficiary field: employer 401(k)s (including old ones), IRAs, life insurance through work and outside it, HSA, brokerage, bank accounts with POD.
2. Log in and read the **current** named beneficiary on each. Not what you remember choosing.
3. Add contingent beneficiaries. If your primary predeceases you and there is no contingent, the asset can fall back into probate — the exact thing you were avoiding.
4. Re-check after every life event: marriage, divorce, birth, death, job change.

**Why this is the highest-leverage hour in personal estate planning:** because **58% of millennials have no estate planning documents at all**, while **73% say estate planning matters to them** (Trust & Will 2026 report, n=5,000). The gap is not apathy, it is that nobody publishes the order of operations. Beneficiaries first, because they are free, fast, and they outrank the expensive document you have not written yet.

Then digital access — Apple Legacy Contact and Google Inactive Account Manager, ten minutes each. Then the will, with an attorney if there are children or property.

Nothing in this post is legal advice and none of it sells anything of ours. It is genuinely just the cheapest useful thing most people reading this have not done.

## #60 [FACEBOOK] — "Five Things That Ruin a DNA Sample, and Four of Them Are Moisture"
**Freshness check:** at-home collection procedure has never been a longpost. Distinct from #33 (rootless-hair forensics, Aug 31) and from any pricing piece.
**Primary audience:** 35-60 practical FB voice; men-25-55 rules respected.
**Trend-hook used:** none needed (evergreen how-to, handling guidance verified this week).
**Lane:** practical guide. ~520 words.

If you ever collect a DNA sample at home — a kit, a family project, an elderly parent you want on record — the procedure is easy and the failure modes are boring. Here they are, in the order they actually go wrong.

**1. Moisture.** The dominant killer. A damp swab grows bacteria and mold, and bacterial activity is what degrades the DNA. Air-dry everything **completely** before it goes in an envelope.

**2. Plastic.** This is the one that surprises people. **Paper breathes; plastic traps.** A sealed plastic bag holds residual moisture against the sample and becomes a mold incubator. Collection guidance is consistent: store and ship in **paper envelopes, never plastic**. If your kit includes a plastic sleeve, that is outer packaging, not sample storage.

**3. Your own hands and your lunch.** Touching a swab tip transfers your skin cells. Eating, drinking, smoking or chewing gum within 30 minutes before a cheek swab adds food debris and bacteria — the most common home contaminant. One sample, one clean surface, one pair of hands.

**4. Heat.** A glovebox in August, a sunny windowsill, a mail satchel on a hot afternoon. Cool and dry is the entire instruction.

**5. Delay.** Every day in an unstabilized envelope is a day of slow degradation. Collect, dry, seal, send the same week.

**Two technical points worth knowing:**

**Cut hair is close to useless.** The nuclear DNA is in the **follicle**, so hair has to be *plucked* with a visible bulb attached. A snipped strand is not a sample.

**Do not freeze it at home.** For dried swabs, hair and nails, cool and dry beats cold and damp — and freezing a moist sample is worse than simply drying it properly.

**One comparative finding**, from work comparing storage over time: **hair follicles held up better than buccal swabs**, with greater deterioration observed in the swabs. If you can collect both, collect both.

That is the whole craft. Seven minutes at a kitchen table, and the hard part was never the collection — it is who is still holding the thing in thirty years, which is a different question and a longer conversation.

Is there anyone in your family whose sample nobody has ever thought to take?

## #61 [REDDIT r/Entrepreneur] — "I had 10 finished scripts and 0 filmed videos. The fix wasn't motivation, it was sorting."
**Freshness check:** build-in-public lane, but the topic (batching by setup instead of by priority) is new — #14 marketing autopilot, #20 pricing error, #25 day-zero ledger, #30 cron fleet, #56 outage post-mortem. New hook structure (inventory-count open).
**Primary audience:** r/Entrepreneur operators.
**Trend-hook used:** honest — it happened this week.
**Lane:** story-driven ops. Low product. ~560 words.

Disclosure: I run a small DNA-storage startup and an agent fleet that writes most of our content. This is an ops post, not a pitch.

**Current state: 10 finished, quality-checked video scripts. 0 videos filmed in 12 days.**

The production side never stopped. Every day a script got written, reviewed against a checklist and filed. Every day nothing got shot. Classic bottleneck, and I spent a week and a half treating it as a discipline problem.

It wasn't. It was a **sorting** problem.

**The realization:** my backlog was ordered by the date each script was written. That is the worst possible order for actually executing it, because consecutive items had nothing physically in common. Script 1 needed a kitchen. Script 2 needed a hardware store. Script 3 needed a desk. Script 4 needed a workbench. Each one looked like its own production day, so each one got deferred.

**The fix took ten minutes.** I re-sorted the same ten scripts by **setup** — location, props, lighting — instead of by date or priority.

Result: **six of the ten shoot in about 90 minutes.** Four of them are the same desk with a different object on it; you change the object, not the lighting. One is the kitchen. One is the workbench. The remaining four genuinely need different locations or permissions, so they got scheduled separately instead of blocking everything else.

Twelve days of paralysis was hiding roughly **90 minutes of actual work**.

**Three transferable pieces:**

**1. Backlogs inherit the order they were created in, and that order is almost always wrong for execution.** Creation order optimizes for the writer. Setup order optimizes for the doer. They are rarely the same person, and when they are the same person, they are not in the same mood.

**2. "One task" is a lie when the setup cost dominates.** Filming one 60-second video and filming four of them at the same desk are nearly the same job. If the setup is 80% of the cost, batch or don't bother.

**3. Sort first, then judge motivation.** I was about to conclude I was avoiding the work. What I was actually avoiding was six separate setups I had accidentally invented by filing things chronologically.

**What I don't know yet:** whether the 90 minutes actually happens. The plan is written, the props are listed, and the honest answer is that a sorted backlog is still a backlog until someone presses record. I'll post the result either way, including if the result is that I still didn't do it.

**Sources / verifiable details (inline, per sub norms):** the scripts, the dated backlog and the reordered shot list are all timestamped in a public repo; the 12-day gap runs 2026-08-30 to 2026-09-11. No claims here about anyone else's process — this is one small company's dumb week, documented.
