## #200 [X] Three vaults built to outlast us, and what each is actually betting on
*Lane: science-explainer. ZERO product mention. 553 words.*

Three serious projects are trying to make information survive past the civilisation that made it. They have picked three different materials, and the material is the whole argument.

**The Arctic one is film.**

On **2 February 2020**, GitHub took a snapshot of every active public repository on its platform. It came to **21 terabytes**. That data was written to piqlFilm and deposited on **8 July 2020** into the Arctic Code Vault, a section of the Arctic World Archive, inside **decommissioned Mine 3** in Svalbard.

Piql's own announcement puts the deposit at **186 reels**. At least one outlet reported 180. *(Company announcement versus press report; 186 is the better-supported figure and the conflict is left standing rather than averaged.)*

Now the number that matters. **The film's documented specification is 500 years.** GitHub's framing was code preserved for **over 1,000 years**, and Piql has described the vault as preserving data for 500 or 1,000. *(Vendor specification versus aspirational claim. Nobody has run the test, because the test takes five centuries.)*

**The Austrian one is clay.**

**Memory of Mankind** was founded in **2012** by ceramicist **Martin Kunze** in Hallstatt, Austria. Text and images are engraved onto ceramic tablets, a medium chosen because the oldest surviving written records we have, Sumerian clay tablets, are made of roughly the same thing.

Each tablet holds about **five million characters**, which the project compares to a 400-page book. **By 2018 there were over 500 tablets.**

They sit about **two kilometres inside the Hallstatt salt mine**, which has been worked for roughly **7,000 years**, under Mount Plassen. The project claims the site could protect the archive for **at least a million years**. *(Project claim, not an independent finding.)*

**And one detail is better than the engineering.**

The former chief officer of the mine signed a contract allowing the archive to remain there **rent-free, forever.**

**That is the real bet, and almost nobody notices it.**

Every one of these projects has two failure modes, and only one of them is about physics. The film can survive five hundred years in a cold mine and still be lost, if the organisation holding it folds, sells the site, or simply stops paying attention. The clay can outlast the alphabet it is written in.

**Material durability is the easy half. Institutional durability is the half that actually kills archives**, and it is the half that never appears in the brochure, because it cannot be specified in years.

Which is why the rent-free-forever contract is the most interesting sentence in this entire subject. Somebody noticed that the mine was a tenancy problem before it was a materials problem.

**Three things worth taking from this:**

**1. When a storage claim gives you one number, ask who tested it.** Five hundred years is an extrapolation from accelerated ageing, not an observation. That does not make it wrong. It makes it a model.

**2. When a claim gives you two numbers, prefer the smaller one.** Five hundred is the specification. A thousand is the ambition. Both got printed.

**3. Ask who is paying the rent in year two hundred.** Nobody has a good answer. The projects that have thought about it at all are ahead of everyone else.

## #201 [FACEBOOK] A potter in Austria has been quietly writing down our century
*Lane: story-driven. Zero product mention. 536 words.*

In 2012 a ceramicist named Martin Kunze started a project in Hallstatt, a small town in Austria, that is either the most sensible thing anyone has done about memory or the strangest. Possibly both.

He engraves things onto ceramic tablets.

Not important things, necessarily. Newspaper pages. Scientific papers. Ordinary people's stories, submitted by ordinary people. One tablet holds around five million characters, which is roughly a four-hundred-page book.

By 2018 there were more than five hundred of them.

And then he puts them two kilometres inside a salt mine under Mount Plassen, a mine that people have been working for something like seven thousand years. Salt is dry, stable and uninterested in your archive. It is very good at leaving things alone.

The project's own estimate is that the site could protect what is in it for at least a million years. That is the project's claim rather than a tested fact, and the number is so large that arguing about it misses the point.

Here is the part I keep thinking about.

He chose clay because clay has a track record. The oldest writing we can still read was pressed into it by Sumerians. Not because they had the best technology, but because the medium they happened to use is nearly indestructible and requires no device to read.

Every storage format invented since has been better in every way except the one that counts. You cannot read a hard drive by holding it up to the light.

And then there is the detail that has nothing to do with materials at all. The former head of the salt mine signed a contract letting the archive stay there rent-free, forever.

Think about what that sentence is doing. Somebody looked at a thousand-year project and understood that the thing most likely to end it was not fire, flood or decay. It was a landlord.

That is true of almost everything people try to preserve. The photographs survive and the person who knew who was in them does not. The file format outlives the company that could open it. The storage is fine; the arrangement around the storage is what quietly fails.

Salt is also doing something subtler than keeping water out. A working mine is a place somebody visits. An abandoned one is a hole. Seven thousand years of continuous work is not just a durability statistic, it is a record of people turning up.

I do not think the answer is that everyone needs a salt mine. Most of what we want to keep is not a civilisational record, it is a much smaller thing: who we were, said plainly, somewhere a person who comes later can actually find it.

But the two questions Kunze answered are the right ones for anybody, at any scale. **What is it written on, and who is responsible for it in fifty years?**

Most of us have a confident answer to the first and have never thought about the second.

What is the oldest thing you own that someone else made, and would you know how to pass it on properly?

## #202 [X] Nobody has ever tested a 500-year storage claim
*Lane: contrarian take, punching at a practice rather than a company. 579 words.*

Here is a sentence that should bother you more than it does.

**Every long-term storage lifespan you have ever read is a model, not a measurement.**

The Arctic World Archive stores data on piqlFilm with a documented specification of **500 years**. GitHub, depositing **21 terabytes** of code there in **2020**, described the goal as preservation for **over 1,000 years**. Piql has described the vault as 500 or 1,000.

*(Vendor specification against aspirational framing, both from 2020, both via coverage.)*

Note what just happened. Two numbers, differing by a factor of two, attached to the same physical object, in the same news cycle.

**Neither is a lie. Neither is an observation either.**

Nobody has stored anything for five hundred years and checked. The oldest photographic film is under two hundred years old. These figures come from accelerated ageing: heat a sample, measure how fast it degrades, extrapolate down to room temperature. It is real science and it is genuinely the best available method.

It is also a straight line drawn through conditions nobody has lived through.

**The contrarian bit is not that the numbers are wrong. It is that the number is the wrong thing to be comparing.**

Three failure modes end an archive, and only the first one has a specification sheet.

**One, the medium degrades.** This is the one everybody models, and the one the industry is genuinely good at.

**Two, nothing can read it.** A medium can be in perfect condition and functionally dead. We can still read Sumerian clay because the reader is an eye. The further a format sits from that, the more it depends on a supply chain that also has to survive.

**Three, the institution stops.** The organisation folds, the lease ends, the site is sold, the person who cared retires. **No accelerated-ageing test models a bankruptcy.**

Memory of Mankind, the ceramic-tablet archive in a Hallstatt salt mine, is the only one of these projects I have seen address the third directly: the former chief officer of the mine signed a contract allowing the archive to stay **rent-free, forever**. *(Project materials, via index.)*

A contract is not a material property. It is the only one of the three failure modes you can actually do something about this year.

**So here is the test, and it works on anyone selling you durability, including us.**

**Ask for the second number.** If they give you one, it is marketing. If they give you a specification and an ambition and tell you which is which, they have read their own data sheet.

**Ask what reads it.** If the answer requires a specific device, a specific company or a specific format, that is a dependency, and dependencies have their own lifespans.

**Ask who pays in year fifty.** Not year five hundred. Fifty. If there is no answer, the five-hundred-year figure is decorative.

We sell DNA storage at an accredited facility, at ambient temperature, for an annual fee. **Every one of those three questions applies to us, and the third one is the one we would want asked.** An annual fee is a recurring relationship, and a recurring relationship can end from either side. That is a real property of the arrangement and not a flaw to be written around.

The brochure number is the easiest thing to produce and the least informative thing on the page.

## #203 [REDDIT] r/transhumanism: the boring failure mode nobody plans for

Disclosure first: I work on a DNA storage service. I am not linking it and this post is not about it. The subject below is one I keep running into and I have not seen it discussed here properly.

Long-term preservation gets discussed almost entirely as a materials problem. Which substrate lasts longest, which encoding is densest, which vault is furthest from a fault line. That is the interesting part and it is also, I think, the smallest part.

Two reference points worth knowing if you do not already:

**The Arctic World Archive**, in a decommissioned coal mine on Svalbard, holds data written to piqlFilm. GitHub deposited a 21 TB snapshot of every active public repository there in 2020. The film's documented specification is 500 years; GitHub's own framing was over 1,000. Both figures were published in the same period. Neither has been tested, because testing takes five centuries.

**Memory of Mankind**, in a Hallstatt salt mine, engraves onto ceramic tablets, about five million characters each, 500-plus tablets by 2018, roughly two kilometres underground in a mine worked for seven thousand years. The project claims protection for at least a million years. That is a project claim, not an independent measurement.

Here is what I think is underrated.

Memory of Mankind's most durable feature may not be the ceramic. The mine's former chief officer signed a contract letting the archive stay rent-free, forever.

That is the only one of the three real failure modes anyone addressed on purpose.

**Failure mode one: the medium degrades.** Heavily modelled, genuinely well understood, least likely to be what gets you.

**Failure mode two: nothing can read it.** Clay works because the reader is a human eye. Everything else inherits the lifespan of its reading stack.

**Failure mode three: the institution stops.** Nobody models this and it is the one that empties archives. Organisations fold. Leases end. The person who cared retires and the replacement does not.

For anything personal, the framing I have landed on is: a time capsule with your genome in it is a storage problem for about a year and a custody problem for every year after that. The hard question is not what it is written on. It is who is responsible for it when you are not around to ask, and what happens if that party stops existing.

**What we do not know yet**, stated plainly: nobody has empirically validated any multi-century storage lifespan, in any medium, including the ones I would benefit from you believing in. The figures are extrapolations from accelerated ageing. That is the best available method and it is still a model. And I have no data on institutional survival rates for archives, because as far as I can tell nobody collects it, which is itself the point.

If anyone has seen actual longitudinal work on archive institutions failing rather than media failing, I would genuinely like to read it.

## #204 [X] The document that fails because of when it is read
*Lane: practical legacy guide. 542 words.*

**DECLARED CROSS-LANE DIVISION:** today's blog article covers the law of disposition authority in full. **This is the one-paragraph action from inside it, and it shares no sentence with that piece.**

Most planning mistakes are mistakes of content. This one is a mistake of timing, and it is almost universal.

**If your funeral instructions are in your will, they will probably arrive too late to be followed.**

Not because the will is invalid. Because of when a will gets read. Guidance on this is consistent: a will is usually not located and read until after services are already under way or finished. The decision the document was written to govern has been made by then, often within forty-eight hours of death, by whoever had the legal authority at the time.

**The legal term for that authority is the right of sepulcher**, and in most US states it runs in a specific order.

**First: a person you named in writing, in the instrument your state recognises.** New York gives first priority to an agent named in a written instrument, under Public Health Law § 4201, and the state health department publishes a form. A handwritten note does not qualify.

**Then: a statutory list of relatives**, which differs by state, and which typically also requires that person to be an adult, mentally competent, and willing to take on the costs.

Here is the structural fact that surprises most people. Read several of these statutes side by side and **the top rank is not a person. It is a document.** Missouri's first rank is an attorney in fact under a power of attorney that **specifically** grants the right of sepulcher. Louisiana starts from a notarized declaration. Relatives only appear once the instrument is missing.

*(State statutes and law-firm commentary, read through a search index. Rankings are state-specific and some retrievable Missouri material was bill drafts rather than enacted law. Nothing here is legal advice.)*

**So the practical version is four lines.**

**1. Use your state's designation form, not your will.** It goes by names like Designation of Agent for Final Disposition. Some states fold the authority into the health care proxy or power of attorney instead.

**2. Get the formalities right.** Washington requires the document to be signed and dated in the presence of a witness. Rhode Island wants a signed, notarized original. A document that misses the formality can rank below a person you did not choose.

**3. If you are relying on a power of attorney, check the grant is express.** At least one state does not assume a general power carries it.

**4. Keep it somewhere reachable in forty-eight hours, and tell the person.** The same sequencing problem that defeats the will defeats a safe deposit box, and an agent who hears about the appointment from a funeral director starts from behind.

One honest qualifier, against the advice's own interest: **your agent is not obliged in any state to carry out wishes that are impractical, illegal or financially burdensome.** The form assigns authority. It does not create an enforceable order.

Which is still far more than a sentence in a document nobody will open until Thursday.
