## #107 [X] You own your DNA in Connecticut as of today. You also cannot sue anyone over it.

Connecticut's genetic-privacy provisions took effect **this morning, 1 October 2026**.

They are real and they are unusually strong. **A consumer has a property right in the biological sample and in the genetic test results derived from it.** Not a privacy interest. Property.

**You can require that the sample itself be destroyed**, which is a separate request from deleting your results. **You can revoke consent.** A company has to obtain express consent for collection, use, disclosure and retention, **and again for secondary uses, third-party transfers and keeping your sample after the test is finished.**

To hand your genetic data to a third party for research, a direct-to-consumer company needs informed consent **in accordance with the federal Common Rule**. That is a research-ethics standard, imported wholesale into a consumer statute.

**Now the part almost nobody mentions.**

### There is no private right of action

**The genetic provisions carry no private right of action.** Enforcement runs **solely through the Attorney General**, under the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act. Reported penalties: **up to $5,000 per willful violation**, plus restitution and injunctive relief.

So the right is yours and the remedy is not. **If a company ignores your destruction request, you do not sue. You complain to one office**, and that office decides whether your complaint is worth its week.

**One detail makes that office more serious than it sounds.** Connecticut's statutory **right to cure expired on 31 December 2024**. Most state privacy laws give a company a mandatory window to fix a violation after notice. Connecticut no longer does. **Counsel advise that a controller receiving a notice of violation should assume the AG is prepared to sue if it is not fixed promptly.**

### What this actually changes for you

**Two requests, not one.** "Delete my results" and "destroy my sample" have always been different asks. In one state they are now two different rights, and a company that answers the first while quietly keeping the second is in a different position than it was yesterday.

**Ask in writing. Keep the reply.** Your leverage is not a lawsuit. It is a documented refusal that an Attorney General can read.

**And check which state's law you are under.** This is Connecticut. Your state may give you none of this.

### The Common Rule clause is the sleeper

Buried in the transfer rules is a requirement that stands out. **To send your genetic data to a third party for research, a direct-to-consumer company must obtain informed consent in accordance with the federal Common Rule.**

That is the standard governing human-subjects research at universities and hospitals. **It is not a checkbox in a terms-of-service document**, and it is a materially higher bar than the consent most consumer genetics companies have historically relied on to move data into research partnerships.

**Whether companies actually meet it is now an enforcement question rather than a drafting question**, which brings us back to the office that does the enforcing.

### Connecticut is not alone, and it is not a national rule

**2026 has been an active year for state genetic-privacy statutes.** Utah and South Dakota both enacted laws this year, and several other states advanced bills. Our own files record **South Dakota's taking effect on 1 July 2026.**

**None of that helps you if you live somewhere that passed nothing.** There is no federal equivalent. A bill aimed at this gap, **S. 1916, was introduced on 22 May 2025 and referred to the Judiciary Committee**, where it has sat since.

So the honest summary of today: **a strong right, in one state, enforced by one office, with no private remedy, and no national floor underneath it.**

## #108 [X] 839 devices got the FDA's fast-track label. 81 reached the market.

Here is a number that should change how you read health-tech headlines.

**Of 839 devices granted FDA Breakthrough Device Designation as of 30 June 2023, 81 had been cleared, approved or granted De Novo classification.** Roughly nine in ten had not reached a market authorization at that point.

**Breakthrough designation is not approval.** It gives a sponsor prioritized review, a dedicated review team, senior-manager engagement and more frequent interaction with the agency.

**It does not lower the standard** for clearance, De Novo classification or premarket approval. It is a queue position, not a verdict.

### Four words that get swapped in every press cycle

**Cleared** comes from the 510(k) pathway. It means a moderate-risk device was found **substantially equivalent to a predicate device already on the market**: same intended use, and either the same technological characteristics or different ones raising no new safety or effectiveness questions. **Reported typical review: around 90 calendar days.**

Read the test again. **The comparison is against another product, not against an outcome.** A device can be cleared because it resembles something that was cleared because it resembled something earlier. Nobody in that chain has to show it helps anyone.

**Approved** is premarket approval, for high-risk Class III devices. It requires **valid scientific evidence, usually clinical data, independently establishing safety and effectiveness.** Clearance compares. Approval evaluates.

**Granted** is De Novo, for a genuinely novel low-to-moderate-risk device with no predicate. A grant authorizes marketing **and creates a new classification and product code that other companies can then cite as their predicate.** One De Novo opens a door others walk through. Reported waits rose to around **420 days in 2024**.

**And an IND is none of these.** An Investigational New Drug application takes effect **30 calendar days after the agency receives it**, unless the agency imposes a clinical hold or says earlier that trials may proceed. **The default is permission.** An IND clearing is closer to a non-objection than a grant, and it means a trial may begin.

### The habit

When a company says it "received FDA" anything, ask which word. **Designated, permitted to start, cleared, granted, approved.** Five events, five meanings, and only two of them rest on an independent evaluation of the product itself.

Then ask two more things.

**Product or establishment?** The agency does not approve establishments at all. **"FDA-registered" means a facility has told the agency it exists and is inspectable**, and it conveys no approval or endorsement of the facility or anything it makes. Registration applies to buildings. Clearance, approval and authorization apply to products. **The agency issues warning letters over stretched "FDA-registered" claims, which tells you how often the phrase gets used as a rating.**

**What is the date of the event, rather than the date of the article?** Coverage recycles. An authorization from eighteen months ago can arrive in your feed looking like this week's news, and **two sources giving two different dates for "the same" event are frequently describing two different events** at different stages of the same product's life.

### Why the predicate chain matters more than it sounds

The 510(k) test compares a device to a predicate. **That predicate was itself usually cleared by comparison to an earlier one.** Follow the chain back far enough and you reach a device that was on the market before the modern framework existed.

This is not a conspiracy; it is how a tiered, risk-proportionate system is designed to work, and forcing a low-risk device through a full approval would be its own kind of harm. **But it does mean the sentence "FDA cleared" carries information about lineage and almost none about benefit**, and a company saying "cleared" while a reader hears "approved" has said nothing untrue.

## #109 [FACEBOOK] Yesterday four things I made failed the same reader, and I had not noticed him leaving

I want to tell you about a bad day that was only visible because of a boring process.

Since **23 August** every piece of content this company makes goes through a bench of three imaginary readers before it ships. A **38-year-old father and provider**. A **29-year-old who is undecided and optimizes everything**. A **50-year-old estate planner**. Each piece gets scored against all three. **Fail two of three and it gets reworked or killed.**

It is a crude instrument. That is the point. A crude instrument you actually use beats a sophisticated one you talk about.

**Yesterday, 30 September, four separate things scored two out of three.** Two image ads and two short text posts. They passed, because two of three is a pass.

**And all four lost the same man.**

The 29-year-old optimizer liked everything. The 50-year-old estate planner liked everything. **The father failed all four**, and the reason was identical every time: the piece was analytical, it was about method or structure or how to read a number, and **there was nothing in it that touched his family.**

One was an ad about infrastructure. One was about how many prices a service displays. One was about why two readings of the same value are not a trend. One was a post about a system failing twice in a row.

Every one of those is true. Every one is useful. **Not one of them is about him.**

I did not notice while making them, because each one passed on its own. **Two of three is a pass, and four passes in a row look like a good day.** The pattern only exists when you line the scores up at the end, which I nearly did not do.

Here is the part I find uncomfortable. **The provider is the reader this company was built for.** A man thinking about what remains of him is the entire premise. And over one day I wrote four things for the two readers who are easier to write for: the optimizer, who likes a procedure, and the planner, who likes a mechanism.

Analytical writing is easier. It never requires you to say anything that might sound sentimental, and it lets you be clever instead of plain.

I have not fixed it by rewriting anything. **Rewriting four pieces overnight would hide the signal and not change the habit.** It is a commissioning problem: what gets ordered in the first place, not how it gets worded afterwards.

So the change is upstream. Before anything is briefed, one question: **who is this for, and if the answer is "everyone", it is for the two easy ones.**

If you make things for a living, you have a version of this. **A score that passes, four times, while the same person quietly leaves the room.**

**Does your process catch patterns across a day, or only problems inside one piece?**

## #110 [REDDIT r/SideProject] Our ad template could only ever show the top 40% of a photo. We found out after four crops.

Founder here, and this is a design bug in something I built, not a complaint about a vendor.

We make static image ads from HTML. Square ones are 1080x1080. The photograph sits underneath, and on top of it sit a gradient and a scrim so the white text stays readable.

**The gradient is 669 pixels tall. The scrim is another 260.** On a 1080-pixel canvas that leaves roughly the **top 40 percent** of the photograph actually visible. Everything below it is decoration.

I knew those numbers. I wrote them. **I had never once asked what they implied about which photographs can work.**

Yesterday I found out.

**The concept was a pricing one: how many prices does a service display?** The photo was a set of three empty fitted shelves in an alcove. The image is a thing you count, which is exactly what the headline asks you to do.

**Four crops.** Every crop that kept more than one shelf pushed the rest under the gradient. Every crop that kept the frame clean showed a single dark horizontal bar that reads as nothing at all.

Then I put the same photograph into our 9x16 layout, which leaves about 60 percent of the frame clear.

**It read instantly. Two shelves, obvious, countable.** Same photo, same tint, same text.

### The rule that came out of it

**A photograph whose meaning is a vertical sequence cannot be read in our square template.** Shelves, stairs, a stack, a ladder of rungs. The format can only ever show you one element of a sequence, and one element of a sequence is not a sequence.

So: vertical-sequence subjects get briefed for 9x16 or not at all.

**And it explained something backwards.** We have shipped ladder photographs before that worked fine. I assumed that meant ladders were safe. **They were leaning diagonals.** A diagonal reads at any crop because it crosses the frame. A vertical stack does not. **I had been drawing the wrong lesson from my own successes for weeks.**

### What we did not know yet

I do not know how many previous briefs died of this without anyone diagnosing it. We kill photographs constantly: three more went yesterday, for brightness, for signage visible only at full resolution, and for repeating an earlier ad's visual hook. **Those kills get logged with reasons. "The template cannot show this subject" was never one of the reasons, because nobody had thought of it.** Some unknown share of the past month's failures may be this bug wearing a different coat, and I cannot currently tell you how many.

The cheap fix is a line in the brief template. **The expensive lesson is that I had a constraint written down in pixels and never translated it into a sentence about subjects.**

If you have a layout with a fixed overlay, go and work out what fraction of the image survives it, then ask what kind of picture that fraction can carry. **It took me one afternoon and four wasted crops to ask a question I could have asked in March.**

## #111 [FACEBOOK] The fact is true. The week most people hear it in is the problem.

Here is something true about cremation, and then something I think is wrong about how it usually reaches people.

**Cremation destroys nuclear DNA.** Reported operating temperatures run roughly **1,400 to 1,800 degrees Fahrenheit**, with sources also describing **870 to 980 degrees Celsius**. At those temperatures the phosphodiester bonds that form the DNA backbone break down, and the molecule fragments past the point where a laboratory can amplify a usable region.

**Forensic work puts hard numbers on where it stops being possible.** One conference study reports that DNA extraction was **not possible** from samples treated at **400C for 10 and 15 minutes; 500C for 15 minutes; 600C for 5 minutes; and 700C for 5, 10 and 15 minutes.** Cremation runs far above all of those.

There are narrow exceptions and they are worth knowing honestly. Laboratories look for **bone that escaped the hottest zones of the retort**: dense material such as **tooth roots** or the **petrous portion of the temporal bone**. **None of that is a plan.** You do not choose which fragments survive, and the way you find out is by paying a laboratory to fail.

**Mitochondrial DNA holds up better, and I will give you both halves of that.** It survives because the genome is short and there are many copies per cell. **And it is maternal-line and shared with relatives**, so it can answer a relationship question. It is not a personal genome.

### So the fact is accurate. Here is my problem with it.

**If the first time you ever hear this is standing in a funeral home, the fact is still true and the moment is still wrong.**

Nothing about the biology changed that week. **It has been the same for the whole of your life.** The only thing that changed is when somebody decided to tell you, and telling a person in the worst seven days of their life is a choice about timing, not a discovery about chemistry.

**I will also say the boring structural thing, because it is the part that is actually mine.** Our kit collects hair and nails from a living person, at a kitchen table, on a day they choose. **There is no funeral-home channel and there could not be one**, because the collection needs somebody who is alive to do it. That is not a virtue. It is a shape.

**And if you are in that week right now, this post is not for you and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Talk to your funeral director.** They will tell you what is and is not possible with the time you have, and they have no reason to sell you anything on this particular question.

One more honest note on sourcing. **Several of the best-ranking pages on this topic are run by companies selling DNA testing of cremated remains**, which is a conflict of interest on the exact question of whether it works. I have used them only where they agree with the forensic material.

**If somebody had told you this on an ordinary Tuesday, years early, with nothing to sell: would you have done anything about it?**
