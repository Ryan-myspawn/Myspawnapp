## #190 [X] The word that breaks the sentence

**Zero-product: no. One mention, at the end.** **40-60 reading: anyone who has read that embryo editing is now precise and quietly filed it as nearly ready.**

In June 2026, a team used base editing on human embryos and the result was reported as a step change in precision.

Not the old molecular scissors that cut the double strand and let the cell repair it however it likes. A single chemical swap. One letter, in place.

The precision claim is real. That is not the interesting part.

**The interesting part is a word in the follow-up coverage: mosaicism.**

In some of those embryos, the intended edit took in some cells and not in others. CNN's July 2026 piece describes it plainly: some of the embryos went on to display what the researchers called "mosaicism," when the intended edit doesn't take effect in all cells.

**Sit with what that does to a sentence you have been reading for a decade. "The edit worked."**

Worked in what? The embryo is not one thing. It is a few dozen cells, and after an edit like this they are no longer all carrying the same genome. One lineage of them will become the body. **Which one, nobody knows at the time.**

**So "it worked" has at least three different numbers behind it**, and they are not interchangeable: the fraction of cells carrying the edit, the fraction of embryos in which anything was edited, and the fraction of embryos in which every single cell carried it. Only the third is the number that would matter if anyone ever did this clinically.

**I went looking for that number. I did not find it.** Four separate pieces of coverage discuss mosaicism in this work and none of them prints a rate. One summary says in terms that it gives no exact figures. *(Peer-reviewed paper: "Base editing reveals an essential role for NANOG in human embryogenesis", Nature, June 2026. Coverage via search index: verify before posting.)*

So I am not going to estimate it, call it low, or call it high. **The absence of the number across four write-ups is itself the most informative thing in the story.**

**Two more facts worth carrying.**

The technique was chosen because the older one was too crude: conventional CRISPR/Cas9 causes too much unintended DNA damage for this kind of question. That is a scientist's reason, not a marketing one.

And the finding itself did not transfer from mice. In earlier mouse work, losing NANOG disrupted both the epiblast and the yolk sac. In the human embryos, it primarily affected the epiblast. **Mouse did not transfer. That sentence is most of the history of this field.**

A separate study, separately labelled: a preprint led by Dieter Egli at Columbia also used base editing on human embryos and, as reported, found off-target edits and mosaic embryos, with the authors concluding that clinical use in IVF remains premature. *(Preprint. Not peer-reviewed. Lower weight, and I am not merging it with the Nature paper.)*

**A concession, because this is otherwise too comfortable.** Mosaicism is not a scandal and not incompetence. It is a known, named, expected problem, and the people who reported it are the same people who did the work. **We are repeating their caution, not discovering it.**

**The transferable tool, which is the only part of this you need to keep.** Next time you read that an edit worked, ask three things: in what fraction of cells, in what fraction of embryos, and in how many embryos was it in every cell. **If you are handed one of those and told it is the success rate, you were handed the flattering one.**

We store DNA records. We make no promise about editing, therapies or descendants, and after a story like this you can probably see why we would not.

## #191 [FACEBOOK] Four doors, and the boldest one has the least oversight

**Zero-product: no. One mention, at the end.** **40-60 reading: the man who has been told a longevity protocol is available to him and has never asked who decides.**

There is a drug on somebody's list that is approved. Just not for the thing they want it for.

That is not a loophole, and it is not rare. In the United States there are four routes to a drug that is not approved for the use in question, they are not interchangeable, and **the one with the boldest name has the least oversight.**

**Route one is the one almost everyone actually travels: off-label prescribing.** The drug is approved. A clinician prescribes it for a different condition, based on medical judgment. This is legal, it is ordinary, and in some fields it is the standard of care. A great deal of what is prescribed to children is not labelled for children at all.

Here is the part worth keeping. **The agency's own position is that it has not assessed the safety or effectiveness of that use.** Approval is a statement about one specific use. Carry it across to a different use and you have carried the brand name, not the evidence.

That is the route a drug like rapamycin travels when it is prescribed for ageing. Its 1999 approval was for transplant immunosuppression.

**Route two is for a drug that is not approved at all.** That is expanded access, under 21 CFR Part 312, Subpart I. The older informal name is compassionate use. It is for patients with a serious or immediately life-threatening condition, outside a clinical trial, and requests run from a single named patient up to larger groups. It involves agency review and an institutional review board.

**Route three is the federal Right to Try Act, signed 30 May 2018.** It creates a parallel path to the same investigational drugs for a narrower group: life-threatening or serious condition, no comparable alternative, unable to join a trial. **What it removes is the FDA authorisation step and the ethics board.**

Now put two and three side by side, which is the only reason I wrote this.

**Reported figure: the FDA authorises more than 99 per cent of expanded access requests, generally within hours or days.** *(Policy literature, read through a search index: verify before posting.)*

**The review was not the barrier.** Right to Try was sold on removing an obstacle that, by that number, was mostly not obstructing.

**An honest counterweight, because the number is not the end of it.** A 99 per cent figure describes requests that were made. It cannot tell you about the request a physician never filed because the paperwork looked impossible. That is a real argument and I am not going to pretend it is not.

**And here is the line that is in none of the brochures, on either route. The manufacturer is under no obligation to provide the drug.** Not under expanded access. Not under Right to Try. **A right to ask is not a right to receive.** Both of these are permission structures. Neither is a supply.

**Route four is the clinical trial**, and it is the only one of the four designed to produce an answer rather than deliver a dose. **The first three can treat you. Only the fourth can tell anybody whether it worked.**

One limit on my own sourcing, since this is about reading carefully: two sources disagreed in front of me about when expanded access was first codified, one saying 1987 and one 2009. I could not resolve it, so I have not used a date.

What is not in any of those four categories is a stored sample. Storing biological material is not a treatment, needs no route, and makes no claim about outcomes. We store a DNA record for $99 a year, billed annually.

**If a treatment you are counting on arrived tomorrow, which of those four doors would it come through, and do you know who is standing in it?**

## #192 [X] Prepaying is lending, and most people do not price it that way

**Zero-product: no. One mention, at the end.** **40-60 reading: anyone who has bought a multi-year plan for anything because the per-year number looked better.**

The moment you pay in advance for something you have not received, you have made a loan.

You are owed performance. If the company fails, that obligation is converted into a dollar claim and ranked against every other claim on the estate. **Secured creditors first. Certain priority claims next. General unsecured creditors after that, which is where almost every consumer sits.** In many liquidations that last group recovers a small fraction, sometimes nothing.

**That is not a scandal. It is the design.** The system exists to divide a shortfall and somebody has to be at the back.

**US law does carve out a consumer protection, and it is narrower than people assume.** Section 507(a)(7) of the Bankruptcy Code gives priority to individual claims arising from deposits made for the purchase, lease or rental of property, or the purchase of services, for personal, family or household use.

**Three limits matter more than the rule's existence.**

**It is capped.** Claims material in a 2025 retail bankruptcy states the figure as **$3,800 for cases filed on or after 1 April 2025**, with **$3,350** for cases filed earlier. *(Taken from a bankruptcy claims notice, not from the statute: verify before posting.)*

**It applies only to what was not delivered.** Money spent on service you already received is not a deposit awaiting performance.

**And it may not cover the instrument you are holding.** A Delaware bankruptcy court held that payment for gift cards does not meet the statutory definition of a deposit, which removed gift card holders from priority treatment. *(One court's decision, reported by a law firm. Not a nationwide rule.)*

So the protection is real and it is measured in thousands, not in whatever you actually prepaid.

**The better question is not what bankruptcy law does afterwards. It is whether your money was ever allowed to be spent in the first place.**

**Some industries are forced to ring-fence it.** New York requires that money received for health club services before a club is fully operating be placed in escrow at a bank or trust company doing business in the state, with an alternative showing of financial responsibility available. New Hampshire requires health club registration with the attorney general and escrow of fees collected before a club begins operating. Missouri requires sellers to place the full amount of payments on a trust-funded preneed funeral contract into trust within sixty days. Georgia requires annual reporting on preneed trust and escrow accounts to the Secretary of State. *(State statutes read through a search index; several results were historical versions or bills rather than current enacted law. Verify the current code.)*

**Notice the pattern, which is more useful than any one citation.** Legislatures ring-fenced prepayments in the industries where consumers were visibly burned: gyms selling memberships before opening, funeral sellers holding money for decades.

**We could not find a state that requires a genetic storage company to escrow prepayments.** If one holds your money in trust, it is doing so by choice. Ask where.

**Four questions, in descending order of how much they tell you.** How far ahead have I paid, which is the size of your exposure and the only part you fully control. Is the money held separately, and by whom, because "held separately" with no institution named is a sentence rather than an arrangement. Who physically holds the thing itself, and are they the same company. **And what is the documented route out: not whether one exists in principle, but what it is called, who signs it, and how long it takes.**

**If a company cannot answer the fourth in writing, that is the answer.**

We charge $99 a year, billed annually, which means your commercial exposure to us is the unused part of one year rather than a decade sitting on our balance sheet. **And run question four on us today and you get the same answer you should accept from nobody: not yet. We have not published a member-facing continuity route, and a structural separation is not the same thing as a written one.**

## #193 [FACEBOOK] A credential is a list, not a gold star

**Zero-product: no. One mention, at the end, and it is an admission rather than a pitch.** **40-60 reading: anyone comparing two providers whose pages both say "accredited" and look otherwise identical.**

Almost everybody checks a laboratory credential the same way. They read the word at the top. Accredited. They stop.

**Here is the version that works, and it takes about ninety seconds.**

**An accreditation is not a verdict on an organisation. It is a list of the specific activities that organisation was assessed for.** The document that holds the list is called a scope of accreditation, and it is usually a separate file from the certificate people frame.

**So the test is simple: find the thing you actually want done, and look for it on the list. If it is not named, it was not assessed.** A laboratory accredited for one method may also, perfectly legally, run tests that nobody assessed. Nothing about that is deceptive. It is just not what the word on the wall implies.

**Three fields to read, in order.**

**The named activities.** Not the lab, not the building, not the company. The products, materials or test types, and the methods used on them.

**The ranges and the points.** Accreditation is often granted for a measurement across a stated range, or at specific points within it. **A result outside the range is outside the accreditation, even though it came from the same instrument in the same room.**

**The date.** A scope expires. **A certificate hanging in a reception area does not announce that it has.**

**The detail that changes how you read the whole document: a scope can be a living thing.** Under the European co-operation for Accreditation's guidance on flexible scopes, EA-4/17, an organisation can be accredited in a way that lets it add or modify activities within defined criteria without a fresh assessment each time. *(Accreditation-body guidance, via search index: verify before posting.)* **Which means the PDF you downloaded last year is a snapshot, not a standing fact.**

**An honest limit, and it is a real one.** Every worked example I could find comes from testing, calibration or medical laboratory accreditation. **Biobanking is accredited under ISO 20387, and I could not find an equivalent published model scope for a biobank.** I am not going to show you a testing-laboratory template and let you assume a biobank's looks the same.

**Why this matters outside a laboratory.** The same move runs through everything: a model law is not an enacted statute; a certification is not an accreditation; a tracking system is not a custody record. **In each case one word is doing the work of a document, and the document says something narrower.**

Now the part that costs us something.

**We have told people for weeks that our storage partner is accredited. We have not published the scopes.** Run the test in this post on us and the answer is: not yet. **Until those documents are published with their named activities, their ranges and their expiry dates, we are asking you to accept a word, which is precisely what this post argues against.**

We store a DNA record for $99 a year, billed annually. That price is published. The certificates are not, and we would rather say so here than wait for someone to ask.

**What is the last credential you accepted without turning the page over?**

## #194 [X] A number that is partly a record of decisions

**ZERO PRODUCT MENTION. No link to us, no price, nothing to click.** This is the per-run zero-product article. **40-60 reading: anyone who has been quoted a survival percentage for anything and assumed it was a property of the patient.**

**Three editorial conditions govern this piece and I am stating them up front: it is not about abortion or any legal viability standard, those words do not appear again, and no country, hospital or clinician is criticised anywhere in it.**

Survival at twenty-two weeks of gestation, pulled from country-level figures in a single published paper: **roughly 0.7 per cent in France. 2.0 per cent in the United Kingdom. 5.1 per cent in the United States. 9.8 per cent in Sweden. 33.1 per cent in Japan.** *(Single-centre paper citing other countries' data. This is the weakest link in the chain and I am labelling it as such rather than leading with it.)*

**That is a forty-fold spread for the same stated gestational age.**

Your first instinct is that somebody is measuring differently, and that is part of it. **But a survey of treatment practice found that differences between hospitals in how often active treatment was offered explained a large share of the variation.**

**Which means a number that sounds like a fact about biology is partly a record of decisions.**

Stronger figures exist and they belong in the same paragraph. **Vermont Oxford Network data from 2022: NICU survival of 21 per cent at 22 weeks, 46 per cent at 23 weeks, 63 per cent at 24 weeks.** *(Network registry data. The strongest numbers here.)* **A published review of care at the limit of viability puts survival at 22 weeks at 1 to 15 per cent and notes that profound disability among survivors is common.** *(Peer-reviewed review.)*

**Now the part that is actually transferable, because three organisations can say "22 weeks" and mean three unrelated things.**

**A statistical definition.** The World Health Organization's lower limit of 22 weeks is described as a statistical definition rather than a clinical recommendation for intervention. **It exists so data can be counted consistently. It is not an instruction to anybody.**

**An operational threshold.** A Swedish centre's guidelines moved to offering predelivery transport to a tertiary hospital at 22 weeks and 0 days, with steroids and resuscitation considered from that point and recommended from 23 weeks and 0 days. **That is a trigger inside one system, not a universal line.**

**A decision framework.** US professional bodies describe shared decision-making below 26 weeks. **That is a process, and a process is not a number at all.**

**One refusal, stated because saying which figures you declined is part of the receipt.** Consumer and commercial pages quote roughly 10 to 30 per cent at 22 weeks where active treatment is offered, attributed to a research network's cohorts. **I could not verify that attribution and I am therefore not using those numbers.**

**And one unresolved conflict of our own, left open rather than tidied.** Our internal research file records one artificial-womb programme as targeting infants "from 22 weeks", citing a June 2026 magazine piece. A sweep this week returned a target population of "23 to 25 weeks". **Both are secondary. Neither is the company's own protocol. I have not reconciled them and I am not going to pick the more convenient one.**

**The transferable instruction, and it is the whole reason to read this.** The next time somebody gives you a survival rate, for anything: **ask what the denominator was, and who decided who went into it.**
