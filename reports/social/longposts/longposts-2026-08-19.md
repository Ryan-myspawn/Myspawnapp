# The oldest baby in the world was frozen when "Jurassic Park" was in theaters

On July 26, 2025, a boy named Thaddeus Daniel Pierce was born in Ohio. Nothing unusual about him, except one line on his paperwork: the embryo he grew from was created in April 1994.

He spent 31 years and 56 days in a storage tank. Guinness World Records certified him as the longest-stored embryo ever carried to a live birth. When his embryo was frozen, Bill Clinton was in his first term and the human genome had not been sequenced.

**The chain of custody is the real story.**

In 1994, a woman named Linda Archerd went through IVF. Her embryos were frozen and kept in storage for three decades. In 2022, at age 62, she placed them for embryo adoption. Her reported words: "I felt all along that these three little hopes, these little embryos, deserved to live just like my daughter did."

Lindsey and Tim Pierce adopted the embryos. Dr. John David Gordon performed the transfer and confirmed it as the longest-frozen embryo to result in a live birth. MIT Technology Review broke the story on July 29, 2025; CNN and Guinness followed within days.

The record Thaddeus broke was itself absurd: the Ridgeway twins, born October 31, 2022, from embryos stored 30 years and 192 days.

**What three decades in a tank did to him: nothing detectable.**

That is the finding that should rearrange how you think about biological time. Vitrified and properly stored, the embryo did not age. The storage interval between 1994 and 2025 cost the cell nothing that standard IVF screening could detect. Reproductive biologists have said for years that there is no known expiration date on properly cryostored embryos. Now the data point is walking around Ohio in a diaper.

**The honest asterisk.**

An embryo in liquid nitrogen and your DNA in an archive are different things, and anyone who blurs that line is selling you something. Thaddeus proves that living reproductive tissue survives deep storage for 31+ years. A preserved DNA sample is not an embryo and cannot become a person with any technology that exists today.

What the two share is the principle: biological information keeps if, and only if, someone deliberately stores it. Time does not pause on its own. In 1994, somebody paid a storage bill nobody thought much about, and in 2025 it became a person's whole existence.

That is the entire logic of genetic preservation, and it is why we built MySpawn: a $99 kit that puts your DNA in accredited biobank storage in New Jersey, at ambient temperature, for decades. Not an embryo. Not a promise of descendants. A sample that exists versus one that never will.

The boy from 1994 is the proof of concept for a very simple idea: the future can only use what somebody saved.

# The "don't die" guy is having doubts. That should worry no one and interest everyone.

On July 29, 2026, Bryan Johnson posted eleven words: "been thinking things over and wonder if i've taken this whole longevity thing too far" (x.com/bryan_johnson/status/2082631490840760622, verbatim).

The man who spent a reported $2 million a year on his own biology, who told Fortune in December 2025 that humans alive today could be "immortal by 2039," publicly wondered if the project went too far.

Context makes the sentence heavier. In early July 2026, Johnson disclosed a diagnosis of autoimmune gastritis, a chronic, currently incurable condition in which the immune system attacks the stomach lining. Per STAT News (July 8, 2026), the diagnosis followed years of persistently low ferritin that supplements never fixed. It was confirmed in May 2026. The most measured man alive was carrying an unmeasured disease the whole time.

**I run a company in the mortality business, so let me say the quiet part.**

Everyone dunking on Johnson this month is missing what his arc actually demonstrates. He is not a fraud. He is the most rigorous self-experimenter of our generation, and rigor is exactly what produced the doubt. He measured everything, optimized everything, and biology still submitted a line item he never ordered.

There are only two honest responses to that. One is to conclude the whole longevity project is vanity, which is lazy. His data on sleep, VO2 max, and biological-age markers is real and useful. The other is to notice the category error: he treated death as an engineering deadline, and deadlines negotiate. Biology does not.

**The stoics had a word for the alternative.**

Not "don't die." Rather: die with your affairs in order, in the largest possible sense. For 10,000 years, the technology for outliving yourself was other people remembering you. Writing extended it. Photography extended it. Genomics extends it again.

Johnson himself seems to sense the pivot. His July 2026 "clone" announcement, covered by NewsNation on July 21, turned out to be cells in a Petri dish for potential organ therapy, not a person. Even the immortalist, pressed by his own diagnosis, reached for a form of biological continuity that is not "me, forever" but "something of me, preserved, useful later." Strip the showmanship and that instinct is ancient. It is the instinct behind wills, letters, family Bibles, and vaults.

Here is my honest position, as someone whose company depends on people thinking about death without panicking: Bryan Johnson's doubt is the most valuable thing he has published. "Immortal by 2039" recruits believers. "I wonder if I took it too far" recruits adults.

The actuarial tables have not moved for him or for you. What you control is not the number. It is what exists when the number comes up: what you wrote down, what you recorded, what you preserved, and who can find it.

The don't-die guy blinked. The rest of us were never not going to die. Plan accordingly.

# The two most viral "artificial womb" stories of the decade are fake. The real one is quieter and much stranger.

You have almost certainly seen one of these two videos.

Video one: "EctoLife," a gleaming facility with 30,000 translucent baby pods. It has circulated since December 2022 and racks up millions of views on every repost. It is not a company. It is a CGI concept film by science communicator Hashem Al-Ghaili, who has said so openly from the start. There is no facility, no pods, no babies.

Video two: the Chinese "pregnancy robot," a humanoid that supposedly gestates a human child for about 100,000 yuan, roughly $14,000, attributed to a "Kaiwa Technology" and promised for 2026. It went viral in August 2025 and resurfaces every few months, most recently through large commentary channels in summer 2026. Live Science investigated and found no verifiable product, no verifiable company operations, and AI-generated imagery. Snopes reached the same conclusion. It is a hoax wearing a lab coat.

**Two fabrications, tens of millions of views. Now here is what is actually happening, with paperwork.**

In 2017, surgeons at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia published a peer-reviewed study in Nature Communications: eight extremely premature lambs lived up to four weeks inside a fluid-filled "biobag" that oxygenated their blood through the umbilical cord while their lungs kept developing. The lambs grew wool inside the bag.

That research became Vitara Biomedical, a Philadelphia company that has raised about $125 million, including a $50 million round in November 2024 led by Sands Capital with participation from Google Ventures and Khosla Ventures. Its system, EXTEND, targets one narrow, deadly problem: infants born at around 22 to 28 weeks, before their lungs work. This is not conception-to-birth gestation. It is a bridge for babies already born too soon.

The regulatory record is public. On September 19, 2023, an FDA advisory committee met specifically to discuss how artificial womb technology could move from lambs to human trials. In June 2026, Fast Company reported that Vitara is submitting its application for first-in-human use, after more than 350 animal tests (company-reported figure; the FDA has announced no decision, so label this reported, not approved).

**Why the fakes matter more than they should.**

The fake versions and the real version tell opposite stories, and the fake ones are optimized to win.

EctoLife and the pregnancy robot sell total ectogenesis: machines replacing mothers, states growing citizens, babies on demand. Horror plus inevitability equals shares. The real project is a rescue device for the roughly 1 in 10 US births that arrive preterm, moving at the speed of institutional review boards.

The gap between those stories is where public opinion is being formed right now. People who believe the robot is real are making up their minds about reproductive technology based on a fabrication. When the genuinely hard questions arrive, and they will, the loudest priors will belong to people radicalized by CGI.

The fertility establishment, for its part, is already governing the future in advance: the American Society for Reproductive Medicine published a formal ethics opinion in 2026 on in vitro gametogenesis, lab-made eggs and sperm, stating plainly that the technology is not feasible in humans and has no human safety data. Referees writing rules before kickoff tells you how seriously they take the next decade.

**A field guide, free to steal:**

1. Real breakthroughs name a journal and a date. Nature Communications, 2017. Fake ones name a render farm.
2. Real breakthroughs are narrow. "22 to 28 weeks, lungs only" is how actual medicine talks. "Grow a baby for $14,000" is how engagement talks.
3. Real breakthroughs come with regulators attached. If there is no FDA docket, no trial registry, no institution with a name and a lawyer, you are watching content, not science.

The future of birth is not a chrome pod. So far, it is a plastic bag full of warm fluid in Philadelphia, 350 animal tests deep, waiting on the FDA. Quieter than the hoax. Considerably more real.

# Your DNA outlived the company that tested it. A practical guide to what 15 million people learned the hard way.

If you or anyone in your family ever spat in a tube for 23andMe, this is the timeline nobody printed on the box.

October 2023: attackers used recycled passwords from other websites (the technique is called credential stuffing) to access accounts, and ultimately scraped profile data tied to about 6.9 million people. The intrusion ran for roughly five months before it was caught.

March 23, 2025: 23andMe filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Its genetic database, about 15 million customers, became an asset for sale like office chairs.

June 2025: Regeneron Pharmaceuticals won the auction at $256 million. Then the court reopened bidding, and founder Anne Wojcicki's nonprofit, TTAM Research Institute, took it back for $305 million. The sale closed July 14, 2025.

Along the way: more than two dozen state attorneys general sued over the transfer of genetic data in bankruptcy, a coalition of 42 states settled cybersecurity claims for $18 million, and a bankruptcy judge signed off on a data-breach settlement of $46.75 million for affected customers.

Nobody's DNA changed hands because they clicked "I agree" to that specific future. It happened because a company's balance sheet failed, and the law treated 15 million genomes like inventory.

**The lesson is not "never trust anyone." It is "ask better questions."**

We are in the first generation that leaves behind digital and biological archives managed by companies. Companies die: acquisitions, bankruptcies, pivots. So the single most important feature of any DNA service is not the price or the app. It is the answer to: what happens to my sample and my data when you fail?

Questions worth asking any DNA company, ours included:

1. Is the physical sample stored at an independently accredited facility, and accredited by whom? (Look for ISO and CAP accreditations and FDA registration of the biorepository itself.)
2. Can I order my sample destroyed, and how fast?
3. If the company is sold or liquidated, what legally binds the successor?
4. Is my data sold or shared for research by default, or only by opt-in?
5. Does federal law protect me? Partially: GINA, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008, bars health insurers and employers from using your genetic data against you, but it does not cover life, disability, or long-term care insurance. Know the gap.

Full disclosure, since this is our lane: our company, MySpawn, stores DNA at GenVault, an independent, FDA-registered biorepository in New Jersey accredited to ISO 9001 and ISO 20387 and by the College of American Pathologists, and we publish our custody answers rather than waiting to be asked. We built the company in the shadow of the 23andMe story on purpose. But whether or not you ever use us, use the checklist.

One more thing worth doing this week: if you have an old account with any testing company, log in, download your raw data file for your own records, and review your deletion and research-consent settings. It takes ten minutes, and it is the difference between being a customer and being an asset.

Our parents kept birth certificates in a fireproof box. Genetic records deserve at least that much intention.

What would you want done with your DNA if the company holding it disappeared tomorrow? Tell me in the comments, genuinely curious what people expect versus what the fine print says.

# The gamete bottleneck is cracking, slowly. An honest 2026 status report on lab-made eggs and sperm, from someone with a conflict of interest.

Disclosure up front: I founded a DNA-preservation startup (storage only, think time capsule with your genome in it), so I watch this field with obvious bias. Correct for it accordingly. What follows is the state of in vitro gametogenesis as of August 2026, with receipts, because most coverage is either hype or dismissal.

**What is peer-reviewed:**

The strongest published human result is from Mitalipov's lab at OHSU (Nature Communications, September 2025). They transplanted skin-cell nuclei into enucleated donor eggs and triggered "mitomeiosis," an induced chromosome-halving, producing 82 fertilizable human oocytes ([OHSU announcement](https://news.ohsu.edu/2025/09/30/ohsu-researchers-develop-functional-eggs-from-human-skin-cells)). The catch, which the authors stated themselves: about 9 percent of resulting embryos reached blastocyst, chromosome errors were everywhere, and none were euploid. Mitalipov's own clinical estimate is roughly a decade, contingent on solving chromosome segregation ([STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2025/09/30/fertility-pioneer-shoukhrat-mitalipov-research-update/)).

Mouse IVG, for calibration, is solved: pups from stem-cell-derived eggs, grown to fertile adulthood, published years ago by the Hayashi and Saitou groups. Humans stall at meiosis. A February 2026 finding gives the concrete reason: lab-grown human germ cells fail a centromere-tethered genome reorganization that natural germ cells perform. Named blocker, unsolved.

**What is company announcement, not science (yet):**

- Conception Biosciences (Altman-backed) [announced](https://www.conception.bio/science-and-updates/the-first-early-human-eggs-from-stem-cells) in July 2026 the first early human oocytes (primary oocytes, immature, not fertilizable) grown from stem cells in lab ovaroids. Plausible per outside scientists, zero peer review so far.
- Paterna Biosciences [announced](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/paterna-biosciences-announces-world-first-achievement-lab-grown-human-sperm) in May 2026 lab-grown human sperm that formed embryos. If true, a century-old problem solved. No paper, no replication, no regulator has seen it. I would bet money the eventual peer-reviewed version is messier.

**What is actually in clinics:** Gameto's Fertilo, iPSC-derived support cells that mature a patient's own eggs outside the body. First baby December 2024, now in US Phase 3 ([NCT06858111](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06858111)), primary completion estimated May 2027. Not IVG (the egg is the patient's), but it is the first iPSC-derived reproductive therapy the FDA has allowed into Phase 3, which tells you regulators will engage with this category.

**The governance tell:** the ASRM published a formal [ethics opinion on IVG](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/ethical-considerations-of-in-vitro-gametogenesis-an-ethics-committee-opinion-asrm-2026/) in 2026 confirming no human feasibility and no safety data, while laying ethical ground rules anyway. Institutions write rulebooks early when they expect to need them.

**What we don't know yet, honestly:** whether human meiosis can be completed in vitro at all; whether the Conception and Paterna claims survive peer review; whether IVG-derived embryos can ever be shown safe enough for a first attempt; and how consent works for gametes made from cells of someone deceased. Anyone stating timelines as facts is selling something. The estimates quoted by careful people run 10 to 20 years, and estimates about unsolved problems have no error bars worth trusting.

Why I care commercially, stated plainly since this sub deserves candor: if gametes can someday be made from ordinary cells, then preserved cells and genomes from people who died childless, or who just waited, stop being purely commemorative. Nobody, including my company, can promise that outcome, and we sell storage on exactly that no-promises basis. But the option value of "the material exists" versus "it does not" is the entire asymmetry, and it only runs one direction in time.

Question for this community, because it is the part I genuinely wrestle with: if IVG matures in 15 years, what does consent look like for the person whose preserved cells are used, and who should hold that veto after death? The estate? A named successor? Nobody?
