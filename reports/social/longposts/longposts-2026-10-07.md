# #122 [X] The tank did not fail first. The alarm did.
**Lane: science-explainer. ZERO PRODUCT. ~640 words.**

On **4 March 2018**, a cryopreservation tank at Pacific Fertility Center in San Francisco lost its liquid nitrogen and **roughly 3,500 frozen eggs and embryos thawed**.

On **10 June 2021**, a California jury awarded **$14.975 million** to five patients. It found the tank's manufacturer, **Chart Inc.**, liable on every claim *(jury verdict, reported; not a peer-reviewed finding)*.

**Here is the part that should change how you think about long-term storage.**

The jury did not simply find that a tank broke. It found **manufacturing and design defects**, and it found that Chart had **negligently failed to recall or retrofit the tank's controller** despite knowing for years that the controller model was prone to malfunction: **reading inaccurate liquid nitrogen levels and temperatures, and issuing constant false alarms.**

**Read that last phrase again. Constant false alarms.**

**The failure mode in long-term biological storage is almost never the dramatic one.** It is not a power cut or a fire. It is a monitoring system that cries wolf until the people responsible stop believing it, and then one day the wolf is real and the alarm sounds exactly like the previous four hundred.

**Three things worth knowing about this category of failure.**

**One: the damage is instantaneous and total.** A document can be water-damaged and partly recovered. A thawed embryo is gone. There is no degraded-but-readable state.

**Two: it is a supply-chain problem, not a clinic problem.** The jury put the liability on the tank maker, not on the lab. **Every facility using that controller had the same defect and none of them could see it.** If you are evaluating any custodian, the question "who made your equipment and what is its service history" is not a pedantic one.

**Three: the litigation did not end with the verdict.** Hundreds of other people have sued the clinic and Chart Industries over the same incident, and a separate wave followed the University Hospitals fertility clinic tank failure in Ohio in the same period *(reported; case outcomes vary and are not summarised here)*.

**The honest general lesson, stated without selling anything.**

**Cryogenic storage buys you capabilities that ambient storage does not, and it buys them by introducing a continuous dependency.** Liquid nitrogen has to be replenished. Controllers have to work. Alarms have to be believed. **The storage is only as good as the least interesting maintenance task in the building.**

**That is not an argument against cryopreservation**, which does things nothing else can do. It is an argument for asking a specific question of anybody holding biological material for you, in writing: **what is your monitoring system, who services it, and what happened the last time it alarmed?**

**A custodian who can answer that in three sentences has thought about it. One who cannot has not.**

**What we do not know:** whether the controller defect has since been remediated across the installed base, and how many facilities still run the affected model. **Neither figure was found in this sweep and neither is asserted here.**

# #123 [FACEBOOK] HIPAA does not say how long your doctor keeps your records. Most people assume it does.
**Lane: practical legacy guide. ~690 words. Ends with a question.**

Here is a thing almost everybody gets wrong, including people who work in healthcare.

**HIPAA does not set a retention period for your medical records.**

It sets one for HIPAA's own paperwork: privacy policies, procedures and training records have to be kept **six years**. For the actual record of your care, HIPAA is silent. **Retention is set by state law and by payer rules, and it varies enormously.**

**Two real examples, both current.**

**Florida** requires records be kept at least **two years after the practitioner's death, the termination of practice, or relocation.**

**Massachusetts** requires a retiring physician or their successor to keep patient records **seven years from the date of the final visit.**

**Two years and seven years are both "the law." They are just different laws.**

**What happens when the practice closes**

This is where people lose things without ever being told they lost them.

When a practice shuts or a doctor retires, the records do not evaporate and they do not automatically follow you. **Responsibility passes to a designated custodian named in the closure plan.** That is usually one of three parties: a successor provider who takes the practice over, a qualified third-party records custodian operating under a Business Associate Agreement, or the retiring doctor personally.

**The custodian has to secure the records, respond to access requests, honor valid authorizations, and retain or destroy on the governing schedule.** The practice is also supposed to notify patients how to obtain copies or transfer records.

**That notification is a letter.** Letters go to the address the practice had. **If you moved, you did not get it.**

**The part that matters for anybody doing family history**

A family medical history is built out of other people's records. **Your father's cardiology notes from 2009 are the most useful document you will ever not have.** And the retention clock on them started running from his last visit, under the law of the state the practice was in, with a two-to-seven-year spread depending on where that was.

**By the time most people think to ask, the window has closed.** Not through anybody's fault. Through a schedule nobody told them about.

**What to actually do, this month**

1. **List every practice that holds significant records for you or for a parent.** GP, any specialist, any hospital stay.
2. **For each one, ask in writing:** what is your retention period for my records, and who becomes the custodian if this practice closes?
3. **Request copies of anything genuinely load-bearing now**, rather than when you need it. You have a right of access and it costs little.
4. **Store the copies somewhere that is not a single device.**
5. **Do this for a living parent while they can authorise it.** After death, access runs through the estate and it gets considerably harder.

**None of this is about us.** We store a biological record, at $99 a year, billed annually, with samples held at an independently accredited third-party facility. **A DNA record is not a medical record and does not replace one.** They answer different questions and you want both.

**The question worth sitting with: if your doctor's practice closed next year, do you know who would be holding your file, and for how long?**

# #124 [X] One state made donor records permanent. The privacy crowd should be arguing about this and isn't.
**Lane: contrarian discourse take. Punches at an idea. ~610 words.**

In **June 2022**, Colorado Governor Jared Polis signed **SB 22-224**, the Donor-Conceived Persons and Families of Donor-Conceived Persons Protection Act. **It is the first US law abolishing anonymous gamete donation.**

The dates matter and get muddled constantly. **The act took effect 1 July 2024. It applies to donations made on or after 1 January 2025. It is not retroactive.**

What it does:

- **A donor-conceived person may learn the name of their genetic parent at 18.**
- **Identifying information stays private until then.**
- **Access to the donor's personal and family medical history.**
- **A worldwide limit of 25 families from any one donor**, with no cap on children per family.
- **Gamete banks and clinics must hold a Colorado licence and periodically ask donors to update medical history and contact details.**
- **Donor records are subject to permanent retention.**

**That last line is the one nobody is arguing about, and it is the most interesting thing in the statute.**

**The contrarian point, and it is about an idea rather than a person.**

Almost every genetic-privacy argument of the last five years treats indefinite retention as the villain. Delete it. Destroy it. Set a clock. **The default assumption is that holding identity-linked biological records forever is a harm to be minimised.**

**Colorado just legislated the opposite, for an identifiable group, on the grounds that the harm ran the other way.** Donor-conceived people were losing access to their own origin and their own medical history **because records were allowed to disappear.** For them, deletion was not protection. Deletion was the injury.

**Both positions are correct and they are correct about different people.** The 23andMe bankruptcy taught one lesson about records outliving the company that made promises about them. Colorado teaches the opposite lesson about records vanishing before the person they describe is old enough to ask.

**So the honest framing is not "how fast should data be destroyed."** It is: **who decides, when, and does the person the record is about get a vote before the clock runs out?**

**A retention period set by a company's convenience is not a privacy policy. A destruction schedule the subject cannot override is not protection either.** Colorado's answer is a specific, auditable one: permanent retention, access gated by age, licensing to make it enforceable.

**Agree or not, it is an actual design.** Most of the discourse is a mood.

**And the design has a hard edge that is worth stating plainly, because it is where the law will actually bite.**

**The act is not retroactive, and it applies to donations made on or after 1 January 2025.** That creates a dated boundary running straight through a population of living people. **A person conceived from a donation made on 2 January 2025 acquires, at 18, a statutory right to their genetic parent's name. A person conceived from a donation made on 30 December 2024 does not, and nothing in this act gives it to them.** Same state, same clinics, same need, **separated by a date on a consent form they never saw.**

**That is not a criticism of the drafting.** Non-retroactivity is standard and the alternative, reopening promises of anonymity already made to past donors, is its own serious problem. **It is a reminder that every retention rule has a cohort it was written too late for**, and the people in that cohort are the ones who go looking and find that the record either was never kept or cannot be opened.

**What we do not know yet:** how the 25-family worldwide cap is enforced against banks operating outside Colorado, and what happens to a record if a licensed bank fails. **Neither answer was found in this sweep and we are not inventing one.**

They were not 2026 stories. **Both URLs contain the date 2024-10-03.**

The copyright line at the foot of a web page is not a publication date. **It renders from the server clock at the moment you load the page.** It will say 2027 in January and the story underneath it will still be from 2023.

**We had already written that rule down, two days earlier, after it caught us once.** What was new last night is where it appeared: **not in the page furniture at the bottom, but in the title**, which is the one field a reader treats as a label rather than as decoration.

**A fact has a half-life, and nothing on the page tells you how much of it is left.**

The 2023 breach is still true as history and false as news, and the page helpfully supplied a year that described nothing except the moment you arrived.

**Three things I now do, and recommend to anybody who publishes anything.**

1. **Get the date from the byline, the dateline or the URL slug. Never from page chrome, and from today, never from a search result title either.**
2. **Past tense is not a defence.** If a reader would walk away believing something current, say what is current.
3. **When you find your own error, say so in the same place you said the thing.** A correction nobody sees is a correction for your own comfort.

We run a company whose entire proposition is keeping something intact for decades. **If we cannot keep a sentence accurate for 48 hours, that is worth knowing about us, which is why this is a post rather than a quiet edit.**

# #126 [X] Your bank does not need your permission to empty your safe deposit box. It needs you to go quiet.
**Lane: practical guide / trust and custody. ~580 words.**

Most people think of a safe deposit box as something that gets opened when they die.

**The more common ending is that it gets opened because nobody heard from you.**

It is called escheatment, and it is the process by which property whose owner has gone silent passes into state custody. **Boxes are included.** The trigger is not death. **The trigger is a dormancy period, usually measured from when you stop paying the rent or the bank stops being able to reach you.**

**The clock, by state:**

- **New Jersey: 5 years**
- **Virginia: 5 years after the lease or rental period expired**
- **Washington: 5 years after the owner stops paying rent**
- **Florida: 3 years of unpaid rental fees**
- **Arizona: 3 years after the owner stops paying rent**
- **California: 3 years**

**Three years is not a long time to not notice a direct debit failing.**

**What happens next is the part nobody pictures.**

The holder is obliged to report and remit the contents to the state. **The state then appraises tangible property and, if the owner does not claim it, sells it at public auction.** The categories that turn up in state auction listings are exactly what you would expect: collectible coins and currency, jewellery, pocket watches, stamps, silver bars, sports cards.

**And here is the detail that makes this a custody story rather than a money story.**

**If your property is sold before you file a claim, the cash proceeds stay in your account with the state and can be claimed indefinitely.** The money is kept forever. **The object is not.**

**Read that as a sentence about the whole category of long-term storage: the system preserves the thing it can value and disposes of the thing it cannot.** Your grandfather's watch becomes a line item. The line item is immortal. The watch is at an auction in another state.

**What to do, and it takes ten minutes.**

1. **Confirm who the box is rented to and whether that person still gets the mail.** A box rented in a parent's name with a lapsed address is the standard way this starts.
2. **Make sure the rent is paid from an account somebody monitors**, not from one that only ever goes out.
3. **Tell one other person the box exists and where.** Most escheated boxes were not forgotten by everyone. They were known to exactly one person.
4. **Check your state's unclaimed property database by name**, including for deceased relatives. It is free and the search takes a minute.

**The general principle, which is why we care about it: no custodian protects you from your own silence.** A bank, a lab, a state program, us. **Every one of them has a rule for what happens when nobody answers, and that rule was not written with you specifically in mind.**

**Ask any custodian what their version of the dormancy clock is. If the answer takes more than two sentences, write it down.**
