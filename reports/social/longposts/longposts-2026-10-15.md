## #142 [X] Trust and custody

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. `ISO 20387` appears in 45 files across this fleet and every single one of them is our own boilerplate. **No asset we have ever produced says what the standard actually requires. That is the article.**

**Primary audience:** Men 25-55

**Trend / hook used:** No news peg, declared rather than dressed up. The hook is an audit of our own marketing language, prompted by nothing but the count above.

---

**We put "ISO 20387 certified" in 45 of our own files before ever explaining what it means. Here is what it covers, and what it does not.**

ISO 20387:2018 is titled *Biotechnology: Biobanking: General requirements for biobanking*. It was published in 2018 and it is the first international standard written specifically for biobanks rather than borrowed from clinical laboratories.

**What it actually governs is three words: competence, impartiality, consistent operation.** The standard sets general requirements for the competence, impartiality and consistent operation of biobanks, so that collections of biological material and their associated data are fit for research and development. *(ISO 20387:2018 scope, via search index: verify before posting.)*

**It is a lifecycle standard, not a freezer standard.** Its requirements run across acquisition and reception, storage, distribution and transport, with traceability and documentation linking each sample to its data and its provenance. It also covers the quality management system, the organisational structure, the facilities, the equipment, and the training and assessment of staff. *(Accreditation-body and assessor-training material, UKAS and BBMRI-ERIC, via search index.)*

**The impartiality clause is the one nobody quotes and the one worth quoting.** Biobanking shall be structured and managed so as to safeguard impartiality. In practice, assessors look for a written declaration of impartiality and a regular, documented exercise identifying and minimising risks to it. *(Assessor training material, 2020, via search index.)* That is a governance requirement, not a temperature requirement, and it exists because a biobank that is also a customer of its own collection has an interest in what it finds.

**Now the limit, and we are publishing it against ourselves.** The standard explicitly does not apply to biological material intended for food production or for therapeutic use. *(Scope exclusions, ISO 20387:2018, via search index.)* So the accreditation says a facility is competently and traceably keeping a sample. **It does not say that anything downstream can be done with it.**

**We could not verify a stronger version of that point and we are not going to assert it.** The question we wanted answered was whether ISO 20387 assesses the analytical validity of anything done to a sample later. Nothing we read said so, and one source lists validation and verification of methods among the standard's topics, which cuts the other way. **Unresolved, and stated as unresolved.**

**What this is worth to a buyer, in one line: an accreditation is a claim about process, audited by a third party, and that is genuinely more than a company's word.** It is not a claim about outcomes in thirty years, because no standard can audit a future.

**The three questions it makes possible.** Who holds the accreditation, by name, and is it the facility or the brand selling you the service? What is the scope on the certificate, since accreditation is always scoped and the scope is a document you can ask for? And when was the last assessment?

**Ours, stated plainly so you can apply the same test to us: the accreditations are GenVault's, not MySpawn's.** GenVault is the independent New Jersey facility that holds our members' samples at ambient temperature, and it is ISO 9001 and ISO 20387 certified, CAP accredited and FDA-registered. **MySpawn is the company that pays them and that sells you the $99 a year, billed annually, with the first 1,000 members locking the founding rate.** Two companies, two sets of obligations, and you should want to know which one is making which promise.

**If a storage company will not name its facility, you are being asked to audit a brand.**

## #143 [X] Contrarian discourse take: ZERO PRODUCT

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. `re-identif` returns two prior hits fleet-wide and `reidentif` returns zero; neither prior hit is about language models. **No asset here has argued that de-identification is a process claim rather than a property of a file.**

**Primary audience:** Men 25-55

**Trend / hook used:** Two 2026 preprints, one from August and one from September, on what large language models can pull back out of clinical text that has already been scrubbed. This punches at an idea and at a word. It names no institution as a wrongdoer.

---

**"De-identified" is not a property of a file. It is a claim about a process that somebody ran, at a date, with the tools available then.**

That distinction used to be pedantic. It stopped being pedantic when the tools got better at reading than the tools that did the scrubbing.

**An August 2026 preprint is titled, in full: "Institution-Specific LLM Prompting Recovers PHI That De-identification Systems and Their Gold Standards Both Miss."** *(arXiv 2608.17051. Preprint, NOT peer-reviewed. Read via search index; arxiv.org failed DNS from here: verify before posting.)*

Read the title slowly, because the second half is the part that matters. Not just the de-identification systems missed protected health information. **The gold standards missed it too.** The gold standard is the human-annotated reference set that everyone measures their scrubber against. If the reference is incomplete, every accuracy figure computed against it is flattering by construction.

**A September 2026 preprint on locally governed de-identification notes, in passing, that text left in de-identified clinical notes enables re-identification of dates.** *(arXiv 2609.10049. Preprint, NOT peer-reviewed. Via search index.)* Dates sound harmless. Dates are a join key. A date of admission plus a region plus a diagnosis is frequently enough to narrow a population to one person.

**And the state of the art at scrubbing is not solved either.** A July 2026 paper in *BMJ Health and Care Informatics* benchmarked large language models for de-identifying electronic health record notes; the best-performing model reached an F1 of 0.8172. *(Peer-reviewed. PubMed 42481207. Via search index.)* An F1 of 0.82 is a respectable research result and a terrible guarantee. It means a meaningful fraction of identifiers in the test set were still handled wrongly.

**Here is the part that generalises past clinical notes.**

Genetic data is the worst possible candidate for the word "anonymous," because a genome is simultaneously the identifier and the payload. You cannot strip the identifying part out of a sequence and keep the useful part, the way you can delete a name from a form. **And re-identification does not require anybody to break anything: genetics combined with dates, locations, admissions or diagnoses, the quasi-identifiers, is frequently enough on its own.** *(Analysis read via search index.)*

**The honest statement, which is less dramatic than the discourse and more useful: a file that was adequately de-identified in 2016 was adequately de-identified in 2016.** Nothing about it changed. What changed is the cost of the attack.

So the question to ask about any dataset built from people is not "is it anonymised." It is **"when was it anonymised, against which threat model, and who is responsible for re-checking that assumption."** Almost nobody can answer the third one, and the third one is the only one that protects anyone.

**What we do not know.** We have not read either preprint at source. Preprints are not peer-reviewed and some do not survive it. We do not know whether the August result reproduces outside the institution it was run at, which is precisely what the word "institution-specific" in its own title warns about. **We are not claiming any named biobank or hospital has been re-identified. We are claiming a word is doing work it cannot do.**

## #144 [FACEBOOK] Story-driven: ZERO PRODUCT

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. `Voyager` and `Golden Record` both return ZERO fleet-wide. **Framing note for the file: this is not the retired letter-to-the-future lane. The Golden Record contains images and sounds and no letter, which is exactly the video-message framing the rules point at instead.**

**Primary audience:** Men 25-55, reading warm at 35-60

**Trend / hook used:** No news peg. A 1977 object, used as the one honest benchmark for how long anybody has ever seriously tried to make a message last.

---

In 1977 a committee chaired by Carl Sagan had to answer a question most of us never bother with: if you only get one shot at leaving something, what do you actually put on it?

They had twelve inches of gold-plated copper.

**They chose 115 images, nature sounds, greetings in 55 languages, and music.** Sources differ slightly on the image count: most say 115 and Wikipedia's own contents page says 116, so take it as 115 to 116. *(Via search index: verify before posting.)*

**No letter.** Think about that for a second. Given one object to carry humanity, the people who thought hardest about it chose pictures, voices and music. Not prose.

**The engineering is the part that gets me.** The disc plays at 16 and two-thirds revolutions per minute, half the speed of a normal LP, because slowing it down bought more running time on the same surface. Testing and materials analysis projected that the record would stay legible for **at least a billion years**, which is to say far longer than the spacecraft carrying it. *(Via search index.)*

**And then they did something I have never been able to stop thinking about. They built in a clock.**

On the cover there is a tiny electroplated sample of uranium-238, with a radioactivity NASA lists at about 0.00026 microcuries. Uranium-238 has a half-life of roughly 4.47 billion years; two NASA pages give it as 4.468 and 4.51 billion, so round it to about 4.5. **Anyone who finds the record can measure how much uranium is left against its decay products and work out, without being told, how long it has been drifting.** *(Via search index.)*

They could not know who would find it. So instead of explaining when it was sent, they shipped the measurement.

**The cover also carries a pulsar map and a time unit built from physics rather than culture.** Written around the cover in binary is the correct playing speed, 3.6 seconds per rotation, expressed in units of 0.70 billionths of a second, the period associated with a fundamental transition of the hydrogen atom. **Not seconds. Not minutes. Hydrogen.** Because seconds are a human convention and hydrogen is everywhere.

**Here is the honest footnote, and it belongs in the story rather than under it.** The uranium clock only works if the finder can make assumptions about the sample's original purity and how well it was preserved. It is an elegant idea with a dependency. *(Via search index.)*

So what did they actually get right?

They assumed the reader would share no language, no calendar, no units, and no context. **And having assumed all of that, they still sent something.**

Most of us do the opposite. We assume our kids will know what the box in the attic is for, that someone will recognise the faces, that the hard drive will open. We assume context survives. **Context is the first thing to go.**

I am not going to pretend a family has a billion-year problem. We have a sixty-year problem, which is harder in one specific way: nobody is going to run materials analysis on your photographs. But the 1977 committee's actual method transfers almost perfectly, and it is three decisions.

**Pick a small number of things rather than everything.** Choose formats that do not need your explanation to be understood. And include whatever lets a stranger work out when and who, because the person who finds it will not have you to ask.

A gold disc is drifting through interstellar space with a built-in timestamp, playable by someone who has never heard of Earth.

Most of us cannot find the password to our own photo backup.

**What would you put on your twelve inches? And be honest: would it be a letter, or would it be your voice?**

## #145 [FACEBOOK] Practical legacy guide

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. `seed phrase`, `Shamir` and `chain of title` return ZERO fleet-wide; `crypto` returns four hits and none is about succession. **Distinguished from #138 on 13 October, which was about whether a DNA result is admissible evidence in an inheritance claim. This is about an asset where the legal claim and the physical access come apart completely.**

**Primary audience:** Men 25-55

**Trend / hook used:** No news peg, declared. The hook is a structural fact about self-custody that most estate plans written in the last decade do not address.

---

There is a category of asset where your heirs can win in court and still get nothing.

**An estate plan has two halves and people only write one of them.** The legal claim is who is entitled. The operational access is who can actually open it. For almost everything you own, those two travel together: a court order and a death certificate will eventually move a bank balance, a house or a car. **For a self-custodied crypto wallet, they do not travel together at all.**

**What a regulated exchange needs, and this part is ordinary.** Most regulated venues ask for a certified death certificate plus letters testamentary or letters of administration, and often a probate court order, before releasing a deceased holder's balance. The heir usually has to pass fresh identity verification on the receiving account. Coinbase runs a dedicated estate form; Kraken and Binance work by email submission. Simple cases are described as taking four to six weeks, and large balances or identity mismatches as stretching to three months or more. *(Law-firm and vendor commentary, not official policy pages. Via search index: verify against each platform's own support documentation before relying on any of it.)*

**What a self-custody wallet needs is a secret, and no process produces one.** There is no provider to compel, no form to file and no judge who can order a seed phrase into existence. If the phrase is gone, the asset is intact, visible on a public ledger, and unreachable. **Forever is not a figure of speech here.**

Two legal instruments are worth knowing by name. **The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, RUFADAA, which nearly every state has enacted, governs whether a fiduciary may access digital accounts at all**, and a provider may satisfy it by handing over exports or records rather than live credentials. Separately, **the IRS treats inherited cryptocurrency as property, with a basis stepped up to fair market value at the date of death.** *(Both via secondary commentary, not read at the Uniform Law Commission or at irs.gov. Via search index.)*

Read those two together and the shape is clear. **RUFADAA can get your executor permission. It cannot get them the key.**

**So the plan has three parts, and they belong in three different places.**

**One: an inventory.** A plain list of what exists and where, with no secrets in it. This is the part almost nobody writes, and it is the part that costs nothing. An executor who does not know a wallet exists will not look for it, and nothing on a public ledger announces itself to a family.

**Two: the authority.** A will or trust that actually names the asset class, so your executor has standing to act rather than discovering a category the document never anticipated.

**Three: the access.** Deliberately separate from the other two, because the document your executor files in a courthouse is a document that becomes visible. A seed phrase in a will is a seed phrase in a public record.

**The failure mode worth naming, because it is the one that catches careful people.** You set this up once, you move the funds two years later, and the sealed envelope now protects an empty wallet. **A plan that is never rehearsed is a plan you are guessing about.** The test is not whether you wrote it down. It is whether somebody other than you could follow it, from the inventory, without calling you.

I will put our own card on the table, because it is the same structure. **MySpawn stores a physical DNA record from hair and nail clippings at GenVault in New Jersey, an independent ISO 9001 and ISO 20387 certified, CAP accredited, FDA-registered facility, at ambient temperature, for $99 a year, billed annually**, with the first 1,000 members locking the founding rate. **And the honest version of our own answer is identical to the advice above: your family needs to know the record exists, and they need to know who to contact.** A stored thing nobody knows about is functionally the same as a lost seed phrase. We do not do cloning, gene editing or anything that creates a descendant; we keep a record, and a record only works if somebody can find it.

**What is the one asset your family would not even know to look for?**

## #146 [X] Science explainer

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. `trehalose` and `lyophil` return ZERO fleet-wide. **Deliberately distinguished from #49 (7 September) and #134 (11 October), which both run on the ensilication and FFPE sequencing paper. Different chemistry, different paper, and #134's binding limits on that paper are not touched here.**

**Primary audience:** Men 25-55

**Trend / hook used:** A January 2026 bioRxiv preprint on vitrified trehalose, read against a 2013 peer-reviewed four-year test that reaches an uncomfortable conclusion. No news peg; the newer item is a preprint and is labelled as one.

---

**The sugar everybody assumes is behind dry-state DNA storage performed worst in the only four-year room-temperature test we could find. We sell ambient storage, and we are publishing that anyway.**

Start with why anyone reaches for trehalose. It is the disaccharide associated with organisms that survive drying out, and in a dried sample it forms a glass. The glass immobilises what is inside it. The idea is intuitive, elegant and genuinely real.

**Now the four-year test.** Ivanova and Kuzmina, *Molecular Ecology Resources*, 2013, compared dry-state stabilisers protecting insect DNA over four years. **Trehalose ranked second at 56 degrees Celsius, which is the accelerated-ageing condition. At room temperature it did not offer good protection: 29 percent of DNA intact, against 2 percent for unprotected DNA, and 98 percent for the best performer, a commercial product.** *(Peer-reviewed. DOI 10.1111/1755-0998.12134. Via search index: verify before posting.)*

Hold those three numbers next to each other, because the ranking inverts depending on which one you look at. **Against doing nothing, trehalose is a fourteen-fold improvement. Against the best available chemistry, it loses by 69 percentage points.** Both sentences are true about the same experiment, and which one a company quotes tells you something.

**The newest work is more modest still, and it is a preprint.** A January 2026 bioRxiv study tested DNA, RNA and enzymes in vitrified trehalose under two drying methods and found **DNA stable for eight weeks, with no significant difference in integrity between drying methods or with trehalose present**, at 10 percent humidity. *(bioRxiv, 16 January 2026. PREPRINT, NOT PEER-REVIEWED. Via search index.)* Eight weeks. **A null result at eight weeks is not evidence of anything at eight years, in either direction, and nobody should use it as such.**

A review of dried plasmid storage reports the same shape from the other side: trehalose gives initial protection from degradation and light-induced damage, and **degradation was observed after eight weeks.** And a cell freeze-drying study puts it bluntly: trehalose cannot prevent DNA damage during room-temperature storage, though the damage is less than without it. *(Both via search index.)*

**Here is the synthesis, stated no stronger than the evidence allows: trehalose reduces dry-state DNA damage at room temperature. It does not eliminate it. Results vary with the drying method, the sample type and the duration, and the longest room-temperature test we located ran four years and did not favour it.**

**Why we are publishing a result that is awkward for us.** We sell ambient-temperature storage. The temptation in this category is to find the paper that flatters your model and stop reading. **We did that once: on 11 October we published a correction because an earlier post of ours had called a preprint peer-reviewed and dated its peer review four months early, in an article that used the paper in our own favour.** The fix is not to be quieter. It is to publish the number that cuts against us, with its date on it.

**What we could not find, and we looked.** No peer-reviewed multi-year, room-temperature trehalose-and-DNA study beyond the 2013 four-year comparison. **That is a thin literature for a mechanism this widely assumed, and the gap is the finding.** We also found nothing in this search on the anhydrobiosis mechanism itself, so nothing here rests on it.

**The question this actually equips you to ask a storage provider, in one sentence: which stabilisation chemistry, at what humidity, validated over what interval, and by whom?**

If the answer is a word rather than an interval, you have the marketing and not the method. **We think that question should be asked of us too, and the facility that can answer it is GenVault, not us: the accreditations are theirs.**
