## ARTICLE #82 [FACEBOOK]
### In a Bankruptcy There Is a Referee. In an Acquisition There Is Not.

**Freshness check:** No longpost has covered acquisition, assignment clauses or the bankruptcy treatment of personal data. #68 covered a state genetic-privacy statute, #73 lab retention policy, #77 platform settings. None touched what happens when a company is **bought**.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** A company in the memory-keeping category filed Chapter 11 in May 2024, an investor bought the assets, and it is operating and expanding in 2026.
**Word count: 615**

Everyone plans for the wrong failure.

Ask what worries you about a company holding your photos, recordings or DNA, and you will describe a shutdown. The site goes dark, nobody answers, the files are gone.

Shutdowns happen, and they are **visible**. You notice. If you are quick, you export.

**The commoner outcome is quieter. The company does not stop. It changes hands.**

**The clause that decides it takes ten seconds to find.** Open the terms of any service you have given something to and search for the word **assign**. You will usually find a line saying the agreement may transfer if the company is acquired, merged or sold. Try **merger**. Try **bankruptcy**. That one sentence is doing more work than the entire privacy policy above it.

**Here is the part that surprised me.** US law takes your data **more** seriously when a company goes bankrupt than when it is simply bought.

**Section 363(b)(1) of the Bankruptcy Code** restricts a debtor from selling personally identifiable information outside the ordinary course of business, where the company had disclosed a policy prohibiting transfer of that information to non-affiliated persons and that policy was in effect when the case began.

**Section 332** creates a **consumer privacy ombudsman**: appointed by the court when a hearing is required, able to appear and be heard at the sale hearing.

**And there is a catch sitting inside it.** The protection is triggered by **what the privacy policy promised.** A company that promised never to transfer your data faces the ombudsman. A company whose policy always reserved the right has, in effect, pre-cleared the sale.

**The machinery is real and it has worked.**

**Toysmart, 2000.** An online toy retailer failed in the dot-com crash and tried to sell customer data, including information about children, despite a privacy policy saying it would **never** do so. The **Federal Trade Commission** brought a deceptive-practices claim.

**RadioShack, 2015.** In Chapter 11 it proposed selling customer personal information. The **FTC warned the court-appointed consumer privacy ombudsman** that the sale would breach the FTC Act's prohibition on unfair or deceptive practices, and **state attorneys general objected**.

**And the big one.** After **23andMe** filed for Chapter 11, a court-approved sale process ran. **Regeneron** was first declared the successful bidder under an asset purchase agreement worth **$256 million**. Bidding reopened, Regeneron declined to go higher, and the assets went to **TTAM Research Institute**, a California nonprofit led by co-founder Anne Wojcicki, for **$305 million**. The sale completed on **14 July 2025**. **Several state attorneys general objected**, arguing customers hold property or privacy rights in genetic data under their own statutes; a group reported to include **California, Kentucky, Tennessee, Texas and Utah** remained opposed. The company and buyer argued no separate opt-in was needed because the acquirer would honor the existing privacy commitments.

Think what you like about the result. **Notice the mechanism.** All of that happened in public, in a court, with regulators in the room, **because it was a bankruptcy.**

**In an ordinary acquisition there is no court, no ombudsman, no sale hearing, and no structured window for anyone to object.** There is a clause and an email about updated terms.

**Our own answer, including the incomplete part.** We store genetic records with an independently accredited third-party biorepository in New Jersey rather than in our own office. **That answers what happens if MySpawn stops. It only half answers who could end up holding this, because a third party can be sold too.** We have not published our continuity document yet. I would rather tell you that than let you find it out.

So: have you ever searched a service's terms for the word assign, and what did it say?

## ARTICLE #83 [X] · ZERO PRODUCT
### Two Numbers That Are Both One Percent

**Freshness check:** No longpost has covered testosterone, cohort effects or the population-versus-individual distinction. #74 covered a detox market, #79 supplement evidence grading; neither touched this measure or this statistical idea.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** "Men's testosterone is falling about one percent a year" is circulating again, and two different one-percent findings are being collapsed into one.
**Word count: 554**

You have heard that men's testosterone is falling about one percent a year.

That is true. There is also a **second** finding, also about one percent, that is also true. They are different claims and almost everybody quoting the first is thinking of the second.

**Number one: individual decline.** As a man ages, total testosterone tends to drift down gradually, on the order of about one percent a year from somewhere around his thirties. That is aging, happening inside one person, over time. *(Peer-reviewed longitudinal literature; the exact rate varies by study and by which measure you take.)*

**Number two: cohort decline. This is the interesting one.**

In **2007**, **Travison and colleagues** published *"A Population-Level Decline in Serum Testosterone Levels in American Men"* in the ***Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism***. It used the **Massachusetts Male Aging Study**, with three data-collection waves across seventeen years: **1987 to 1989, 1995 to 1997, and 2002 to 2004.**

They found an **age-matched decline of roughly 1.2% per year.**

**Age-matched.** Meaning: a **fifty-year-old man measured in 1988 had a higher level than a comparable fifty-year-old man measured in 1996.** Same age. Later decade. Lower number. *(Peer-reviewed.)*

That is not men getting older. **That is fifty-year-olds getting different.**

**The obvious explanations were tested and did not account for it.** The age-matched declines remained essentially unchanged after controlling for **chronic illness, general health, medications, smoking, body mass index, employment and marital status.** A companion paper the same year examined the relative contributions of aging, health and lifestyle specifically. *(Peer-reviewed.)*

Later work looked at national data: **Nyante and colleagues** examined trends in sex hormone concentrations in US males from **1988 to 1991 and 1999 to 2004**. Read it rather than trusting anyone's summary, including mine. *(Peer-reviewed.)*

**So why does the distinction matter?**

If someone quotes you the **first** number, the implied story is *you are running out of time*. There is usually something for sale at the end of that sentence.

If they mean the **second**, the implied story is that *something changed between those decades that nobody has pinned down*. That is a research problem, not a personal countdown, and **nothing you buy this week addresses it.**

**What would the second one even mean?** Nobody knows, and that is the honest state of it. The candidates people raise are environmental exposures, measurement and assay changes across decades, shifts in what a "healthy" comparison group looks like, and factors nobody has named yet. **The researchers tested the obvious ones and the gap survived.** An unexplained finding that survives its obvious explanations is more interesting than a explained one, and it is also exactly the kind of finding that gets flattened into a headline.

Both numbers are real. **They point in opposite directions, and the one being sold to you is usually the wrong one.**

**The reusable version, which costs about ninety seconds:** when a number is quoted at you, ask three things. **Is this about one person over time, or a population across decades? What was controlled for? And what is being sold at the end of the sentence?**

I am not a doctor and this is not advice about your body. If you want your own number, the test is cheap and a physician can interpret it. **A statistic about a population has never told anybody anything about themselves.**

## ARTICLE #84 [FACEBOOK]
### The Shoebox Beats Everything I Sell at One Thing

**Freshness check:** No longpost has argued for a physical, non-digital archive, and none has conceded a category advantage to the alternative. #75 was a file-naming guide, which is the opposite move.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** Four well-known companies in the memory-keeping category shut down, left the category or went quiet in roughly two years, and one survived by being bought.
**Word count: 521**

Most people's archive is a shoebox in a cupboard.

I sell an alternative, so you would expect me to be rude about it. I am not going to be, because the box has one enormous advantage over everything in this industry and it is worth saying out loud.

**Nobody has to stay in business for it to work.**

No company. No subscription. No login, no password reset, no migration email. If every business involved disappeared tomorrow, the box is still a box. **Almost nothing digital has that property**, and this year made the point for me: in roughly two years, four of the best-known companies built to keep people's memories shut down, left the category or went quiet. One of them filed **Chapter 11 in May 2024** and survived only because an investor bought the assets.

**Now the flaw, which is just as large.**

**A box has no date and no name on it.**

It opens when somebody happens to find it. That is usually while clearing a house, which is the worst possible moment, and frequently the wrong person. Everything in the box was for someone. The box has no idea who.

**So the two properties are: survives without anyone, and arrives at nobody in particular.** Every company in this space, mine included, is trying to buy the second one without losing the first.

**And here is where I have to be straight with you.** We sell a date and a named person. That requires us to still exist. **A box does not.**

Our partial answer is that the samples sit with an independently accredited third-party biorepository in New Jersey rather than in our office, so we are not the only thing standing between you and the thing. **That is real and it is not complete**, and the rest belongs in a continuity document we have not published yet.

**Three things you can do this week that cost nothing:**

1. **Keep the box.** Genuinely. It is doing more than you think.
2. **Put a name and a date on it.** Not a label saying "photos". A label saying who it is for and when you would want it opened. That is a ten-second fix for the flaw.
3. **Photograph the back of the prints**, not just the front. The names and dates penciled on the back are the part that gets lost first, and once the last person who recognised the faces is gone, an unlabelled photograph is decoration.

4. **Ask the harder version of the question of anybody selling you a date**, including us. Not only "what happens if you shut down", but **"who is allowed to buy you"**. Most consumer terms let an agreement transfer on acquisition, merger or sale, and that clause decides who ends up holding your things. It is one search away in any terms of service: the word is **assign**.

The free letter tool we run is the closest thing we make to the box: sealed, dated, no account, nothing to lapse. Everything else we sell is us promising to be here.

What is in your box, and does anyone else know it exists?

### Somebody Finally Studied What Childfree People Actually Say

**Freshness check:** No longpost has cited discourse research, and none has covered the childfree lane through a peer-reviewed method. #11 used the fertility-gap framing and is on correction-watch; #63 used a family-history collection rate. Neither touched what people in this conversation actually say.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** A 2026 peer-reviewed analysis of #childfree TikTok, newly surfaced.
**Word count: 528**

Almost everything written about people who do not want children is written **about** them, using survey answers, by people who are not them.

A 2026 paper did something different. **Beringer and King**, in the ***Journal of Community & Applied Social Psychology*** (doi 10.1002/casp.70314), analyzed **88 TikTok videos and 440 associated comments** posted under **#childfree** and **#childfreebychoice**, transcribed them, and ran a **thematic discourse analysis**. *(Peer-reviewed. Publication month not established: date it before you quote it.)*

They found people occupying **two positions**.

**The responsible childfree individual**, constructing the choice as **moral and socially conscientious**.

**The enlightened childfree individual**, constructing it as **rational and autonomous**.

And the paper's broader finding: TikTok is a space where **pronatalist narratives are both reinforced and contested**, with **stigma persisting alongside emerging counter-discourses** that legitimize the childfree identity.

**Here is what struck me, and it is not in the paper's conclusions, it is in its structure.**

**Both positions are defensive.**

One says *I am being responsible*. The other says *I have thought about this properly*. Both are arguments. Both are answers to a question that was asked first by somebody else.

**Nobody has to construct a position called "the responsible parent."** That one comes free. You are not required to justify it on camera to an audience of strangers, and if you did, the comments would not be a debate.

That asymmetry is the finding, whether or not the authors framed it that way. **The stigma is not that people disagree with you. It is that you are the one who has to make a case.**

**The paper also found the other half**, and it matters: **counter-discourses are emerging** that legitimize the position rather than defend it. That is a different thing from stigma management. A defense answers a charge. A counter-discourse changes what the charge is worth. If you want to know where this conversation goes next, that distinction is probably the whole map.

**And a caution on the method, since I am praising it.** 88 videos and 440 comments is a **qualitative** dataset, not a representative sample of anybody. Discourse analysis tells you **what positions exist and how they are built**. It does not tell you how many people hold them, and nobody should use this paper to claim a proportion.

**Why I am posting this.** We work in a category next door to this argument and we sell to people on both sides of it, so our standing rule is simple: **never mock anybody for having children or for not having them.** Both are customers and both are adults.

But we have also been sloppy. **Two separate methods challenges sit in our own research file against framings we have used**, including one arguing that the "fertility gap" is largely an artifact of survey design, because US instruments ask about an **ideal** family size for a hypothetical family rather than what a respondent **expects** for themselves.

This paper is the first thing I have read in the lane that measured the conversation instead of interviewing it. **That is worth more than another poll.**

If you write about this subject, read it before you write the next thing. I am going to.

**SOURCES (reply thread):**
1. Beringer and King, *Journal of Community & Applied Social Psychology*, 2026, "#Childfree: Exploring How TikTok Users Discursively Construct the Choice to Remain Childfree in a Pronatalist Context", doi 10.1002/casp.70314: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/casp.70314 **Peer-reviewed.** *(via search index: verify, and establish the publication month, before posting)*
2. Method as reported: 88 TikTok videos and 440 associated comments under #childfree and #childfreebychoice; thematic discourse analysis.
3. The survey-design critique referenced: Rackin et al., *Population and Development Review*, on whether respondents distinguish ideal, desired and expected fertility. **Peer-reviewed.**
4. **The observation that both positions are defensive is mine, not the authors'.** Do not attribute it to them.

## ARTICLE #86 [REDDIT r/Entrepreneur]
### We corrected ourselves five times in seven days. The pattern was an ordering problem, not a carelessness problem.

**Freshness check:** #66 was an ops essay about a stale-item checklist; #76 about channel timing; #81 about a positioning error found in a competitor sweep. **This is about the sequence in which our own gates fire**, which none of them touched, and it ends with a proposed process change rather than a lesson.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** Five factual corrections in one week, all caught before publication.
**Word count: 582**

Disclosure: I work on a small paid product. No link, nothing to buy here, and this post is about a process failure rather than the product. Mods, remove if it reads wrong.

Last week we corrected ourselves five times in seven days. **Every one was caught before anything was published.** That sounds like a functioning system, and it mostly is, but the pattern underneath it is worth describing because I do not think it is unique to us.

**What the five were:**

1. An approved internal wording about a scientific field, **written on 14 September and already false when we wrote it down.**
2. A line saying no platform lets your nominated contact access anything. **True of three platforms, false of Google**, whose inactive-account tool sends a real download link.
3. A framing describing four companies in a category as having shut down, pivoted or paused. **One of them filed Chapter 11 and came back**, so listing it among failures was misleading by omission.
4. A date on a clinical trial, **off by one day**, because I had taken an aggregator's publication date instead of the event date.
5. A statistical claim I had made **too clean**: I said a well-known one-percent figure described a population trend rather than individuals. Both a population trend and an individual trend exist and both are near one percent.

**The pattern is not "we were careless."** Four of the five were caught by our own research step. The pattern is that **the research gate keeps firing after the writing gate.**

We research, we write, and then the next day a different piece of research touches the same subject and the first thing it does is disprove yesterday's sentence. That is the correct order for **finding** errors and the wrong order for **avoiding** them, because by then the thing is written, logged and scheduled.

**Two things I actually think are true here, neither flattering.**

**One: the errors cluster in a specific shape.** They are all claims about the *state of a field*, not claims about a single checkable fact. "Nobody has done X yet." "None of them do Y." **A sentence about a pattern carries a date whether or not you print one**, and it expires quietly. A sentence about a single fact does not.

**Two: our fastest, most confident output is the most dangerous.** Every one of the five was a line that sounded clean. Rhetorically tidy sentences are exactly the ones that escape checking, because they feel finished.

**The change I am making, and I would be interested if anyone has tried it:** a **24-hour hold on any asset whose spine is a pattern rather than a fact.** Not everything, because that would halt the operation. Just the ones asserting that a category behaves a certain way. **Three of the five would have been caught by that alone.**

**What I do not know yet:** whether a 24-hour hold actually catches errors or just delays them, and whether the discipline survives a week when something is genuinely urgent. I have not run it long enough to say.

If you produce a lot of content quickly, my honest suggestion is to go and count your near-misses rather than your mistakes. **The interesting number is not how many errors you published. It is how many you caught, and at which step.** Ours is five in seven days, all at the research step, and that tells me the research step is carrying weight the writing step should be sharing.
