## #92 [FACEBOOK] Can you get DNA from a hairbrush? Mostly no, and the reason is stranger than you think.

**Freshness check:** No longpost has ever covered hair biology or artifact testing. #75 covered scanning prints, #84 the shoebox; neither touches DNA recovery.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend / hook used:** A 2026 peer-reviewed paper that changed part of the answer, found by checking our own files.

Somebody in your family has a hairbrush, and somewhere in the back of your head is the thought that it would do, if it came to that.

It mostly would not. And the reason is the best piece of biology I have learned this year.

**A hair is not a growing thing. It is a column of cells that were ordered to destroy themselves.**

At the base of each follicle, keratinocytes divide, fill with keratin, and then run a programmed self-destruct called **cornification**. It is not ordinary cell suicide. Three things happen: the machinery that lets the cell respond to its surroundings is degraded, **the mitochondria are removed so energy production stops**, and **the nucleus is dismantled and its DNA destroyed** (review: *Cell Death by Cornification*, **Biochimica et Biophysica Acta, 2013**: peer-reviewed).

There is even a dedicated enzyme for the demolition. **DNase1L2 is the keratinocyte-specific endonuclease that removes nuclear DNA from hair and nails** (**peer-reviewed**, [PMC3185332](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3185332/)). A 2018 *British Journal of Dermatology* paper describes the sequence with a title that does the work for me: **"The walking dead: sequential nuclear and organelle destruction during hair development"** ([Jones, 2018](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/bjd.16148): peer-reviewed).

**So the strand you can see is the finished product of cells that deliberately deleted the thing DNA lives in.** That is why the rule was always: the root has the full profile, the shaft does not. Pull, do not trim.

**Here is the part that changed, and I want to be careful with it.**

In **2026**, *Genome Biology* published **"Rootless hair as a reliable source of forensic genetic information"** (DOI 10.1186/s13059-026-03981-8). **Peer-reviewed.** Sequencing built for very small, very broken fragments can recover **nuclear** DNA from a rootless shaft. The DNA was in there the whole time. **The old method could not read it.**

**Epistemic label, said plainly: we have not read that paper.** The journal and PubMed are both unreachable from our network, so **no figure from it appears anywhere in this post**. We published an article on 24 September that graded this result as "emerging research" rather than peer-reviewed. **That was one notch too low and we corrected it the same day.**

**None of which makes a hairbrush a backup.** It is a research and forensic technique, not necessarily what a commercial lab runs when you post them a brush. And a brush is not a sample: it is hair from several people, of unknown age, handled by everyone in the house.

**The prices, all vendor-advertised rather than measured.** Artifact testing starts around **$240 USD** at one US lab and around **$310 AUD** at an Australian one. Difficult samples run to **$2,000 or more, per sample**. On something like a licked envelope, **one lab reports roughly a 10 percent success rate.** You pay for the attempt either way, **and the test consumes the sample.**

**And the bit nobody finds out until afterwards:** of the major consumer genealogy databases, **only FamilyTreeDNA and MyHeritage accept uploaded third-party profiles.** So the prize for a successful one-in-ten attempt is a profile searchable against two databases rather than the whole field. **Confirm that with the database before you spend anything, because upload policies change quietly.**

While somebody is alive, none of this applies. It is a cheek swab or a hair pulled with the root still on it, and twenty minutes.

Is there a hairbrush in your family you have privately been counting on?

## #93 [X] 25 percent of American 40-year-olds have never married. Check your time capsule.

**Freshness check:** No longpost covers unlock-date selection or uses demographic base rates this way. #68 and #82 are custody; #84 is the shoebox.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend / hook used:** 2026-current US demographic data applied to a product decision almost nobody checks.

If you have set a message to unlock "on her wedding day," there is a one-in-four chance it never opens.

**Pew Research Center found that 25 percent of 40-year-olds in the United States had never been married.** 28 percent of men. 22 percent of women. **In 1980 the figure was 6 percent.** (Pew Research Center, **published June 2023, using a 2021 data year**: both dates stated because both matter.)

Graduation is not safer. **Six-year bachelor's completion at four-year institutions runs roughly 60 to 64 percent** depending on which cohort you count (NCES). **So a graduation trigger has a real chance of never firing either.**

**And here is the nasty part: it does not fail loudly.** No error, no notification, no alert to the family. The message simply sits there, for decades, not playing.

**Three ways event triggers break, in order of likelihood.**

**1. The event never arrives.** See above.

**2. The event arrives twice.** Second marriages exist. Whoever administers your capsule then has to decide which wedding you meant, and they will be guessing.

**3. The event arrives at the worst possible moment.** A wedding week has no attention to spare. A graduation is usually a move, a job hunt and a money cliff at once. **A recorded message lands hardest when the recipient has room for it**, and event dates are exactly the days with no room.

**Now the fact that surprised me most, and it is free to check: "18" is not one date.** The age of majority is 18 in most US states, **19 in Alabama and Nebraska, and 21 in Mississippi.** Tie an unlock to legal adulthood rather than to a birthday and the date moves depending on where the recipient lives when it comes.

**One correction to the obvious conclusion, because the obvious conclusion is wrong.** Marriage is not vanishing. **There were 2,082,354 marriages registered in the US in 2024, a rate of 6.3 per 1,000, the highest count since 2019** (CDC/NCHS). Marriage is **moving later and becoming less universal.** Only the second half affects your date.

**Small honesty note on a number I will not give you.** Two sources report the 2024 median age at first marriage as roughly **30.2 and 28.6**, and as **30.8 and 28.8**. Both call it a Census record high. **I am not picking one.** The direction is later; the precise figure is in dispute and I would rather say so.

**What to do instead: a ladder, not a date.**

Three or four ages, spread across twenty years. One near legal adulthood. One in the mid-twenties. **One around thirty, which is the one almost nobody sets and most people later wish they had.** Then two exceptions that are not ages: **on confirmation of your death**, which is the only event guaranteed to fire exactly once, and **on request, any time.**

That last one matters more than it sounds. **A capsule that can only open on a schedule is a capsule that cannot answer a question.**

And say the reason out loud in the first fifteen seconds of the recording. If you picked 30 because your own father died at 32, they will not know that unless you tell them. **No scheduling system does that part for you.**

## #94 [X] Two research firms put the same market's 2024 size 72 percent apart. ZERO PRODUCT.

**Freshness check:** No longpost has audited a market-size figure. #90 audits dates; this audits a denominator, which is a different failure.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend / hook used:** Yesterday's own competitor sweep, which produced nine incompatible figures in one afternoon.

I went looking for the size of the market I work in. I got nine answers.

**For the year 2024: a year that has already finished: two research firms report the size of the "digital legacy" market as $13.07 billion and $22.46 billion.** Precedence Research gives the first. Zion Market Research gives the second. **That is a 72 percent gap on a measurement, not a forecast.**

**The forecasts then diverge further, which is at least what forecasts are for.** Precedence: **$55.75 billion by 2034, 15.61 percent CAGR.** Zion: **$78.98 billion by 2034, 13.40 percent CAGR.** Custom Market Insights: **about $77.96 billion by 2034 at 12.97 percent**, distributed via **GlobeNewswire on 4 June 2025: a paid press-release wire, not journalism.** Adjacent scopes go higher still: one outlet reports **$118 billion** for "AI afterlife avatars", and another reports **$60.99 billion** for "digital immortality", **also via a paid release wire.**

**Being fair, because the fairness is the whole point.** These are not nine lies. **"Digital legacy" can include password managers and estate administration. "Digital afterlife" can mean griefbots and voice memorials. "Digital immortality" is narrower again.** Scope differences are real and some of this spread is legitimate. **No single firm here is necessarily wrong.**

**The problem is that the category has no agreed denominator and publishes as though it does.**

**Here is how one of these numbers travels.** A newsletter item reports **$22 billion in 2024 growing to $80 billion by 2034** and credits "Hospice": a hospice-trade publication. Those figures are within rounding of Zion's pair. **So the likely chain is: a research firm's paid report, into a trade article, into an AI newsletter, arriving in your feed as this month's news.** **That chain is a hypothesis, not a finding. I could not open the newsletter to confirm it and I am telling you that rather than asserting it.**

**And now the part I am obliged to include.**

Our own internal competitor file has carried **"roughly $5 billion"** for this category since 10 September. It was never traced to a primary source. Its scope was never written down. **Yesterday it became the smallest number in a pile of nine, and we retired it.** **No asset of ours will quote a market-size figure for this category again, including that one.**

**What to do with any market-size number, in three questions.**

**Who paid for the report?** Market-research firms sell reports. The press release is the advertisement. That is a normal business, not a scandal, and it is still not journalism.

**What is inside the denominator?** If the answer includes both a password manager and an AI avatar of your dead father, the number is measuring a category that does not exist as a market.

**Which year was counted?** A finished year should not be in dispute. When it is, you are looking at modeling, not counting.

**None of this means the underlying thing is not growing.** It obviously is. **It means that anyone who opens with "this is an $80 billion market" has told you nothing you can check**, and now you know exactly how to ask.

## #95 [REDDIT r/SideProject] I overstated my own mistake, in the direction that made me look better for admitting it.

**Freshness check:** #66 and #86 are error-counting ops essays about process ordering. **This is about the SIZE of an error being wrong**, which neither covers, and the failure runs in the flattering direction rather than the embarrassing one.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend / hook used:** Something that happened yesterday afternoon while writing something else.

Founder here, building a DNA-storage product. Disclosing that up front. No links to us in this post.

Yesterday I published an article about whether you can recover DNA from a hairbrush after someone dies. Four hours later, while drafting something unrelated, I found a 2026 peer-reviewed paper that changes part of the answer: **and it had been sitting in my own production notes since 30 August.**

So I wrote it up as a failure. My notes said, roughly: *the article states flatly that a shed hair shaft is a mitochondrial-only sample, and a paper in our own archive contradicts it.*

Then, before committing, I opened the article instead of remembering it.

**It does not state that flatly.** It carries an explicit caveat saying "never is too strong". It names the active research. Its FAQ hedges with "by standard methods" twice. **The body was careful.**

The actual defect was much smaller: **the article graded a peer-reviewed journal paper as "emerging research", which is one notch too low, and its one-line summary was flat over a careful body.** A labelling error, not a factual reversal. I fixed it in five places and added no figures, because I still have not been able to open the paper itself.

**Here is the part worth posting about.**

I got my own mistake wrong **in the direction that made me look more rigorous for catching it.** A big self-caught error is a better story than a small one. It reads as humility. It is the most comfortable kind of error to make and the hardest to notice, because nothing inside you objects.

I have a checklist that says open the source rather than recall it. **I had never applied it to my own published work.** Our claims check verifies external sources. It has no step for verifying a claim about something we wrote ourselves.

**What I changed, and what I did not.**

Changed: every claim about our own article, rewritten to the narrower true version, in four files.

Not changed: I have proposed adding "open your own asset" to the checklist and I have not adopted it unilaterally, because a checklist that grows every time someone has a bad afternoon stops being used.

**Why I think the error runs in this direction structurally, rather than because I am vain.**

The incentive to write up a correction is roughly proportional to the size of the thing you caught. A small labeling error is a housekeeping note nobody reads. A big self-caught contradiction is a post. **So the moment you decide a correction is worth publishing, you have already applied upward pressure to your own estimate of it**, and nothing downstream checks that estimate, because everyone downstream is busy being impressed that you caught anything.

There is also an asymmetry in who pays. **An understated error costs the reader, who keeps acting on something wrong.** An overstated one costs almost nobody anything immediate: the fix still gets made, and the writer gets credit. **That asymmetry is exactly why the overstatement is easy to miss. It has no victim standing in front of you.**

**What I do not know yet:** whether this generalizes. It is one instance. It is possible I am now overcorrecting in the other direction and will spend next week understating real errors. **The only honest thing I can say is that self-reported error sizes are unreliable in a specific direction, and that direction is flattering.**

If you run a project where you publish corrections publicly: do you check the size of the error, or only that you caught it? I had a process for the second and nothing for the first.

**[REDDIT: what we don't know yet]** This is a single instance from one afternoon, not a pattern with a sample size. I have not tested whether the proposed checklist step would have caught it prospectively, and I am wary of adding a rule on the strength of one example.

## #96 [FACEBOOK] No human has ever been gestated in an artificial womb. Here is exactly how far it has got. ZERO PRODUCT.

**Freshness check:** No longpost covers artificial wombs. The lane exists on the science watchlist and has never been written up.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend / hook used:** The category is discussed constantly and almost always at the wrong distance from the evidence.

Every few months a picture goes round of rows of babies in glass tanks. It is a render. The company behind the most famous one has never built anything.

**Here is the real work, and it is better than the render.**

In **2017**, a team at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia published **"An extra-uterine system to physiologically support the extreme premature lamb"** in ***Nature Communications*** ([ncomms15112](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms15112): **peer-reviewed**).

**Eight lambs**, delivered by caesarean at about **110 days of gestation**, which is developmentally equivalent to a **human infant at 23 to 24 weeks**: the edge of viability. They were placed in a fluid-filled **"Biobag"** and supported for **up to four weeks.**

**The engineering choice that matters is the one nobody mentions.** There is no pump. **The fetal heart drives blood through the umbilical cord into an external oxygenator**, which stands in for the placenta. Adding a pump risks overloading a fetal heart that is not built for it. **The system was designed around that constraint**, and the first version was partly assembled from ECMO components the hospital no longer needed.

Growth, breathing patterns and movement in the bag tracked a normal lamb's.

**Now the honest status, as of September 2026.**

**No human has been gestated, fully or partially, in an artificial womb.** The technology is not commercially available. **No human trial has begun.** The CHOP team behind EXTEND has been in discussions with the FDA about a first-in-human trial; **discussions are not an approval and not a trial.**

**And the distinction that gets collapsed constantly.**

**Partial ectogenesis** means rescuing an already-developing fetus at around 22 to 24 weeks. That is what the lamb work is about. It is plausibly a few years to a decade from human trials.

**Full ectogenesis**: conception to birth outside a body: is something else entirely. **No mammal has ever been gestated start to finish outside a uterus.** The early and late ends of pregnancy are different engineering problems, and nothing in the lamb data touches either of them.

**The specific reason full ectogenesis is not the same project.** The Biobag replaces one job the placenta does: **gas exchange.** The placenta also runs endocrine functions and an immune interface between two genetically different bodies, and it does all of it while being built, in place, by the pregnancy itself. **Supporting a fetus that already has a functioning placenta-equivalent circulation is an engineering problem. Starting one from nothing is a biology problem, and nobody has solved it in any mammal.**

There is a second constraint that the renders never show: **the system has to stay sterile for weeks.** Infection, not oxygenation, is the thing that kills extremely premature infants in conventional care, and a closed fluid-filled circuit with an umbilical connection is a demanding thing to keep clean. That is one of several reasons why "we supported lambs for four weeks" and "we are ready for people" are separated by nine years and counting.

**Why the difference matters beyond pedantry:** every time coverage blurs the two, the people who need the real thing get an argument about science fiction instead of a conversation about extremely premature infants, which is a live clinical problem with a real mortality rate right now.

One more label, because this is a lane full of fakes: **the "artificial womb facility" concept video and the "pregnancy robot" story are not real products.** They are a concept render and a misreported claim respectively. **If a source cites either as an existing service, stop reading that source.**

What is actually happening is eight lambs, one paper, nine years, and a regulator still being talked to. **That is slower than the renders and far more impressive.**

Does the distinction between rescuing a 23-week infant and growing a person from scratch land differently for you than the headlines suggest?
