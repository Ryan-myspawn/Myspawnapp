## #177 [FACEBOOK] The $1,250,000 line nobody mentions when they tell you to add a beneficiary

**Freshness check:** The controlling rule took effect 1 April 2024 and the newest item is an American Bar Association report dated September/October 2026 on a revised uniform act. **Declared adjacency: longpost #128, 9 October, used a beneficiary form as its subject through Kennedy v. DuPont and ERISA. This article shares neither the case, the statute, nor the hook: it is about deposit insurance arithmetic and children, and #128's own stated limits explicitly excluded POD accounts.**
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55, plus 40-60 planners.
**Trend-hook used:** None. Standing search intent.

Adding a payable-on-death beneficiary to your bank account takes about five minutes and it genuinely works. The money skips probate. The person named brings a death certificate and the bank pays them.

Then there is the part nobody mentions.

**Since 1 April 2024, the FDIC calculates deposit insurance on trust accounts, which includes POD and "in trust for" accounts, as $250,000 multiplied by the number of eligible beneficiaries, capped at $1,250,000 per owner per bank.** Name six people and you are still capped at five tranches.

**Two details inside that rule bite.** Only **primary** beneficiaries count toward the multiplier. Contingent beneficiaries do not. And the cap applies across **all** your trust-type accounts at that one institution, not per account.

**There is a group who lost coverage on that date and mostly do not know it.** Before the change, revocable trust accounts were not capped at five beneficiaries, so a trust naming eight or ten people could be insured well above $1.25 million at a single bank. Those depositors are not fully insured now. The FDIC expected most people, who hold less than $1.25 million at one bank, to see no change at all. **If you are above it, you are the exception the rule was written around.**

**Then there are children.**

Banks will not hand a lump sum to a minor. The consistent position across bank and law-firm guidance is that funds are not distributed directly to anyone under 18, and that where a minor is the named beneficiary a court generally appoints a guardian or conservator to hold the money. **So the account dodges probate and lands in a guardianship proceeding. You have swapped one court for another.**

The usual fix is to name a custodian under your state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, or a trustee, rather than the child alone.

**And here the dates are genuinely unsettled, so I am going to give you the disagreement rather than a clean answer.** UTMA custodianship ends at an age set by state law: **most commonly 21, with the real range across states running roughly 18 to 25**, and some states letting whoever opened the account choose later. California is widely cited as defaulting to 18 while permitting 25. **Published state-by-state tables contradict each other on individual states, including two tables from the same publisher**, which is why I am not reproducing one.

**An American Bar Association article dated September/October 2026 reports a new Uniform Transfers to Minors Act raising the custodial termination age to 25.** That is a model law. It governs nothing until your state adopts it, and I could not confirm which states have. Direction of travel, not your answer.

**What survives all that uncertainty is simple: a child named directly on a POD form gets the whole balance in cash the day they hit whatever age applies, with no conditions attached.** If that bothers you, the form is the wrong tool and you want a trust.

**The free thing worth doing this week:** go through every account, confirm there is a named primary beneficiary AND a named contingent beneficiary, and check the percentages add to 100. Banks want numbers, not instructions. "Split between my kids" is not a percentage.

## #178 [X] "We sequence 99% of the genome" answers a question nobody asked

**Freshness check:** The controlling source is a peer-reviewed professional-society statement published April 2024, used here for the first time at article length. **`polygenic` and `Orchid` both return ZERO in the longpost log.** Declared adjacency: `ACMG` appears in four log lines, always as a warning cited in passing, never as the subject.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend-hook used:** None.

There is a number in the embryo-screening market that sounds like it settles the argument. One company says it sequences more than 99% of an embryo's genome where existing options read under 1%. That is their own marketing claim.

**Assume it is completely true. It still does not do what it looks like it does.**

Reading a genome is a measurement problem. Predicting a trait from it is a modelling problem. **Solving the first one superbly does not touch the second.**

**The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics published a points-to-consider statement in *Genetics in Medicine* in April 2024 (26(4):101052, epub 23 February 2024) and its conclusion is not hedged: preimplantation polygenic risk score testing is not yet appropriate for clinical use and should not be offered at this time.** The authors judged that the risks of harm currently outweigh the benefits.

**Three findings from that statement are worth memorising.**

**One. Very few published studies support the validity of reconstructing an embryo's genome from biopsy data plus parental sequences, and at the time of writing only ONE published study had shown a strong correlation between embryonic and postnatal polygenic scores.** That is the entire empirical bridge between the test and the person.

**Two. The analytic problem is physical.** Amplifying DNA from the limited cells of an embryo biopsy complicates analytic validity, and a single biopsy may not accurately represent the cell mass.

**Three, and this is the number that reframes everything: even among people in the TOP decile of polygenic risk for coronary artery disease, more than half are never diagnosed with it. And 16% of people in the BOTTOM decile develop it anyway.** That is the predictive power on a well-studied, heavily-researched disease.

**So what does more sequencing buy?** Precision, not accuracy. Picture three darts in a two-inch cluster six inches from the bullseye. Throwing more darts tightens the cluster. **It does not move the cluster.** A polygenic score is bounded by the population the model was trained on and by how the trait was defined, not by how thoroughly the new individual was read.

**The question to ask is not how much did you read.** It is: what population was the model trained on, how was the trait defined, and what is the validated performance in someone like the person in front of me. **If a company answers the first question loudly and the other three quietly, that tells you which of its numbers is strongest.**

Labels, honestly: the ACMG document is an expert consensus statement, not an experiment, and it can be argued with. The 99% figure is a company's own marketing. The limitation findings are peer-reviewed.

No screening, no scoring, no prediction. **A stored sample makes one claim: this existed, and it is still here. It is the only claim in this field that does not need a model to be true.**

## #179 [X] Three ways to count the same thing, three different answers

**Freshness check:** `Gallup`, `1971`, `fertility rate`, `Harvard Youth Poll` and `NCHS` all return ZERO in the longpost log. The newest figure is 2024 data and is labelled as reported.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend-hook used:** None, and that is stated rather than disguised.

There are three ways to count whether people are having children. They give three different answers. All three are real.

**Ask what is ideal, in the abstract.** Gallup has American preference for families of three or more children at its highest level since **1971**, and young adults are MORE likely than older adults to say three or more is ideal.

**Ask what people personally want and expect.** Roughly **one in three** young American adults neither have children nor want them. A Harvard Youth Poll put "having children" at **48%** importance among 18-to-29-year-olds, **the lowest of six life goals measured**, n=2,096.

**Stop asking, and count the babies.** The US fertility rate hit a record low in 2024, around **1.6 children per woman**. (Reported figure, 2024 data, carried from secondary coverage and not read at source.)

**These do not contradict each other. They are three different questions.** A person can believe three children is ideal and plan on none without being confused or dishonest.

**Here is the part worth keeping.** A survey measuring the ideal and a survey measuring the expectation diverge most precisely when conditions are hardest. **The gap between them is not a values story. It is closer to a price story.** The reasons attached to the second number support that: one survey put **23%** citing finances as the reason for not planning on parenthood, another put roughly **39%** saying they had delayed a child because of the economic climate. (Mixed-quality sources, some marketing-sponsored, methodologies not comparable, and I am not stacking the percentages.)

**The climate answers sit in the same pile and get quoted just as selectively: one study reported **38%** of Gen Z saying climate change is directly putting them off having children, while a separate survey found **25%** of younger adults saying people should stop having children because of harm to others, animals or the environment. (Advocacy and marketing-sponsored sources, listed here so you can see the spread rather than be handed the most dramatic one.)**

**And the behaviour number has its own history: a CDC analysis of 2023 birth-certificate data had already shown the US birth rate at a record low before the 2024 figure landed. The direction has been consistent for several years, which is exactly why a single year's number is a weak thing to argue from.**

**Almost every article you read picks ONE of those three numbers.** One of them sells a panic. One sells a rebuttal. The third is just arithmetic and gets ignored.

So when someone hands you a birth-rate number and tells you what it means about the future, ask which of the three it is. **They will usually have picked the one that suits the argument.**

I do not know which way the next thirty years go. Neither does anyone quoting a single number at you. **And if you are genuinely undecided about children: undecided is a valid plan.** It is not a failure to have an opinion yet.

What you can do under that uncertainty is make the decisions that cost the same in every version of the future. Very few qualify.

## #181 [FACEBOOK] A law that passed in 2026 and still governs nobody

**Freshness check:** `uniform act` returns ZERO in the longpost log. The subject item is dated September/October 2026.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55, plus 40-60 planners.
**Trend-hook used:** None.

Here is a small piece of civic literacy that saves you from a particular kind of confident wrong advice.

**An American Bar Association article dated September/October 2026 reports a new Uniform Transfers to Minors Act that raises the age at which a child's custodial account terminates to 25.**

Read quickly, that sounds like the law changed. **It did not. Not in your state, not yet, and possibly not ever.**

**A "uniform act" is a model.** It is drafted so that states have a well-made text to copy instead of each inventing their own. **Until your state's legislature actually enacts it, it is a very good suggestion with no force at all.** Some states adopt a uniform act nearly word for word. Some adopt it with changes. Some ignore it for decades. Some never take it up.

**This matters because the gap it addresses is real and people get burned in it.** Under existing state UTMA law, a custodial account terminates and pays out at an age set by that state: **most commonly 21, with the range across states running roughly 18 to 25**, and some states letting the person creating the account specify later. California is widely cited as defaulting to 18 with 25 available by election.

**The published tables disagree.** Two state-by-state tables from the same publisher list different ages for the same states. **That alone should tell you not to rely on any table, including one you find on a bank's website.**

**So the practical sequence, if you are naming a young person on anything:**

**One.** Find out what your own state's statute currently says. Not a table, the statute, or a lawyer in your state reading it.

**Two.** Ask whether your state permits you to specify a later termination age, and if it does, decide deliberately rather than taking the default.

**Three.** Do not plan around the 2026 uniform act. Watch for your own legislature taking it up. **A model law is a forecast about legislation, and forecasts are not entitlements.**

**Four, and this is the one people skip: ask what actually happens on the termination date.** The answer is usually that the entire balance becomes the young person's outright property, in cash, with no conditions. **If what you want is conditions, no version of a custodial account at any age gives you that. You want a trust, and the difference is the whole point.**

**One more reason not to plan around a model law: adoption is slow and uneven by design. The uniform-act process exists precisely because each state legislature decides for itself, which is a feature of the system rather than a delay in it.**

The wider habit is worth more than the specific rule. **When someone tells you "the law changed," the useful follow-up is: which legislature passed it, and when does it apply to me?** A surprising amount of confident advice does not survive that question.
