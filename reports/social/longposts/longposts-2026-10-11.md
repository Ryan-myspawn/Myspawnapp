Ten named plaintiffs uploaded DNA to a genealogy site. They used the privacy setting. The setting was real. On or about 30 September 2026 a federal judge ruled they can take to trial the claim that law enforcement users could get around it anyway.

The case is *Hutcheson et al. v. Verogen, Inc.*, No. 3:24-cv-01977 in the Southern District of California, filed 24 October 2024. Verogen owns GEDmatch. Qiagen, a European multinational, acquired Verogen in 2023. **U.S. District Judge James Simmons Jr. partially denied the defendants' motion to dismiss** *(reported by Courthouse News and Bloomberg Law; the order itself was not opened: via search index, verify before posting)*.

Three distinct claims, three different outcomes. That is the part worth your attention, because only one of the three is the thing people argue about online.

**The claim that survived is about a technical loophole, not a policy.** Plaintiffs can pursue the allegation that **GEDmatch's PRO platform let law enforcement users circumvent the opt-out privacy setting.** The judge described that access as, in the reported phrasing, **"at the very least, a substantial privacy invasion."** Note what is not alleged here: that the company published a dishonest policy, or that users failed to read it. The allegation is that the switch on the dashboard did not control the thing it appeared to control.

**The claim about the advertising pixel survived with a date attached.** Allegations concerning **Meta's tracking pixel** can proceed, but the judge **limited them to users who uploaded their DNA after the pixel was incorporated on the site.** That is a ruling about a timestamp. Whether you are in the class depends on which month you uploaded.

**The claim about the acquisition failed.** On the 2023 sale to Qiagen, the court found plaintiffs **had not stated a legally sufficient claim** that their privacy was violated by commercial activity following the change of ownership. The most intuitive fear in this whole category, that a new owner is the danger, is the one claim that did not get past the pleadings here.

**What is left standing is state law, not federal.** Per Bloomberg Law's reporting, claims survive under **genetic privacy statutes in Illinois, Oregon and New Hampshire, plus the California Invasion of Privacy Act** *(via search index)*. There is no federal genetic privacy statute doing this work. Four state laws and one state wiretapping law are carrying it.

**Epistemic status: this is a reported ruling on a motion to dismiss, not a verdict and not a finding of fact.** Surviving a motion to dismiss means a court accepted that the allegation, if true, states a claim. Nothing here has been proven. The defendants have not had their evidence weighed.

The transferable lesson is narrow and it is not "privacy settings are a lie." It is this: **a privacy control is a piece of software, and software has implementations.** When you audit a company holding your genetic data, the question is not whether the setting exists. It is who tested whether the setting works, and whether anyone outside the company has ever checked.

**And this is not the only suit in the file.** A separate reported class action alleges GEDmatch sent Illinois users' genetic data to Facebook, which is the same pixel conduct pleaded under a different state's genetic privacy statute by a different set of plaintiffs *(reported by ClassAction.org; UNDATED in our reading, which for a live case is a real limitation and the reason it is one sentence here rather than a section)*. Two suits, two states, one mechanism. **The Illinois statute keeps appearing because it is one of the few that gives private plaintiffs something to sue with**, and that is a fact about where the law is thin, not about where the conduct happened.

Most of us have never asked any of this about anything.

Now the finding, with the numbers we did not print last time.

The study compared **ensilication**, which seals DNA inside a glass-like silica shell at room temperature, against standard frozen storage. On whole-genome sequencing, **frozen samples carried 5,466 artefactual C>T mutations per million bases against 3,307 for ensilicated samples**, which the authors report as statistically significant at **p = 0.006**, and which they describe as **up to 65 percent more** artefactual damage in the frozen arm. The changes **concentrated at CpG dinucleotides**. On a routine lung cancer panel the two methods agreed, and ensilication **detected every actionable variant, including at low variant allele frequencies.**

That is a real result in a real journal, and it points the way our business model would like it to point. Which is exactly why the next three paragraphs exist.

**The sequencing arm used 28 days of storage.** Twenty-eight. We sell an annual subscription against a horizon measured in decades. **A 28-day result tells you about the first month and says nothing arithmetical about year thirty.** Degradation chemistry is not linear in a way that lets you multiply.

**The material was FFPE tumour and normal tissue, 32 matched pairs.** Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded clinical specimens are not buccal swabs and not whole blood. The damage chemistry of FFPE material is its own subject. **A study about FFPE blocks is not a study about the thing in our vault.**

**The comparison is the authors' own.** The ensilication-versus-frozen result comes from the group with the method, which is normal in early-stage work and is not an accusation. It does mean the independent replication is the thing to wait for, and a vendor write-up by Fulcrum Genomics describes a 14-day panel comparison alongside the 28-day sequencing arm, which is a commercial party describing a study it participated in evaluating.

**Even the mechanism is contested.** One reading attributes the lower damage to the silica matrix chemically shielding DNA from hydrolysis. A separate commentary in a Springer journal suggests the frozen samples' extra damage comes from **continued hydrolysis driven by co-stored fixation byproducts**, which is a claim about the frozen arm's handling rather than about silica's virtues. **Those are different stories with the same chart.**

So what do we actually have? A peer-reviewed 2026 result, on fixed tissue, over four weeks, from the method's own authors, with a disputed mechanism, pointing in a direction that favours ambient storage. **That is genuine evidence and it is nowhere near a decades claim.** Anyone selling you long-term storage on the back of it, including us in September, went further than the paper does.

The receipt that actually carries weight for long intervals is not this study at all. It is the American newborn blood-spot archive: paper cards held at room temperature for decades, from which researchers have recovered usable genotypes. **That is an existence proof with real years on it.** This paper is a mechanism result with 28 days on it. We should have kept those two in separate sentences, and from today we will.

**Primary audience:** Men 25-55 (and this sub skews that way already).
**Trend-hook used:** a lawsuit filed **22 July 2026** in the Northern District of Illinois seeking fifty-year retention of donor medical records.
**Lane:** story-driven. **One disclosure, no pitch.**

Disclosure up front: I run a DNA storage company, so I have an interest in how long records last. I am not selling anything in this post and there is no link to my site in it. I want to put one number in front of this sub because it undercuts a lot of what gets discussed here.

Every preservation plan has three layers. **The material. The institution holding it. And the records that say what the material is and where it came from.** This sub spends most of its energy on layer one, some on layer two, and almost none on layer three. **Layer three has the shortest clock in the stack, and in one case it is written into federal regulation.**

Here is the case. Under the FDA's rules for human cells, tissues and cellular and tissue-based products, records for a particular product must be retained for **at least ten years** after administration, or after distribution, disposition or expiration where the administration date is unknown, at [21 CFR 1271.55(d)(4)](https://www.fda.gov/media/142768/download). Ten years is a floor, not a ceiling, and establishments may keep things longer. But the floor is the floor.

**Now the arithmetic, which is the whole post. If a sperm or egg donation is used, and the records clock starts, the regulatory minimum expires when the person conceived from that donation is ten.** Which means a genetic parent's medical information can lawfully be destroyed **before the person it describes most urgently reaches the age of majority.** Not through negligence. Through compliance.

**This is being litigated right now.** A donor-conceived adult **petitioned the FDA in 2016** to require longer retention. The agency denied the petition. A suit filed **22 July 2026 in the Northern District of Illinois** asks a court to require **fifty-year retention** of medical records for donors of sperm or oocytes, on the argument that the current policy permits destruction before donor-conceived people reach adulthood. The petition itself is public at [regulations.gov](https://downloads.regulations.gov/FDA-2016-P-2955-0010/attachment_1.pdf). **The suit is at an early stage. There is no ruling. Do not plan around it.**

**One state has already gone the other way, which is the interesting part for anyone here who thinks of long retention as the privacy villain.** Colorado's Donor-Conceived Persons Protection Act **took effect 1 January 2025**. Licensed gamete banks, clinics and agencies must keep donation records **permanently**, must **periodically ask donors to update their medical history and contact information**, and identity is released to offspring at 18. It is **prospective only**: donations before 2025 are not reopened. For one group of people, the harm was never retention. It was deletion.

**Why this should change how you think about any preservation scheme, including the ones this sub likes.** A sample with no provenance record is a tube of material. The chain that makes it mean something, who it came from, what they were screened for, what was known about their health and when, is paperwork, and paperwork has retention schedules, corporate owners and shredders. **A time capsule with your genome in it is only as good as the document that says whose genome it is.** Ten-year minimums, two-year state revisions, a company changing hands: those are the realistic failure modes, and none of them is about molecular chemistry.

**What we do not know yet, honestly:** I have one secondary legal-news source for the 22 July 2026 filing date and the fifty-year figure and I have **not** checked it against the court docket. The Colorado detail comes from advocacy and law-firm summaries rather than the statute and the final state rules, and I could not establish the exact interval meant by "periodically." Whether a court moves the federal floor at all, nobody knows. **And I have no idea what the actual average retention practice is, as opposed to the minimum, because I could not find a survey of it.** If someone here has that, I would genuinely like to read it.

The question I would put to this sub: if you were designing the records layer from scratch for something meant to last a century, what retention period would you mandate, and who would you trust to still be enforcing it in year sixty?
