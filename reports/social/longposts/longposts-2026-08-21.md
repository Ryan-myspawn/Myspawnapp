## ARTICLE 1 [X] — "The Pentagon put two price tags on human biology" (~620 words)

On September 14, a new United States military benefit goes live: the Department of Defense will reimburse service members for freezing their reproductive cells before they walk into danger.

The numbers, from the Federal Register notice published August 13: up to $10,000 per year for egg retrieval and cryopreservation. Up to $500 per year for sperm.

Congress ordered this in the fiscal 2025 National Defense Authorization Act. It runs as a three-year pilot. Eligibility is precise: active-duty members with hazardous-duty orders, those likely to receive them within 120 days, or anyone separated from a spouse or partner for at least 180 days on military orders (as reported by Army Times, August 12, 2026).

It covers military hospitals and civilian clinics. It explicitly does not cover IVF or embryo creation (The Hill, August 2026).

The internet found the story and immediately started the wrong argument: $10,000 versus $500, sexism or not. Here is the boring answer: egg retrieval is surgery. Hormone injections, monitoring, anesthesia, an operating room. The widely cited US market range is $10,000 to $15,000 per cycle. Sperm banking is a clinic visit and a storage fee. The Pentagon did not price men and women. It priced two procedures.

The interesting part is what almost nobody said.

The largest employer in the United States just wrote into policy a very specific piece of logic: before exposure to irreversible risk, preserve the biological material that cannot be recreated. That logic used to be private advice whispered in fertility clinics. Now it has a budget line, a start date, and a Federal Register citation.

And the military is not alone. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine's 2026 committee opinion treats fertility preservation programs as standard-of-care infrastructure for patients facing gonadotoxic treatment, not as an exotic add-on. The referees and the institutions are aligned: preservation before risk is now the establishment position.

Civilians do not get deployment orders. Our risks are quieter and less scheduled: illness, accidents, time. Nobody publishes a Federal Register notice about your particular life.

Full disclosure, since we are a company that lives adjacent to this: gamete freezing is fertility preservation and it is a medical decision made with a clinic. DNA banking, which is what we do at MySpawn, is record preservation. Different jobs. But the underlying policy logic the Pentagon just adopted is the same one we built a $99 kit around: back up what cannot be rebuilt, before you need it.

The government figured out that biology needs a backup plan. It is allowed to be ahead of you. Barely.

## ARTICLE 2 [FACEBOOK] — "The government is building an ark. Check the passenger list." (~590 words)

In June, with very little fanfare, the United States government started backing up life itself.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service announced a partnership with Colossal Biosciences to collect and cryopreserve the DNA, tissue, and reproductive cells of America's endangered species. More than 2,300 species are on the list. The samples go into a network of storage called BioVault: liquid nitrogen, redundant sites, custody logs (Gizmodo, June 2026).

Think about the reasoning for a second, because it is beautiful in its bluntness. Extinction is forever. A genome that took millions of years to write cannot be rewritten. So you preserve it now, while it exists, for uses nobody can fully predict: disease research, genetic rescue, restoration, questions no one has thought to ask yet. This is an institutional program. Real tanks, real federal partnership, real accession numbers.

Conservationists did not wait for the science of "bringing back" anything to be settled. That debate is loud and unfinished. They preserved first, because preservation is the only step with a deadline. The deadline is: while it still exists.

Now here is the thought that has been sitting with me since I read the announcement.

Every one of those 2,300 species gets this treatment because its population is small enough to be precious. A few thousand condors. A few hundred red wolves.

Your family line is a population of one.

There is no federal program for it. No agency files your grandmother's genome. When she is gone, that exact sequence, the one that produced her laugh and her stubbornness and half of you, is gone in a way even the condor's is not, because somebody thought to save the condor's.

I think about the things families do keep: a recipe card, four photo albums, a wedding ring. Beautiful, and biologically silent. The one thing no attic in America holds is the actual biological record, and for most of history that was because it could not be done. That excuse expired. Preserving a DNA sample today is a $99 errand that takes seven minutes at a kitchen table; ours goes into an accredited, FDA-registered biorepository in New Jersey, the same kind of custody the ark uses.

The government looked at irreplaceable genomes and decided backing them up was obvious.

So here is my question, and I mean it as a real question, not a pitch: if your family had an ark, who and what would you put in it first?

## ARTICLE 3 [X] — "The 100-year test" (~640 words, ZERO product mention)

Look around the room you are in and pick the five things you would want your great-grandchildren to have. Now apply the 100-year test: how many of them will physically exist in 2126?

The honest answers are grim, and the numbers are specific.

**Your burned CDs and DVDs are already dying.** Recordable discs store data in an organic dye layer that degrades whether or not you touch them. The Canadian Conservation Institute and the Council on Library and Information Resources put typical CD-R life at roughly 5 to 25 years depending on dye and storage; only gold-layer archival discs claim 100. The photo CDs from 2005 in your closet are at the far edge of their design life right now.

**Your printed photos depend on which decade printed them.** Dye-based inkjet prints from the 2000s fade in as little as 25 to 30 years. Pigment prints on archival paper can last 100 to 300. Most family photos were not printed on the good stuff, and the ones displayed in sunlight are fading on schedule (PetaPixel's print-permanence summary is a sobering read).

**Your cloud is not a vault, it is a subscription with a heartbeat monitor.** Google's official inactive account policy: two years without a login and your account, photos included, becomes eligible for deletion. Enforcement began in December 2023 and continues in waves. Facebook will memorialize an account if someone tells them you died and files the paperwork; if nobody does, inactive accounts are eventually deleted. The keyword in every policy is "inactive," and being dead makes you very inactive.

**The companies themselves fail the test too.** HereAfter AI sold families "a living memorial that lasts forever": record your parents' stories, talk to them after they are gone. In July 2026 its homepage became a shutdown notice telling users to email support to retrieve the recordings. By August the homepage was an error page. Forever lasted about seven years.

Run the test on the usual suspects. Phone: fails (batteries, ports, cloud lock). Laptop: fails. Social accounts: fail without active heirs doing paperwork. Burned discs: failing now. Standard prints: coin flip. USB drives: bit rot, 10-ish years unpowered.

What actually passes? Boring, physical, low-tech things. Paper with pigment ink, stored dark and dry: centuries. Stone: obviously. Archival microfilm: 500 years, which is why governments still use it. Things made of stable matter that require no company, no login, and no electricity to remain themselves.

The pattern is almost embarrassing: survivability correlates with how little a thing depends on anyone remembering to maintain it.

One hundred years is not even a long time. There are people alive today who will see 2126. The question is not whether they will want to know who you were. Genealogy is one of the most searched hobbies on earth; people pay strangers to find one photograph of a great-grandparent. The question is whether anything you own will still be able to answer.

Audit your five things tonight. If they all live on a server you pay for monthly, you do not have an archive. You have a tab open.

## ARTICLE 4 [REDDIT r/SideProject] — "I built a letter tool with a 100-year delivery date. Here's what I learned." (~600 words)

On December 31, 1900, the city of Detroit sealed a copper box with letters from its citizens addressed to the Detroit of 2000. When it was opened a century later, city officials read predictions from 56 prominent Detroiters to a new millennium's audience. In 1940, Oglethorpe University went further and welded shut the Crypt of Civilization, a room-sized time capsule with instructions not to open it until the year 8113.

I could not stop thinking about how you would build that today, so I built it: a free web tool where you write a letter that stays sealed until January 1, 2126.

Disclosure first, per sub rules: I am a founder. My company sells a DNA preservation kit (think: a time capsule with your genome in it). The letter tool is the free part, no account, no email capture on the letter itself, and this post is about what building it taught me, not a funnel.

**What I learned shipping a 100-year product:**

1. The hard problem is not code, it is custody. Any promise measured in decades outlives the server, the domain registrar, and statistically the founder. The Detroit box worked because a city government held it. My honest answer is layered redundancy and printing: we tell users the letter is also theirs to print and seal physically. A century promise you cannot fully guarantee should say so out loud. Ours does.

2. Dated delivery changes what people write. Feedback surprised me: people said writing to 2126 felt more honest than journaling. You stop performing. There is no audience to impress for 100 years, and the reader you imagine has no stake in your ego. Several users told me they cried writing it. Zero said that about their newsletter.

3. The deadline does the marketing. "Sealed until January 1, 2126" is the whole pitch. I have watched people try to explain the tool to friends and every one of them leads with the date.

4. Old time capsules are a design spec. The Crypt of Civilization includes a machine to teach English to whoever opens it, because its builders assumed nothing about the reader. That assumption-free mindset is the right one: we keep the letter format plain text at heart, the most future-proof format humans have.

What I do not know yet, honestly: whether any web-native promise can actually survive 100 years, and whether the right long-term answer is partnership with an institution that already thinks in centuries (a university, an archive). If anyone here has shipped genuinely long-horizon software, I want to hear what I am missing.

Tool is free, linked in my profile per sub rules. Happy to answer anything about the build or the weird product decisions a 2126 deadline forces.

## ARTICLE 5 [X] — "Read the actual Gen Z surveys. They don't say 'never.'" (~630 words)

The headline says Gen Z refuses to have kids. The data says something more interesting, and almost nobody quotes it.

Start with the numbers everyone skips past. Independent Center polling of Gen Z and millennials: 30 percent do not have and do not want children. But 44 percent say they want children within the next five years (as reported by Newsweek, 2026). The single largest camp is not "never." It is "yes, but not under these conditions," followed closely by "not sure yet."

The conditions are not a mystery; they are itemized. A Securian Financial survey from early 2025: 84 percent cite money as a key influence on the decision, and 68 percent say the cost of raising children specifically makes parenthood less likely. MassMutual polling found almost 1 in 4 millennials and Gen Zers saying they will not have kids for financial reasons (CBS News). Current estimates put raising a child in the US at $20,000 to $26,000 per year, well over $300,000 to age 18.

And the mood: in the Care.com perception-of-parenting survey, 52 percent of respondents feel pessimistic about raising kids in the US today. Only 17 percent feel optimistic.

So the accurate one-line summary of the research is not "Gen Z doesn't want kids." It is: a generation is doing cost-benefit analysis out loud, in public, on a decision every previous generation made mostly on autopilot, and a large share are answering "not yet" or "not like this."

Both culture-war teams hate this reading. The pronatalist side wants the data to say selfishness so the fix can be shame. The anti-natalist side wants it to say liberation so the trend can be a victory lap. The surveys stubbornly describe neither. They describe deferral under uncertainty: housing, wages, climate, childcare math.

Here is the part I find genuinely under-discussed: deferral has a biology problem. You can postpone the decision indefinitely. Your cells do not postpone with you. That asymmetry is exactly why egg freezing exists, why the Pentagon began reimbursing deploying troops up to $10,000 a year for it starting September 14, and why "fertility preservation" has gone from taboo to employee benefit at major companies in under a decade.

You do not have to want kids to see the design principle: when a decision is genuinely undecided, the rational move is to keep the option alive cheaply, not to let the deadline decide for you.

That principle scales down to almost nothing. Freezing gametes is a medical decision with a clinic and a real price tag. But preserving your DNA, the record itself, for whatever purpose your future self or family ever finds, is a $99 errand (that is what we make at MySpawn; storage only, and nobody should promise you more than storage). And writing down who you are for whoever comes after you, related or not, is free.

Undecided is a valid plan. The surveys prove an entire generation is living it. The only unforced error is letting "not yet" quietly harden into "too late" because nobody kept the option open.
