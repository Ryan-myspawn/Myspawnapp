## #62 [X] — "Two and a Half Points"
*~760 words · contrarian science read · hook: the arithmetic nobody puts in the ad*

There is a number at the centre of the embryo-screening industry that almost never appears in its marketing, and it is not a secret. It has been in a peer-reviewed journal since 2019.

Here it is. If you screen five embryos and pick the highest-scoring one, the expected gain is about **2.5 IQ points**, or about **2.5 centimetres** of height. If you could somehow choose from fifty embryos — which no one can — it rises to roughly **4.5**.

That is from Karavani et al., *Cell*, 2019, "Screening Human Embryos for Polygenic Traits Has Limited Utility." **Peer-reviewed.**

**Why the number is so small.** You are not selecting from the human range. You are selecting among embryos from the same two parents. Siblings share roughly half their DNA. The spread you are choosing across is narrow by construction, and no improvement in the score fixes that — the paper notes the gain rises very slowly with the number of embryos and much faster with the predictive power of the score itself.

**The part of that paper people should quote more.** The same team checked the prediction against reality using 28 families with ten or more adult children. In most of those families, **the child with the highest polygenic score for height was not the tallest one.**

**What the scores do well, because this matters.** Polygenic scores are not fake. Across populations they genuinely stratify risk. One peer-reviewed analysis (*eLife*, 2021) put the proportion of variance in liability explained at roughly **6% for Crohn's disease, 7% for schizophrenia and 9% for type 2 diabetes**. Real numbers, real signal.

The problem is not the statistics. It is what happens when a population statistic is pointed at one embryo.

**Three things that shrink it further.**

1. Scores are validated on unrelated people, which inflates them. Some of the apparent power comes from population stratification, assortative mating, and indirect parental effects — "genetic nurture," where a parent's genotype shapes the environment rather than the child's biology. None of that transfers when you compare embryos from the *same* parents. Sibling comparison is the weakest setting for a polygenic score, and it is the only setting this product operates in.
2. Accuracy drops substantially outside European-ancestry datasets, because that is where the underlying association studies were done.
3. Real IVF does not present a shelf. Modelling on actual patient data shows most cycles yield one usable embryo or none. With one embryo there is nothing to select.

**Where the professional body landed.** On **8 December 2025**, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine's Ethics and Practice Committees concluded that polygenic embryo screening **is not ready for clinical practice and should not be offered as a reproductive service at this time**, recommending research settings under IRB oversight until the concerns are addressed. *(Professional society position statement.)*

**And one month later**, on **12 January 2026**, MIT Technology Review named embryo scoring one of its 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2026. *(Editorial selection — the same piece calls the technology controversial, and an editorial list is a bet on what will matter, not a clinical endorsement.)*

Both are true at once. A technology can be real, funded and canonised and still not be ready to make a decision with.

**The ask, and it is not to buy anything.** If you are the kind of person who has abandoned a supplement because the only evidence was a rat study, apply the same standard here. Ask for absolute numbers rather than relative ones — a "40% reduction" on a 1% baseline is 1% to 0.6%. Ask which ancestry the score was validated in. Ask how many embryos you are likely to actually have. And separate disease-risk screening from trait screening in your own head, even when a company sells them together.

I run a DNA preservation company. We store records; we assign no scores and rank nothing, and we are not in this market, which is exactly why I will say the number out loud.

## #63 [FACEBOOK] — "In 2004, 30% of Americans Wrote Down Their Family's Health History. In 2024, It Was 15%."
*~870 words · practical legacy guide · **ZERO PRODUCT MENTION** · hook: a number that went backwards*

Here is a statistic that stopped me.

In the CDC's 2004 HealthStyles Survey, **96.3%** of Americans said knowing their family health history was very or somewhat important. Only **29.8%** had actually collected one.

Twenty years later, in a survey run in **January–February 2024**, **94.8%** still said it was important. The share who had actually collected one had fallen to **15.2%**.

We did not get better at this. We got worse, by half, during two decades in which genetic testing became a supermarket product. *(Both figures reported in the family-medicine literature on barriers to family health history collection — via search index, worth opening before you quote it.)*

I think that is because the DNA test felt like it replaced the conversation. It doesn't. Here is what the conversation actually buys you.

**It changes your calendar, not your biology.**

If you have a first-degree relative diagnosed with colorectal cancer or advanced polyps **before age 60**, or two or more first-degree relatives diagnosed at any age, American College of Gastroenterology guidance is to start screening at **age 40, or ten years before the youngest affected relative's diagnosis, whichever comes first.**

Read that again. That is potentially a fifteen-year head start on the most survivable-when-caught-early cancer there is, and the entire trigger is a fact you either know or don't.

It runs through everything. NCCN criteria route people toward BRCA1/BRCA2 testing largely on family pattern — who was affected, how young, which side. Lynch syndrome surveillance schedules key off the earliest diagnosis in the family. Prostate referral criteria look at a first-degree relative with prostate cancer alongside close relatives with triple-negative or male breast cancer — a pattern that looks like unrelated bad luck until someone writes it down.

**The part everyone skips: ages.**

"My dad had colon cancer" is a story. "My dad was diagnosed with colon cancer at 52" is an instruction to a doctor. If nobody remembers exactly, write the decade and mark it approximate. A rough age beats a blank.

**Seven questions. That's the whole thing.**

For each blood relative — you, siblings, children, parents, aunts, uncles, grandparents:

1. Living? If not, what did they die of, and at what age?
2. What have they been diagnosed with, and **at what age for each**?
3. Any cancer — which organ, specifically?
4. Heart disease, stroke, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes?
5. Mental health or neurological diagnoses?
6. Pregnancy losses, birth defects, children who died young?
7. Ancestry and country of origin?

Three generations is the useful depth. Further back gets unreliable faster than it gets valuable.

**Do it one person at a time, not at the table.** People are more precise one-on-one, and the family-legend version gets corrected faster. Record the conversation on your phone. Your memory of what your aunt said is not a source.

**Then put it where it can be used.** The CDC, with the National Human Genome Research Institute, maintains a free tool called **My Family Health Portrait** that organises it and lets you share it with your doctor and your relatives. A plain document is fine too. What matters is that it is written, that a clinician can see it, and that **at least one other person knows it exists** — the usual way a family record is lost is not decay, it's that the only person who knew about it is gone.

Update it when someone gets a new diagnosis. A history written once at 35 is a snapshot.

**Why I'm posting this with nothing attached.**

I run a company in the preservation business, and this costs nothing and involves no product of mine. I am posting it because it is the only item on any legacy list with a real deadline. The people who remember what your grandfather died of, and how old he was, are a finite resource, and every year some of that becomes permanently unrecoverable. No service can reconstruct it afterwards. Mine certainly can't.

If you do one thing this month, do this one.

Which relative are you going to call first — and do you actually know what age your grandparents were when they got sick?

## #64 [X] — "$435 Million, and the First Human Study Is in 2027"
*~690 words · science explainer · hook: three dollar figures against three dates*

Longevity had a very good funding year. I want to put the money next to the evidence, because they are not the same thing and the gap is where people get hurt.

**The money.**

- **NewLimit** raised **$435 million** on **2 June 2026**, at roughly a **$3.1 billion** valuation. Its first human study is expected in **2027**. *(Company/investor announcement.)*
- **Retro Biosciences** has gone from a $180M seed to over **$1.18 billion** cumulative. In **August 2025**, with OpenAI, it announced AI models had made cellular reprogramming roughly **50 times more efficient**. *(Company announcement — that is an engineering throughput claim, not a clinical result.)*
- **Altos Labs** remains the best-capitalised name in the field.

All three are working on the same idea: **partial reprogramming.**

**What that actually is.** In 2012 Shinya Yamanaka won a Nobel for showing an adult cell can be pushed back to an embryonic-like state using four factors. Full reprogramming makes a cell forget what it was — useful in a dish, catastrophic in a body, because a liver cell that forgets its job is a tumour risk. Partial reprogramming applies the factors briefly: push part of the way, reset age-associated markers, stop before the cell forgets.

Whether that reverses aging or merely resets the markers we happen to measure is the open question. Hold onto that distinction. It is the one the marketing loses.

**The evidence, in three tiers.**

**Tier 1, peer-reviewed, mice.** In 2020, Yuancheng Lu and colleagues published work showing a reprogramming therapy restored vision in aged and injured mice. In 2024, Altos scientists published work showing targeted partial reprogramming of age-associated cell states extended mouse lifespan.

**Tier 2, running in humans, no result.** On **11 September 2026**, MIT Technology Review's Innovators Under 35 reported that a near-identical version of that vision therapy is now being tested in people with eye disease. That is genuinely significant — and it is a trial with **no published outcome**. "In humans" is not "works in humans." A trial is a question, not an answer, and the people running it would say so first.

**Tier 3, money.** See above. Valuation is not evidence. I'd say that about my own company.

**Why I'm drawing the line so hard.** Three separate times in the last nine days, our own research sweeps surfaced funding rounds and old papers phrased as though they were fresh results. The UK fertility regulator's recommendation on lab-grown gametes keeps resurfacing as news; it is dated **27 January 2025**. The Oregon skin-cell-to-egg work keeps resurfacing as a breakthrough; it published **30 September 2025**, and the lead researcher described it at the time as "partially works, and partially doesn't" — a proof of concept. *(Both peer-reviewed or institutional, both old.)*

If a professional research operation has to catch that six times in nine days, a normal person scrolling has no chance.

**So here is the reading rule.** When you see a longevity headline, sort it into one of three buckets before you feel anything: *published result in animals*, *running in humans with no result*, or *money*. Most of what excites people is the third bucket wearing the first bucket's clothes.

None of this is a knock on the field. The mouse data is real, the trials are real, and I hope every one of them works. But the honest state of play is that every serious bet has a date on it and none of the dates is now — the best-funded reprogramming company's first human study is next year.

Which makes the rational move boring: stay alive, stay healthy, and don't lose the inputs. Everything in this field operates on a living person and on material that still exists. None of it is retroactive.

## #65 [FACEBOOK] — "California Just Wrote Rules for AI Companions. Every One of Them Protects Someone Who Is Alive."
*~800 words · trust/custody discourse · hook: the protection that stops at the edge of a life*

On **10 September 2026**, the Governor of California signed a package of thirteen bills aimed at protecting young people from technology. Two of them are the first real rulebook anyone has written for AI companions.

The provisions, as reported: companion chatbots must carry **crisis protocols** for suicidal ideation. They must **verify age**. Parents get **controls**, and get **notified if a child switches the safety settings off**. Social platforms may not serve behaviourally addictive features to users **under 16**. Toys containing AI companion chatbots are **banned**.

One of the laws is named after a teenager who died. I'm going to leave it at that, because his family did not put his name there so that people like me could use it as a hook.

I want to talk about the structure of what was signed, because I am in an adjacent business and I think the gap matters.

**Every protection in that package is for a person who is alive.**

That is the right place to start. Children first is not a controversial ordering, and nobody is arguing with it.

But follow the logic one step. If a company builds a chatbot of your father — trained on his messages, his voice notes, his emails — nothing signed that day applies to him.

He cannot be screened for risk. He cannot disable a setting. He cannot be notified. He has no standing at all, and the person paying the subscription is not him. The consent question has no one to answer it, because the only person who could give consent is the reason the product exists.

**This is not hypothetical.** The AI-afterlife category is real, it has customers, and it has already produced at least one documented failure: a company that held recorded voices of people's dead relatives and stopped answering, leaving families with whatever copies they had made themselves. Families discovered the difference between *access* and *possession* at the worst possible moment.

So: there is now a rulebook for chatbots of the living, and there is none for chatbots of the dead. The second market is entirely on the honour system.

**I am in that market, and I am not asking for a law.**

I have an obvious interest in saying this and you should discount it accordingly. A company arguing for regulation of its own sector is usually building a moat. I'm not proposing rules. I'm pointing out which parts of this have them and which don't, and then telling you where we stop, so you can hold us to it while nobody is making us.

**Here is our line, in plain words.**

We store DNA records. We store video and voice notes that a member recorded themselves, sealed until a date they chose. We do not generate speech in anyone's voice. We do not build a model of a person. We do not sell a conversation with the dead.

Our one AI thing, ECHO, speaks as a fictional descendant in 2126. It is not a simulation of a real person, living or dead, and it never will be.

**Why it's a line and not a feature.** The distinction that matters is not how good the technology gets. It's this: **a recording can only ever repeat what someone actually said. A model will eventually say something they never said.** That isn't a bug you engineer out. That is what the thing is for.

That choice costs us a product line that would probably sell well. I think it is the only version of this business that is defensible in twenty years.

**What I'd actually ask of you.** Not a purchase. If anyone in your family is paying for a service that holds recordings of someone who has died, go and check two things this week: whether you can download your own copy, and what happens to it if the company is acquired or closes. The answer is usually in the terms, and it is usually shorter and worse than people expect.

The rules are coming for the living first, and that's correct. Just know which side of the line your own family's recordings are sitting on.

Have you ever actually tried to download something you pay to "keep"?

## #66 [REDDIT — r/Entrepreneur] — "Six times in nine days, our research surfaced old news as current. So I built a checklist."
*~740 words · story-driven ops essay · disclosed founder · hook: an error count*

I run a small preservation startup and we publish research-heavy content daily. Over the last nine days our own sweeps surfaced **six** items framed as current news that turned out to be months or years old. None of them got published, but only because someone checked. Here is the pattern and the fix, because I don't think this is specific to my industry.

**The six.**

1. A national fertility regulator's recommendation on lab-grown eggs and sperm, presented as a fresh policy move. Actual date: **27 January 2025**. Twenty months old.
2. A university lab creating human eggs from skin cells, presented as a breakthrough. Published **30 September 2025** — and the lead scientist described it at the time as "partially works, and partially doesn't," a proof of concept. One aggregator had it as women in their sixties and seventies being able to have genetically related children, which is not what the paper supports.
3. A $435M funding round, surfacing as a result rather than a raise.
4. An AI-efficiency announcement from **August 2025**.
5. A lifespan study from **2024**.
6. Survey figures on birth rates that were several years old, recirculating as this week's data.

**Why this happens, mechanically.**

Search indexes rank by relevance and freshness *of the page*, not freshness of the event. A blog post published yesterday about a 2024 study looks new. Aggregators rewrite old papers with present-tense verbs. And an AI summary of search results will confidently hand you a date-free paragraph that reads like breaking news, because the underlying pages don't lead with dates either.

The failure mode is not that the facts are false. **They're true. They're just not new.** Which is worse, because a true-but-stale fact survives a fact-check and still makes you look like you don't read your own field.

**The fix, which took about twenty minutes.**

I stopped treating this as a discipline problem — "check your dates" is advice that fails exactly when you're busy — and made it a lookup problem instead.

We now keep a **stale-item checklist**: a plain list of every item that has actually recurred in a sweep framed as current, with its real date and, where relevant, the specific wrong gloss that keeps getting attached to it. Before anything goes in a draft, it gets checked against the list. That's it.

Three things made it work:

- **It only contains items that have actually recurred.** Not a theoretical watchlist. Repeat offenders are a small, predictable set.
- **It records the misleading framing, not just the date.** "Skin cells to eggs, Sept 2025" is useful. "Skin cells to eggs, Sept 2025 — do NOT repeat the gloss about women in their 60s and 70s having genetically related children" is what actually prevents the error.
- **The rule is one line:** if an item is on the list, it is not news. It is a receipt. Receipts are still useful — they just can't be the lead.

**The generalisable bit.**

Every content operation eventually drifts toward writing from memory, because memory is fast and research is slow. The checklist doesn't fix that. What it does is make the fast path safe for the specific errors you have already made, which is the only category you can actually predict.

I'd also say: **count your near-misses out loud.** We caught six. If I hadn't written the number down I'd have experienced it as "occasionally the search results are a bit off" instead of "this is our most common failure mode by a wide margin." The count is what turned it into a fixable process problem.

**What we don't know yet:** whether the checklist holds up as it grows. Right now it's eleven entries and easy to scan. At a hundred it becomes something someone has to search rather than read, and I suspect it quietly stops getting used. I'll post again if it breaks.

*Disclosure: I run MySpawn, a DNA preservation service — essentially a time capsule with your genome in it. Nothing to sell here, this is just the ops lesson.*
