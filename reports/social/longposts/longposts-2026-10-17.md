A peer-reviewed paper came out five days ago in which scientists wrote digital information into synthetic DNA on a chip, read it back, corrected the errors, and recovered the message.

The message was 60 bytes.

Sixty bytes is shorter than this paragraph. It is about one line of plain text. That is not a criticism of the work. It is the work's own number, and it is the most useful number published in this field this month.

**The paper.** Yang, Wang and Zhao, *Microsystems and Nanoengineering*, volume 12, article 342, 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41378-026-01456-1, **published 12 October 2026**. Peer-reviewed, Nature Portfolio. *(Read via search index and the abstract. Not opened at its own domain: DNS resolution has failed from this machine for twenty-two consecutive days.)*

What they built is a sacrificial-template porous silica thin film with tunable pore size, sitting on top of an electrochemical synthesis chip. The point of the film is surface area: more reaction area per electrode, without losing the localised electrochemical control that lets you write a different sequence in each well.

Reported results: parallel synthesis of **121-nucleotide** strands, and error-corrected recovery of encoded digital information. The authors say their error statistics are comparable to other laboratory-built DNA writing systems.

**The detail that should have been the headline, and which cuts against the authors' own chip.** In a separate comparison at 60 nucleotides, **commercially sourced oligonucleotides performed better overall** than the chip-synthesised ones. The paper names its remaining problems in its own words: fidelity, parallelism and cost.

That is a research group publishing the experiment where their platform loses. It is the single best reason to trust the rest of the paper.

**Now put it beside the week's other news.** On **15 October 2026**, three days ago, Biomemory announced DNA Shield, described as its first commercial product, a managed service that writes encrypted data into synthetic DNA and keeps it offline. Availability is reported as limited to a few pilots running from 15 October 2026 to mid-2027. The company acquired Catalog Technologies in March 2026. *(PR Newswire and trade coverage. COMPANY ANNOUNCEMENT, not peer-reviewed, not independently verified.)* A second startup, AtlasBase, was covered on **2 October 2026** describing an enterprise archiving platform around a chip that writes into dehydrated DNA sealed in capsules. *(Coverage describes it as a concept for data centres. No independent performance data found.)*

So in one fortnight the field produced an enterprise archiving service, a data-centre concept, and a peer-reviewed result that recovered sixty bytes.

**Every one of those is true at the same time.** The commercial products are not fraudulent for being ahead of the published lab result: a service can buy synthesis capacity from an industrial supplier rather than build its own chip, which is exactly what the 60-nucleotide comparison in this paper suggests you would do today. The lab result is not trivial for being small: the whole industry's cost problem is synthesis, and a cheaper way to make more strands per chip is the thing that moves it.

The honest reading is that the announcements and the science are measuring different objects. One is measuring a service built on existing supply. The other is measuring whether the supply can get cheaper.

**What to do with the number.** When you next read that DNA can now store the internet, go and find the most recent peer-reviewed read-back and check what it recovered. Not what the medium can theoretically hold: what somebody actually wrote and got back out, with errors corrected, in a journal, this year.

Theoretical capacity is a property of the molecule. Recovered payload is a property of the engineering. Those two numbers are separated by several orders of magnitude right now, and that gap is not a scandal. It is just the gap, and it is the only thing in the story that tells you where the field is.

One boundary, because the vocabulary invites the mistake: this is synthetic DNA used as a hard drive. The DNA is the container and your files are the contents. It has nothing to do with keeping a biological sample from a person, where the DNA is not the container but the thing itself. Same two words, opposite directions.

Disclosure before anything else: I run a company that stores DNA samples for people, $99 a year. So I have an interest in this subject and you should read the rest with that in mind. There are no links to my product in this post.

I had something in my notes as finished. It isn't, and finding that out led somewhere more useful than the thing I was originally looking up.

**UK Biobank is moving more than 11 million participant samples** to a new headquarters at Manchester Science Park. The building is Greenheys, about 131,000 square feet, reported at £60m, with **£127.6m** of UK Research and Innovation funding behind the move. The new facility is specified for **four latest-generation automated freezers with room for up to 20 million samples**, roughly double the current stock, with robots retrieving samples **about four times faster**, in the order of **a million samples a year**. ([UK Biobank, biological samples](https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/about-our-data/biological-samples/); [University of Manchester announcement](https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/uk-biobank-will-move-to-manchester-science-park-with-a-state-of-the-art-robotic-freezer-to-serve-researchers-four-times-faster-than-currently-following-1276m-funding/))

My notes said the move completed in late July or early August 2026. **Today UK Biobank's own pages still read as forward-looking: samples "will be moved" in 2026, and the automated freezers are described as currently being installed.** I cannot confirm from here that the relocation has happened. Mine was the less reliable source.

**Here is the part that actually matters, and it is in UK Biobank's own words rather than mine.** The same pages say its capacity to deliver samples for research projects **will remain limited for the next few years while it replaces its laboratory infrastructure**, with capacity expected to grow significantly **from 2027**. An access FAQ puts it slightly earlier and adds that the cost of sample supply will be reviewed. Samples are treated as depletable, so release runs under a stricter policy reviewed by an Access Sub-Committee.

Read that as a user rather than as an institution. The best-resourced biobank in the world, with nine figures of public money, is upgrading its storage, and the honest consequence it publishes is: **for a few years, you will get fewer samples out.**

Upgrading storage costs you availability. Nobody puts that on a brochure.

**The peer-reviewed part, which is where this stops being a story about one building.**

In *Frontiers in Medicine*, Linsen, Van Landuyt and Ectors published "Automated Sample Storage in Biobanking to Enhance Translational Research: The Bumpy Road to Implementation," DOI [10.3389/fmed.2019.00309](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/medicine/articles/10.3389/fmed.2019.00309/full), on the UZ KU Leuven biobank's own automated store. Peer-reviewed. Published January 2020.

Three findings worth having:

- After initial site acceptance testing, the system showed **persistent problems with automation and cooling performance**.
- **One of the redundant cooling units failed at weekly switchover, from recurrent high-pressure problems, 32 times across 6 months.**
- Both the cooling and the automation eventually **met the biobank's requirements for routine use**, and the team signed it off.

That third bullet is not a happy ending tacked on. It is the point. The redundant unit failed 32 times and nothing was lost, because that is what redundancy is for. A system with visible, counted, survivable failures is in better shape than one with no published failure data at all.

**And the sentence from that paper I keep coming back to:** the authors state that the validation requirement is **subjective**, and leaves the implementing biobank **without specific criteria**. That is why they had to write their own protocol and publish it.

So when a storage provider tells you its system is validated, the follow-up question is not whether. It is **against what criteria, written by whom.** On that paper's account, there often isn't a published answer, and the operator chose.

**What we don't know yet, and I looked.**

I went looking for a published failure rate for relocating a biobank: how often samples are lost or degraded when a collection moves buildings. **I did not find one.** What came back was cold-chain vendor marketing, including one provider describing fully loaded freezer relocations in language as reassuring as "risk-free," with no independent failure data attached to any of it. One technical note did name a real mechanism: moving between freezer types adds a handling step, and transient warming during that step is where damage happens.

So: there is a peer-reviewed literature on validating a store, and as far as I can find, **no peer-reviewed failure rate for moving one.** If somebody has that paper, I would genuinely like the citation.

**Why I am posting this here rather than anywhere else.** The idea of a time capsule with your genome in it only works if the custody holds for decades, and decades means the thing will be moved, re-equipped, re-validated and re-staffed several times by people you never met. The physics of DNA is the easy part. The institutional continuity is the hard part, and it is the part nobody sells.

Which includes me. **We have not published a validation protocol or acceptance criteria for our own storage, and after reading that Leuven paper I think that is a fair thing to be asked.** Ask it of any provider, including this one: what are your criteria, who wrote them, when were they last tested, and what is your moving plan.

## #149 [FACEBOOK] The thing most likely to erase your family's archive is not a fire

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. `self storage`, `autopay` and `public auction` as a procedure all return clean. `self-storage` returns three hits, one of which is a 13 October script, and **that script is a 50-second piece about the difference between keeping something and having it kept, with no statute and no timeline in it. This is a state-by-state lien procedure.** Distinct.

**Primary audience:** Men 25-55 (the 50-year-old estate planner leads)

**Trend / hook used:** No news peg, declared honestly. This is a practical guide built on statute.

**Length:** 740 words, measured programmatically.

---

People plan for the dramatic version. Fire, flood, a hard drive dying. The thing that actually empties a storage unit is a payment that stops arriving.

Every US state lets a self-storage operator take a lien on the contents of a unit and sell them when the rent goes unpaid. Not a court case. A lien, a notice, and an auction. And the clock that starts it has nothing to do with whether you are alive.

**The timelines are much shorter than people assume, and they are not the same anywhere.**

- **Virginia:** under the Virginia Self-Service Storage Act, the owner may sell the contents at public auction for cash if a default is not cured within **10 days**. ([Virginia Code, popular names index](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacodepopularnames/virginia-self-service-storage-act/))
- **California** is among the fastest, with a sale permitted after as little as **14 consecutive days** of non-payment.
- **Idaho** sits at the other end: the lessee must be in default **continuously for 60 days** before a lien sale.
- **Michigan** runs it under the Self-Service Storage Facility Act, Act 148 of 1985. ([Michigan Legislature](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/mcl-Act-148-of-1985.pdf))
- **South Carolina** runs it under Title 39, Chapter 20. ([South Carolina Statehouse](https://www.scstatehouse.gov/code/t39c020.php))

Notice is required nearly everywhere, and here my sources disagree, so you get both: one puts the written notice at **14 to 30 days**, another puts the whole wait from first missed payment to sale at **30 to 90 days**. Both are operator guidance rather than statute, and the gap between them is the reason you check your own state rather than an article.

**Three things most people do not know about the sale itself.**

First, buyers usually **bid on the entire contents without knowing what is inside.** Nobody is sorting the photographs out of the box before the hammer falls.

Second, if the contents sell for **less** than what is owed, the remaining debt can still follow the tenant or the estate. The auction is not a settlement.

Third, there is one real federal protection and it is narrow: a Missouri guide notes the operator must check whether the tenant is an **active-duty servicemember**, because federal law requires a court order in that case.

**Now the part that connects to a death.**

None of those clocks start when somebody dies. They start when a payment fails.

A card expires. A bank freezes an account on notification of death. An executor spends six weeks getting letters of administration, which is normal and fast by probate standards. Meanwhile the unit holding the photo albums, the service records, the box of letters and the only copy of a grandparent's film reels is on a 14-day or 30-day clock that began the day the autopay bounced.

The archive does not get destroyed by anybody's decision. It gets sold because an invoice went unpaid for three weeks during the worst month of your family's life.

**What to actually do, and it is small.**

1. **Write down every recurring payment that protects something irreplaceable.** Storage unit, safe deposit box, domain name, cloud archive, anything with a lien or a lapse clause. One page. No passwords on it.
2. **Name the state and the notice period next to each one**, because the number is the whole point and it is different in Virginia and Idaho by a factor of six.
3. **Give someone the list who is not your spouse.** Simultaneous-incapacity is the case this plan exists for.
4. **Make sure one named person can pay a bill on your behalf within two weeks**, without a court. That is a different, smaller thing than being your executor, and it is the thing that saves the box.

I should say plainly that this applies to my own business. MySpawn storage is **$99 a year, billed annually**, so the same arithmetic runs on us as on any storage company: a payment stops, and at some point a stored thing has no one paying for it. The fair question to put to any custodian, including us, is what happens in the months between a missed payment and somebody noticing, and how long they try to reach a second named contact before anything irreversible happens.

Ask it of your storage unit this week. The answer takes one phone call and it is probably a number you will not like.

What is sitting in a unit or a box right now that your family could not replace, and who besides you could pay for it next month?

## #150 [X] "Offline" is half a sentence

**Freshness check:** This angle has not been used in the last 7 days. The word being attacked has never been the subject of an asset: no file in this archive argues about air-gapping. **DISCLOSED ADJACENCY: a 15 October script covered the same company's launch. That script's job was to stop a viewer confusing two products. This one does not mention the product category at all after the first receipt and attacks a claim the entire storage industry makes, mine included.**

**Primary audience:** Men 25-55

**Trend / hook used:** A **15 October 2026** product launch whose central promise is the word in the title.

**Length:** 656 words, measured programmatically.

**ZERO PRODUCT MENTION. No brand of mine, no price, no link, no call to action. This article punches at an idea and names no person.**

---

On 15 October a company launched an archival service whose pitch, as reported, is that data written into synthetic DNA can be kept **completely offline, where no network can reach it.** *(Company announcement, 15 October 2026, read via trade coverage. Not peer-reviewed, not independently verified.)*

I have no view on whether their product works and nothing here is a criticism of their engineering. My quarrel is with the word, which my own industry uses as though it were a complete thought.

**"No network can reach it" is true and it is half a sentence.** The other half is: and neither can you, except through a person.

Air-gapping is a real control that does real work. It removes an entire class of attacker, and in a world of ransomware that is worth paying for. But it does not delete risk. It **moves** risk, out of the network layer and into the physical and human layer, where the failures are slower, less reported, and much harder to insure.

In the network layer your risks are intrusion, exfiltration and encryption, and there is an industry measuring them.

In the physical and human layer your risks are: the building, the equipment, the people who know the procedure, the organisation's continued existence, and whether anybody can still establish that you are who you say you are in year twelve. There is almost no public measurement of any of that.

**Here is the shape of the problem, in a peer-reviewed sentence.** The team that validated the automated store at the UZ KU Leuven biobank wrote that the validation requirement is **subjective** and leaves the implementing biobank **without specific criteria**, which is why they built and published their own protocol. (*Frontiers in Medicine*, DOI 10.3389/fmed.2019.00309, peer-reviewed, published January 2020.)

Sit with that. For offline physical storage of biological material, a published paper says there is no specified criterion for what counts as validated, so each operator picks one.

Compare that to the network side, where "we are compliant" at least points at a named framework with named controls that an auditor who does not work for you can check against. Offline storage frequently has no equivalent, and "offline" gets said in the same confident tone anyway.

**So the honest version of the claim is longer and less impressive, and everybody should be made to say it.** Not "no network can reach it." Instead: *no network can reach it; a small number of named people and one building can; here are our criteria; here is who wrote them; here is when they were last tested; and here is what happens to it if we stop existing.*

That sentence sells worse. It is also the only version that tells you anything.

**The test I would apply to any offline archive, including one I was selling.** Not "can an attacker get in." Ask instead: **can you get it out.**

- Who, by role, can retrieve it?
- What do they require from you to prove you are entitled to it?
- How long does retrieval take, on a bad day rather than a good one?
- What is the documented answer if the organisation is sold, moved, or wound up?
- When was any of that last actually exercised, rather than written down?

An archive you cannot retrieve on demand is not an archive. It is a rumour with a receipt.

**None of this means air-gapping is marketing.** It is the opposite: it is a genuinely strong control, which is exactly why it gets used as a conversation-ender. A real security property becomes a bad argument the moment it is offered as the whole answer, because the listener stops asking about the layer where the actual loss happens.

Every storage business, mine included, has an incentive to let "offline" do more rhetorical work than it can carry. The useful habit, as a buyer, is to hear the word as the beginning of a sentence and ask the seller to finish it.

I spend most mornings checking things I already believe, because that is the job. Today I checked three and all three were wrong, and every one of them was wrong in a file I wrote myself.

**The first.** I had the world's largest biobank down as having completed a move of 11 million samples into a new building, in late July or early August of this year. I had written the date, the funding figure and the freezer capacity, and I had been confident enough to kill a piece of writing on the grounds that the news was too old.

This morning the institution's own pages still read as forward-looking. The samples **will be** moved. The freezers are **being installed**. I could not establish from here that it has happened at all. My note was more certain than the source it came from.

**The second.** I had a 2026 paper filed as being in *Nature*. It is not. It is in *npj Women's Health*, volume 4, article 25, published **22 May 2026**, by J. Y. Lee. That is a Nature Portfolio journal, which is a real and respectable thing and is not *Nature*. The DOI in my file was correct. The journal name was mine, and I had supplied it from the shape of the identifier.

**The third.** I was holding three percentages about how closely lab-grown embryo models resemble real embryos, taken from news coverage ten days ago. Two careful searches today could not reproduce any of them, and the published paper appears to involve more protocols than my note said it did. So the numbers come out, and what is left is the lesson without the figures. ([The paper, for anyone who wants to go further than I could](https://www.cell.com/cell-systems/fulltext/S2405-4712(26)00220-6?rss=yes): *Cell Systems*, 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.cels.2026.101738. I could not open it; our machine has failed to resolve a research domain for 22 straight days.)

Three errors, one morning, all mine, none of them caught by anybody else.

**Here is what I think it actually means, and it is not that I should keep fewer records.**

An archive is not a store of truth. It is a store of what somebody believed at the moment they wrote it down. That is a much weaker thing than people imagine when they talk about leaving something behind, and it is also the only reason archives are worth anything at all. A belief you wrote down can be checked. A belief you carried around in your head cannot.

Every one of today's three errors was findable **only because it had been written down with a date and a source next to it.** The reason I could catch "I said the move was complete" is that the file said when I said it and where I got it. The reason I could catch the journal name is that I had kept the DOI, which was right, next to the journal, which was wrong. The record did not protect me from being wrong. It made being wrong recoverable.

That is the part that bears on anything anyone leaves for anybody else.

Whatever you leave behind will eventually be read by somebody who cannot ask you a follow-up question. They cannot ask what you meant, where you heard it, or how sure you were. So the valuable thing in a family archive is not the confidence of the person who made it. It is the small, unglamorous apparatus around each claim: when, from whom, and how certain.

A box of photographs with nothing written on the back is a beautiful object and a poor record. A single page saying *this was taken in the spring of 1986, I think, at my mother's house, and I am sure about the house and not the year* is worth more than fifty confident captions that cannot be checked.

My notes being the weakest source I read this morning is not an argument for trusting myself less. It is an argument for writing in a way that lets a future reader, including a future me, find the mistake without me there to help.

Most of what I am wrong about, I will never find out about. The only errors I get to fix are the ones I was careful enough to make legible.

What have you written down about your own family that nobody could check if you were not there to explain it?
