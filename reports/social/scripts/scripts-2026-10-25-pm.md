# THREADS-STYLE TEXT POSTS (2)

**1.**
A thing I keep running into when people compare DNA storage options.

Everyone asks about temperature. Almost nobody asks about custody.

Temperature is a solved engineering problem with a known failure mode: the power goes out, the compressor dies, someone gets an alarm. Ambient storage removes that failure mode entirely, which is genuinely why we use it.

But there is a second failure mode nobody advertises, because no provider looks good describing it. The company stops paying the facility. The company gets acquired. The company closes. Your sample is at exactly the same temperature it was yesterday and it is in much more trouble.

So the question that actually sorts providers is not how cold. It is: who holds it, under what accreditation, and what is the documented process if the company is gone.

Ask us that. Ask everyone that.

**2.**
A correction I owe anyone who has read our stuff on ambient storage.

We say ambient storage works. We believe that and we use it. But I went looking tonight for the independent literature behind it, and the oldest thing I found, a protocol study, called it premature to move fully to room-temperature storage and described dry storage as an additional layer of security for frozen samples rather than a replacement.

That study is thirteen years old and the chemistry has moved a long way since. The newest commercial room-temperature preservation product I could find is from an MIT spinout and is labelled research use only.

None of that means ambient is wrong. It does mean "ambient is simply fine" is a stronger sentence than I can currently source, so I am not going to keep saying it that way.

What I can source is the facility, the accreditation and the chain of custody. That is what we will keep selling on.
