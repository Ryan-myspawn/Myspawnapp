### Title
**"Your 'Biological Age' Score: What It Actually Measures"**

### Script

**[COLD OPEN — B-roll: unboxing a generic bio-age test kit, no brand shown]**
There's a number going around the gym group chat. Guys are paying a few hundred dollars, mailing in blood or saliva, and getting back a verdict: "your biological age is 34." Cue celebration or crisis. This video is about what that number actually is, where it's genuinely impressive, and the one sentence from the field's own founder that every buyer should hear first.

**[SECTION 1: THE IDEA — B-roll: DNA methylation animation, dimmer switches on a DNA strand]**
Your DNA sequence doesn't change much with age, but its DECORATIONS do. Chemical tags called methyl groups attach to DNA at specific sites and act like dimmer switches on genes. In 2013, Steve Horvath showed that reading a few hundred of these sites lets you predict a person's calendar age with startling accuracy, across almost every tissue in the body [peer-reviewed, 2013; the "Horvath clock"]. Correlations with actual age run around 0.96. That part is real, replicated, and honestly beautiful science.

**[SECTION 2: FROM PARTY TRICK TO PROGNOSIS — B-roll: cohort-study graphics]**
Predicting the age you already know is a party trick. The interesting move came with second-generation clocks, GrimAge, PhenoAge, DunedinPACE, trained not on calendar age but on health outcomes. In large epidemiological studies, being "older" on these clocks than your birth certificate correlates with higher risk of disease and mortality [peer-reviewed cohort literature; e.g. the clinical-use review in GeroScience/PMC, 2026]. At the POPULATION level, these clocks work.

**[SECTION 3: THE SENTENCE THE ADS SKIP — quote card]**
Now the audit. Here is Steve Horvath himself, the man the first clock is named after, on the state of consumer testing: the field has not yet achieved reference standards, and it's not yet possible to go to different validated labs and get the same readout [as quoted in The Scientist's 2026 piece on why results differ across tests]. Translation: the same blood, sent to two companies, can come back with different biological ages. Technical reliability varies by clock: the best modern versions (PC-clock reformulations, SystemsAge, GrimAge derivatives) achieve excellent test-retest stability, replicates agreeing within about 1.5 years [bioRxiv reliability preprint, 2025, labeled preprint], while cheaper implementations wobble by years. And remember this week's King's College study in *Science*: individual aging trajectories can run opposite to population trends, and a clock score is exactly that, a comparison to a population.

**[SECTION 4: HOW TO BE SMART ABOUT IT — checklist on screen]**
If you buy one anyway, buy like an engineer: (1) demand the clock's NAME (GrimAge2, PhenoAge, DunedinPACE), not "proprietary algorithm"; (2) prefer PC-clock versions for repeatability; (3) treat one reading as noise, trends across repeated tests as signal; (4) never make a medical decision on it alone. A biological-age score is a population statistic wearing your name tag. Useful, motivating even. Not an oracle.

**[CLOSE — direct to camera]**

MySpawn keeps a DNA record, hair and nails, in an accredited vault for $99 a year, because the one lesson under all of this, from the clocks to this week's Science study, is that the future of aging science is longitudinal and personal. Scores change with the model. The record is the constant. myspawn.me.
