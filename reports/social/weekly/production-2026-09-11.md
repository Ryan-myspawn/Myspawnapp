## 1) PODCAST EPISODE PACK

**Freshness check:** the *legitimacy* angle on polygenic embryo screening has never been produced. We have the method explainer ("What a Polygenic Score Actually Tells You," Aug 31) and the access-price episode ("The $50,000 Door," Sep 1). This is neither: it is about what happens when a contested technology gets canonized by a mainstream institutional list, and what the newest data says about the thing being canonized.
**Primary audience: Men 25-55** (29-year-old optimizer lead; 50-year-old estate planner strong on the institutions angle).
**Trend-hook used:** PGT-P named to MIT Technology Review's 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2026 (newly discovered in our Sept 10 sweep), read against the *Nature* personality-GWAS heritability ceiling published this month.

**Title options:**
1. "The Year Embryo Screening Became Respectable"
2. "Nine Percent, and a Place on the List"
3. "How a Technology Gets Canonized"

**COLD OPEN (word-for-word, ~90s):**
"Every January, MIT Technology Review publishes ten breakthrough technologies of the year. It is one of those lists that does something subtler than describe the world: it sorts things into the category of serious. Being on it does not make a technology work. It makes it respectable, and respectability is what unlocks conferences, and capital, and the sentence 'well, MIT called it a breakthrough' in a sales conversation you will have one day in a fertility clinic. This year, one of the ten was preimplantation genetic testing for polygenic disorders. Embryo screening by polygenic risk score. The same technology that, eighteen months ago, was drawing comparisons to Theranos from named bioethicists. So today I want to hold two things next to each other. On one side, a mainstream institution declaring this a breakthrough of 2026. On the other, a study published this month in Nature, the largest of its kind ever run, which found that all the common genetic variants they could identify explain somewhere between five and nine percent of the differences between people on the traits they measured. Both of those things are true at the same time. Understanding how is the most useful hour you can spend on this subject."

**SEGMENT 1 — What the list actually does.**
- Receipt: PGT-P named one of MIT Technology Review's 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2026; the accompanying framing describes startups using AI to analyze whole-genome sequences and generate polygenic risk scores to help select embryos. (Epistemic label: editorial selection by a journalism outlet, not a scientific finding.)
- Stance: the list is a legitimate signal about *attention and momentum*, not about efficacy, and conflating those is the year's most likely error.
- Talking points: what a "breakthrough technologies" list is and is not; why institutional canonization changes markets faster than evidence does; the useful analogue of past list entries that later underdelivered, framed without cherry-picking.
- **CLIP MOMENT:** "The list is not wrong. It just answers a different question than the one you are asking in the clinic."

**SEGMENT 2 — The number that has to sit next to it.**
- Receipts: *Nature*, 2026, peer-reviewed — the largest personality GWAS yet: **1,260 lead variants, 824 novel, 46 cohorts, 611,037 to 1.14 million participants**; common variants explain **4.8-9.3%** of variation (7.6-13.4% within-family), against twin-study estimates of **40-60%**; the paper's own single-cell integration found **no straightforward reductive mapping** from traits to neuron types.
- Stance: this is the strongest possible illustration of the gap between "real signal" and "individual prediction," and it comes from the most favorable possible conditions — a million people and the best methods available.
- Talking points: why a thousand variants means the opposite of a blueprint; why population-level variance explained is not individual predictive power; the difference between disease-risk scores for well-characterized conditions and scores for diffuse traits.
- **CLIP MOMENT:** "A million people, the best methods anyone has, and the answer was single digits. That is not a failure. That is the actual size of the thing."

**SEGMENT 3 — What is fair to say about screening in 2026.**
- Stance: steelman it properly, then state the limit.
- Talking points: the honest case — for some monogenic conditions, embryo testing is established and valuable, and nothing here argues against it; the contested case — polygenic scores for complex traits, where the effect sizes above apply and where professional bodies have been cautious; the portability problem across ancestries; what a family is actually buying when the marketing implies more than the score can carry. Keep every criticism aimed at claims, not at parents making hard decisions.
- **CLIP MOMENT:** "Nobody in this story is a villain. The tests are real, the scores are real, and the marketing is doing something the math cannot."

**SEGMENT 4 — Why a storage company keeps talking about selection.**
- Stance: name our own position and its bias.
- Talking points: we sell the opposite thing — preservation, not selection — so discount us accordingly and check the citations; what we will never offer (any product that ranks or selects); why "keep the record" and "choose the child" are different moral categories and we would rather lose a customer than blur them; the four custody questions applied to anyone in this space, us included.
- CTA, one breath: "Ninety-nine a year, storage only. We do not score anything, and we do not rank anyone."
- **CLIP MOMENT:** "Preservation and selection are not two flavors of the same product. One keeps what exists. The other decides what gets to."

**OUTRO:** restate the two facts side by side; ask listeners what they would need to see before believing a score about a trait.

**3 SHORTS CUT POINTS:** (1) the cold open through "both of those things are true at the same time." (2) the million-people/single-digits line. (3) the preservation-vs-selection close.

## 2) YOUTUBE LONG-FORM SCRIPT (6-10 min) — "There's a Word for It Now: Digital Necromancy"

**Freshness check:** yesterday's episode covered the AI-afterlife category (economics, consent, our boundary). This is narrower and format-distinct: it engages the critique's own vocabulary, concedes what is correct, and states the boundary as a constraint. Never produced as an explainer.
**Primary audience: Men 25-55.**
**Trend-hook used:** honest and stated on camera — this is a vocabulary consolidating in the 2026 literature and commentary, not an event that broke today.

[OPEN — plain screen, the words DIGITAL NECROMANCY typed, then deleted one character at a time.]
"There is a name now for the thing people are afraid of. It shows up in journal articles and in comment sections, and it is aimed at companies that build chatbots of the dead. I want to take the accusation seriously, because I run a preservation company and the lazy version of this conversation ends with us lumped into it."

[BLOCK 1 — what the critics actually say. B-roll: journal pages, no faces.]
"Three arguments, and I think all three are basically right. One: **the dead cannot consent to a new conversation.** Whatever they said while alive, they never agreed to keep talking. Two: **the data was theirs** — their messages, their voice, their face — and inheriting a hard drive is not the same as inheriting permission. Three: **living with a simulation may prolong grief rather than resolve it**, which researchers in grief studies have raised repeatedly. That is not a fringe worry; it is the central one in the 2026 literature."

[BLOCK 2 — the distinction that does the work. B-roll: a waveform, then a text cursor blinking.]
"Here is the line I think actually matters, and it is not about technology quality. **A recording can only ever repeat what someone really said. A model will eventually say something they never said.** That is not a bug you can engineer away; it is what a generative model is for. The moment a system can produce new sentences in a dead person's voice, somebody is going to hear something that person never thought, and no amount of disclaimer text changes what that does to a family."

[BLOCK 3 — the one version that survives the critique. B-roll: a phone recording a conversation.]
"There is a case that clears the bar, and it is worth naming because it is instructive: a person who, while alive and competent, deliberately records themselves for a specific purpose. Consent is explicit and prior. The reported example of a terminally ill man recreating his own voice for his wife is the strongest version of this category precisely because he chose it, in advance, for a known recipient. Everything gets murkier as you move away from that."

[BLOCK 4 — our boundary, stated as a constraint on us.]
"So here is ours, in public, where you can hold us to it. We store DNA records. In the Memory Vault we store what a member actually recorded — their real video, their real voice — sealed to a date they chose. **We do not generate speech in anyone's voice. We do not build a model of a person. We do not offer a conversation with the dead.** ECHO, the thing on our site, speaks as a fictional descendant from 2126 — a character, explicitly not a real individual — and if we ever blurred that, you should say so loudly."

[BLOCK 5 — the honest close.]
"I am not going to pretend our corner is morally superior by accident. It is a choice, and it costs us a product line that would probably sell. Ninety-nine dollars a year, storage only. If a preservation company ever tells you it can give someone back, the word for that is in the title of this video."
