### SEGMENT 1: What a preprint is, and what it is not (4 to 5 min)

- **The receipt, exactly:** medRxiv preprint **2025.10.30.25339175**, posted **30 October 2025**. Journal version ***NAR Molecular Medicine*** **2026;3(1):ugag011**, DOI **10.1093/narmme/ugag011**, **PMID 41797988**, **PMCID PMC12961428**, e-pub **3 February 2026**.
- **Stance:** a preprint is not a fake paper and it is not a lesser paper. **It is the same work before anybody independent has had a turn.** Most of what you read about science online is one of the two and almost nothing tells you which.
- **Talking point with the science receipt:** the gap here was about **three months and four days** from preprint to journal. That is fast. **The point is not that it took long. The point is that during those three months the paper was true-or-false in exactly the same way, and only the LABEL changed.** Which is why the label is the only part a reader can use.
- **MARKED CLIP MOMENT:** *"A preprint is not a rumour. It is a paper that has not been argued with yet."*

### SEGMENT 2: The bigger error, which nobody caught because it sounded like a win (6 to 7 min)

- **What the paper actually found, with the numbers the 6 September script never gave:** frozen samples carried **5,466 artefactual C>T mutations per million bases against 3,307** for ensilicated samples, **p = 0.006**, described by the authors as **up to 65 percent more** damage in the frozen arm, **concentrated at CpG dinucleotides.** On a routine lung panel the methods agreed and ensilication **detected every actionable variant, including at low variant allele frequencies.**
- **Now the four limits, and this is the segment's spine:** the sequencing arm ran **28 DAYS**. The material was **32 matched FFPE tumour and normal pairs**, which is fixed clinical tissue, **not blood and not swabs**. **The comparison is the authors' own.** And **the mechanism is contested**: one reading credits the silica matrix with shielding DNA from hydrolysis, and a separate commentary in a Springer journal argues the frozen arm's extra damage comes from **continued hydrolysis driven by co-stored fixation byproducts**, which is a claim about handling rather than about silica.
- **Stance, and it is against us:** we used a four-week result on fixed tissue to support a storage product sold on a horizon of decades. **The label was wrong by four months. The extrapolation was wrong by about thirty years.**
- **The receipt that can actually carry a decades claim, and it is a different source entirely:** the American newborn blood-spot archive. **Paper cards held at room temperature for decades, from which peer-reviewed work has recovered genome-wide genotypes and methylation profiles.** That is an existence proof with real years on it. **The 6 September explainer had that receipt in it, in the section BEFORE the mistake, and then stacked the weaker evidence on top as though it strengthened the case.**
- **MARKED CLIP MOMENT:** *"If your evidence runs for twenty-eight days and your product runs for thirty years, the gap is not a detail. The gap is the entire question."*

### SEGMENT 3: Who found it, and why that is the uncomfortable part (4 to 5 min)

- **Stance:** this was not caught by a sourcing review, a fact-check pass or a second reader. **It was caught by a grep that exists to stop us publishing the same idea twice.** A rule written to protect against repetition found a citation error as a side effect.
- **Talking point:** that is **luck, not process.** A process that catches errors only when they happen to collide with a different rule will catch the errors that collide and miss the ones that do not.
- **What changed today, concretely, and it is small on purpose:** the watchlist entry now carries **four limits that must be restated every time that paper is cited**, and an explicit line that **the paper cannot carry a decades claim.** Not a promise to be more careful. **A constraint attached to the specific sentence that went wrong.**

### Title
**"'Presented At' Is Not 'Published In' (and in two weeks you will need to know the difference)"**

### Script

**[COLD OPEN: direct to camera]**
In thirteen days, the largest fertility medicine meeting in America opens in Baltimore. **The ASRM Scientific Congress, 24 to 28 October, at the Baltimore Convention Center.** For about two weeks afterwards, your feed is going to carry headlines that start with the words "a new study shows."

**Most of them will not be about a study you can read.**

**[SECTION 1: what a conference abstract actually is. B-roll: a single page, word count visible]**
A conference abstract is a summary, usually a few hundred words, submitted months before the meeting. It gets screened by a programme committee for relevance and quality. **That screening is not full peer review.** There is no reviewer sending it back for another experiment. There is no methods section for anybody to pick apart, because there is usually no methods section at all.

**None of that makes abstracts bad.** They are how a field talks to itself at speed. **They are just not the thing a headline implies when it says "a new study."**

**[SECTION 2: the number, and it is a range from several sources. B-roll: three figures appearing one at a time]**
Here is the part almost nobody is told.

**A Cochrane methodology review pooling 425 reports and roughly 307,000 conference abstracts found that only 37 percent of all abstracts were ever published in full, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 35 to 39 percent.** For abstracts describing randomised controlled trials the figure was higher, **60 percent, confidence interval 52 to 67.** And its survival analysis found that **even after ten years, only 46 percent of all abstracts and 69 percent of RCT abstracts had been published.**

A narrower meta-analysis of eye and vision conferences, **19 reports and 12,261 abstracts, found 38.0 percent published in full**, confidence interval 31.7 to 44.3, and concluded that **more than 60 percent were not published in full within two years.** A single-conference study of a psychiatry meeting found **27 percent within five years.**

**[ON SCREEN: honest labelling, read aloud]** **These are systematic reviews and meta-analyses, so tier one, and they are drawn mostly from clinical medicine rather than from reproductive medicine specifically. The exact figure varies by field, by study type, and by how long you wait before counting. We are giving you a range from three sources rather than one number, because that is what the evidence supports.** **And we could not establish the Cochrane review's publication year from our sources, which for a load-bearing statistic is a real limitation and we are telling you rather than rounding it off.**

The direction, though, is not in dispute across any of them. **Somewhere between a quarter and two fifths of what gets presented at a conference ever becomes a paper.**

**[SECTION 3: the four things that change between the podium and the page. B-roll: side-by-side pages]**
When an abstract does become a paper, the paper is often not the same.

**Numbers move**, because the abstract was written on an interim dataset months before the talk. **Sample sizes change.** **Conclusions soften**, because reviewers ask for the hedges the abstract did not have room for. **And authors drop off or get added.**

So "presented at ASRM" and "published in a journal" are two different claims about the same work at two different stages of its life. **The first one tells you a committee thought it was worth an audience. The second tells you that strangers tried to break it and could not.**

**[SECTION 4: the filter, four questions. B-roll: each question on screen]**
So in two weeks, when the headlines come:

**One. Does the article say "presented at" or "published in"?** If it says presented, you are reading about an abstract.
**Two. What is n?** An abstract will usually tell you, and it is usually smaller than you expect.
**Three. Is there a DOI or a PMID?** A conference abstract often has neither. **A paper you can look up is a paper somebody can check.**
**Four. Is anyone selling something adjacent to this result?** Including, and I mean this, us.

**[CLOSE: direct to camera]**
I am not telling you to dismiss conference news. **Half of what matters in a fast-moving field shows up at a meeting first, and waiting for the paper means waiting two years for the 37 percent that arrive at all.**

I am telling you that **"presented at" and "published in" are different words because they describe different amounts of scrutiny**, and that the people writing headlines will use them interchangeably.

**And the preserve-now thesis, stated without overpromising: none of this changes whether a sample you keep today is still readable in thirty years.** That question is answered by decades of newborn blood-spot archives, not by anything anybody presents in Baltimore. **MySpawn is $99 a year, storage only, and we will have no announcements out of ASRM, because we are not doing any research.** Sources in the description. **Including one we corrected today.**
