# 1. PODCAST EPISODE PACK (18-22 min)

**Freshness check:** Never produced. The log has a **corporate-continuity custody explainer** (Sep 9) and an **AI-afterlife category episode** (Sep 10), but both examine *other companies* failing. **No asset we have ever made turns that question on MySpawn.** That inversion is the episode.
**Primary audience: Men 25-55**, with the 50-year-old estate planner in the lead seat for once.
**Trend / hook used:** Connecticut's genetic-privacy law takes effect **October 1, 2026**, sixteen days out, and creates a consumer **property right** in a biological sample held by a testing company. That is a dated, imminent, external reason to ask the question now rather than a manufactured one.

### Title options
1. **"What Happens To Your Sample If We Go Under"**
2. **"The Question I Have Been Asking Other Companies"**
3. **"Sixteen Days From Now, Your Spit Becomes Property"**

### Cold open (word for word, 75-85 seconds)

> For about four weeks I have been pointing at other companies.
>
> 23andMe filed for Chapter 11 on March 23rd, 2025. Its business went through a court-approved sale to a nonprofit called the TTAM Research Institute for 305 million dollars, approved that July. Along the way the attorneys general of California, Nevada, Utah and Massachusetts told their own residents to go and delete their data, which is not a thing attorneys general usually have to say about a company that still exists.
>
> HereAfter AI put a shutdown notice on its homepage in July 2026. That company recorded people answering questions about their lives, in their own voices, for their families. By August the site would not load. The iOS app had not been updated since September 2023. The only route anyone had to get their own recordings back was to send an email and hope.
>
> I have used both of those stories in our own marketing. They are true and they make our argument for us, and every time I used one I was implying something about us that I have never actually written down.
>
> So today I am going to ask it the other way round. If MySpawn fails, what happens to your sample?
>
> I do not think you should take my word for the answer. I think you should make me show you the document. And I should tell you now: right now, there isn't one.

**[CLIP MOMENT: the last nine words. "And I should tell you now: right now, there isn't one."]**

### Segment 1 (4-5 min): The law is about to make this concrete

- **The dated fact:** Connecticut's SB 4 genetic-privacy provisions take effect **October 1, 2026**. They create a consumer **property right** in the biological sample given to a direct-to-consumer genetic testing company and in the results of testing on it, plus rights to access, delete, require **destruction of the physical sample**, and revoke consent. Enforcement sits **solely with the Connecticut Attorney General**; there is no private right of action. *(State statute; effective date verified via multiple law-firm summaries, Sept 15 2026.)*
- **Context, not hype:** South Dakota's SB 49 took effect **July 1, 2026**. One 2026 law-firm survey counts **thirteen states** with direct-to-consumer genetic-testing regulation. *(A survey, not an official registry. Say so on air.)*
- **Federal:** two bills, neither law. **S.863**, the Genomic Data Protection Act, would make access, deletion and sample destruction an FTC-enforced requirement. **S.1916**, the Don't Sell My DNA Act, would amend the bankruptcy code so genetic information cannot be sold, leased or used in a bankruptcy unless every affected person consents **in writing, after the case begins**. S.1916 was introduced and referred to the Judiciary Committee on **May 22, 2025**. A House companion followed on **July 17, 2025**. Both are still in committee, roughly sixteen months later.
- **Stance:** the interesting bill is the bankruptcy one, because it is the only one written by people who noticed that the danger is not malice. It is insolvency. A company does not have to want to sell your DNA. It only has to run out of money in a jurisdiction where a judge decides what the assets are.
- **Talking point to land:** a privacy policy is a promise made by a company. A bankruptcy court is a venue where promises get re-read by someone who does not work there.

### Segment 2 (5-6 min): The four questions, asked about us

Take the custody heuristic we have used on everyone else and run it on MySpawn, on air, in order.

1. **Where is the sample physically?** A GenVault-operated biorepository in New Jersey, ambient temperature. GenVault is ISO and CAP accredited and FDA-registered. **Say the qualifier every time: those are GenVault's credentials, not ours, and FDA registration means the facility is registered with the agency, not that the FDA has approved or endorsed anything.**
2. **Who can order it destroyed?** You can. That is the easy one and it is the one every company answers loudly.
3. **What happens if MySpawn stops existing?** This is the one. The storage is operated by a third party under a commercial relationship. If we stop paying, that relationship is what decides the outcome, and the customer is not a party to it. **[CLIP MOMENT]**
4. **What would a good answer look like?** A named successor or escrow arrangement, a funded period of storage independent of our revenue, a published notification procedure, and a default that returns or destroys rather than transfers.
- **The honest position:** we do not have that document today. Most companies our size do not. That is an explanation, not a defence.

### Segment 3 (4-5 min): Why the absence of the document is normal, and why that is the problem

- **The pattern in both precedents:** in neither the 23andMe case nor the HereAfter case did the *material* fail. Genetic data did not corrupt. Voice recordings did not degrade. **The company degraded, and the material was downstream of the company.**
- **The industry's standard answer is a promise in a terms-of-service document**, which is exactly the instrument that gets reinterpreted at the moment it matters.
- **The demand side is already awake.** An executive said **nearly two million** 23andMe customers requested deletion. *(Reported statement via trade press, not a filed figure. Label it on air.)* Whatever else that number means, it means ordinary people understood the custody question without anyone explaining it to them.
- **Stance:** the correct response to a category-wide trust problem is not better marketing copy. It is a document that costs us something to write.

### Segment 4 (3-4 min): What I am proposing, and what it costs us

- **The commitment, FOUNDER-GATED:** *"Before the end of this year we will publish a continuity plan: what happens to stored samples if MySpawn ceases to operate, who is notified, and what the default action is. It will be a page on the site, not a paragraph in the terms."*
- **FALLBACK LINE IF NOT AUTHORISED, use verbatim instead:** *"I am not going to announce a policy on a podcast. What I will say is that this document is the thing I would want to see from any company holding something of mine, and that we do not have it yet. Hold us to that."*
- **Why it costs something:** publishing it converts a vague reassurance into a testable claim, and it invites the obvious follow-up question about who funds the storage if we are gone. That is the right question and we should be made to answer it.
- **The competitive point, made once and quietly:** every company in this adjacency should be asked this. We are asking ourselves first because we are the ones who kept using other people's failures in our marketing.

### Outro CTA
No product pitch. One ask: **"Whoever holds something of yours, a testing company, a photo service, a storage locker, send them the third question. What happens to my thing if you stop existing. Then tell me whether you got a document or a sentence."**

### Three Shorts cut points
1. **"And I should tell you now: right now, there isn't one."** The cold-open button. Strongest 20 seconds in the pack.
2. **"The material never failed. The company failed, and the material was downstream of the company."** Segment 3 open.
3. **The four questions, delivered straight to camera as a list, 45 seconds.** This is the reusable one.

# 2. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM SCRIPT (7-9 min): "The Only Complete Copy"

**Freshness check:** Never explained here. The log contains a second-genome explainer (Sep 3, mitochondrial DNA) and a genetic-nurture explainer (Sep 13). Programmed DNA elimination is a different phenomenon and has never appeared in any MySpawn asset before this morning.
**Primary audience: Men 25-55**, optimizer lead.
**Trend / hook used:** *Nature Reviews Genetics* published a review titled "The mechanistic and evolutionary diversity of programmed DNA elimination" on **September 10, 2026**, five days ago.

**[COLD OPEN, 0:00-0:30]**
**[B-ROLL: slow push on a still image of a sea lamprey.]**
> This animal throws away about a fifth of its own genome before it is finished being an embryo. Not by accident. On schedule, in the same cells, every generation.

**[0:30-1:30] What the phenomenon is**
**[B-ROLL: simple two-branch animation, one branch labelled BODY, one labelled EGGS AND SPERM.]**
> Early in development an embryo sorts its cells into two destinies. Some become the body. Some become eggs or sperm. In several unrelated lineages, at a fixed moment, the body-destined cells physically discard part of the genome. Chunks of chromosome. Sometimes entire chromosomes. In some organisms, most of the genome.
> A review published in *Nature Reviews Genetics* on September 10th, 2026 lays out how many different mechanisms do this: chromosome breakage, whole-chromosome elimination, whole-genome elimination. **[ON-SCREEN CITATION: journal, title, date.]**
> **[HONEST LABEL ON SCREEN: "Peer-reviewed review article. Summarises existing findings; not a new experimental result."]**

**[1:30-3:30] The numbers**
**[B-ROLL: each figure typed on screen as it is said.]**
> Sea lamprey: roughly **20 percent** of the genome removed from the cells that will build the body. What goes includes germline-specific chromosomes and hundreds of protein-coding genes whose jobs are germline jobs.
> Songbirds: most of them eliminate an entire germline-restricted chromosome from the body lineage during early development. In the zebra finch that chromosome is more than **10 percent** of the genome, over **150 million base pairs**.
> Some ciliates: more than **90 percent** of the genome eliminated from the somatic lineage.
> And the first person to see any of this was Theodor Boveri, in the **1880s**, watching roundworm chromosomes get chopped under a light microscope, decades before anybody knew what DNA was.

**[3:30-5:00] The idea underneath**
**[B-ROLL: a single master file icon, then several lossy copies exported from it.]**
> Here is what I find hard to stop thinking about. The organism keeps **one complete copy of its own information**, in a cell lineage that does nothing else, and deliberately sends a **reduced copy** to the part of itself that lives and eats and dies.
> The body is the working copy. The germline is the master.
> And this was arrived at independently, in lampreys, in songbirds, in roundworms, in ciliates. Which suggests the archive-versus-working-copy distinction is not a metaphor we imported onto biology. It is a solution biology keeps finding.

**[5:00-6:15] What we do not know, stated plainly**
**[B-ROLL: plain text on black.]**
> Nobody has settled why it evolved repeatedly. There are three main candidate explanations and all three are hypotheses: silencing transposable elements, resolving conflict between the germline and the body, and protecting body tissue from genes that are only safe to switch on in the germline.
> And the part that matters most for anyone watching this: **mammals do not do it.** Your body cells carry the same genome as your germ cells. So nothing in this video is a fact about you. It is a fact about how information gets partitioned across the tree of life.
> **[ON-SCREEN: "Not applicable to humans. Stated because it matters."]**

**[6:15-7:30] Preserve-now close, without overpromising**
> I am not going to pretend this is a reason to buy anything, because it is not. Nothing in this paper says a stored sample does anything for your health, your longevity or your children.
> What it does is describe an architecture: keep one clean copy, let the working copies wear out. That is what we are for. MySpawn stores a DNA record and sealed video messages at **99 dollars a year, billed annually**, with the samples held at a GenVault-operated biorepository in New Jersey. GenVault is ISO and CAP accredited and FDA-registered. **Those are GenVault's credentials, not ours, and FDA-registered means the facility is registered with the agency, not that the FDA approved or endorsed anything.**
> We store. We do not sequence you, score you, or offer anything reproductive. The lamprey is not an argument for our product. It is just the oldest version of the idea.

**[OUTRO CARD]** "Review: *Nature Reviews Genetics*, September 10, 2026. Authorship verify pending."

**OPEN ITEM BEFORE PUBLISHING:** the review's authorship is ambiguous across index results, with one naming P. D. Currie and another crediting the Duharcourt lab at Institut Jacques Monod. **The script deliberately never names an author.** Resolve before the outro card is finalised.
