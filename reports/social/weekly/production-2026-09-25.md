## 1. PODCAST EPISODE PACK

**Freshness check:** **NEW LANE, and the distinction is real rather than cosmetic.** The log holds four episodes about *finding* an error: 14 Sep (rhetoric beats fact-checking), 20 Sep (the StoryFile revision), 22 Sep (the Receipt Check pilot), 24 Sep (the retrieval layer). **None is about what to do when you find a number is wrong and cannot obtain the right one.** That is a method problem, not a discovery problem, and it is the one this week actually hit twice.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend / hook used:** Two things that happened today: a competitor's price in our own published article turned out to be contradicted by our own files, and a claimed refutation of one of our published posts collapsed under a single check.

### Title options

2. "The Error That Was in Their Favor"

### Cold open, word for word (about 85 seconds)

> Today I found out that a price we have published since August is probably wrong by about half.
>
> Not ours. A competitor's. We have been telling people that a live-cell banking service costs around a hundred dollars a year to store your cells. Our own production notes, written in August and again in September, say sixteen dollars a month. **Sixteen a month is a hundred and ninety-two a year.** Those cannot both be true, and they have been sitting in the same archive, contradicting each other, for five weeks.
>
> An independent check today came back with sixteen a month, and separately a hundred and ninety a year. So the hundred was almost certainly ours. A transcription error from the eighteenth of August that walked into a published article and stayed there.
>
> Here is the part I want to talk about. **That error was in their favor and against us.** If their storage is really a hundred and ninety, then our ninety-nine dollars a year is less than half their annual cost, and we have spent five weeks describing ourselves as roughly at parity with something we are half the price of.
>
> Every correction we have made on this channel so far has cost us an argument. This one gave us one back, and we still nearly missed it, **because nothing itches when a number is unflattering to you.**
>
> And then the second problem, which is the actual subject of today: **I still cannot tell you what the right number is.** Their pricing page will not load from where I work. I do not know if those figures are US dollars or Canadian. I do not know if the two prices describe the same plan.
>
> So: what do you do with a number you know is wrong and cannot replace?

### Segment 1: The discrepancy, read out as a table (about 4 minutes)

**Stance:** State it flatly, with dates, and do not perform contrition.

- **The two families of figure in our own archive.** Watch files of **18 and 19 August**, and our published article, say **"about $100 a year."** Production notes of **20 August** and longposts of **3 and 5 September** say **"about $16 a month."**
- **$16 a month is $192 a year.** The archive contained a doubling and nobody noticed for five weeks.
- **Today's independent check:** roughly **$16 per month to store 50 follicles**, and separately **$190 per year per individual**. **Both consistent with the $16 line.**
- **Epistemic labels:** all of these are **secondary sources and provider-page summaries**, not a pricing page we have read ourselves.
- **[CLIP MOMENT]** *"Two numbers, same archive, five weeks, one of them double the other. Nobody noticed, and the reason nobody noticed is that the small one made us look worse."*

### Segment 2: Why we did not just write in the new number (about 5 minutes)

**Stance:** This is the segment that justifies the episode. **Replacing an unverified number with another unverified number is not a correction. It is a second guess wearing the first one's clothes.**

- **The rule already existed and we wrote it down on 23 September**, about a different company's pricing: swapping one unverified figure for another **repeats the error rather than fixing it.** Today is the first time it has been applied to a number that helps us.
- **Three things we genuinely do not know**, and any single replacement number would hide all three. **One: the currency.** The company is Toronto-based. **US or Canadian dollars is not established**, and at current rates that is a large difference. **Two: the plan.** "$16 a month for fifty follicles" and "$190 a year per individual" may be two products. **Three: the source.** Their own pricing page is **blocked by our own network**, which is our container refusing the connection and **is not a fact about them.**
- **What we did instead:** the published article now carries **a range with all three caveats attached** and a dated correction note, and the internal file marks the old figure **contested** rather than fixed.
- **The general form, and it is usable by anyone:** when you find a number is wrong, you have three honest options. **Publish the range. Publish the uncertainty. Or remove the number.** **Substitution is not on the list unless you have actually seen the source.**
- **[CLIP MOMENT]** *"A correction that replaces one thing you did not check with another thing you did not check is not a correction. It is a second draft of the same mistake."*

### Segment 3: The same day, the opposite error, caught in one search (about 4 minutes)

**Stance:** Direction matters. Today produced one error that flattered a competitor and one near-error that would have flattered our own humility.

- **The setup.** A 2026 guide states flatly that a major short-video platform offers **no memorialization and no advance legacy planning**, and that the only family option is requesting deletion. **We have published a long post and an episode built on that platform's legacy-contact restrictions.** If the guide were right, both would be wrong about a feature existing.
- **One search settled it.** The platform does prompt users to choose someone to manage the account after death, and that contact can **update the bio, accept new friend requests, download copies of posts, manage privacy settings and request deactivation.** Users can instead choose delete-after-death. **That is our restriction list. Our post stands.**
- **What IS contested is memorialization specifically**, which is a different feature. **We assert nothing about it, and nothing we have published depends on it.**
- **The standing rule that covers both cases:** the archive outranks the search summary, and **a summary that contradicts a verified entry is the error until proven otherwise.**
- **[CLIP MOMENT]** *"I was one paragraph from announcing that our own published work was built on a feature that does not exist. The check cost one search."*

### Segment 4: The direction of an error tells you where to look (about 4 minutes)

**Stance:** The practical takeaway, and it generalizes well beyond us.

- **Errors do not distribute evenly.** They survive longest where nobody has an incentive to look. **A number that makes you look bad gets checked by you. A number that makes you look good gets checked by your rivals. A number that makes your rival look good and you look bad gets checked by nobody at all.** That is the gap the $100 sat in for five weeks.
- **Yesterday's version of the same lesson, from the other side:** we overstated the size of one of our own errors, in the direction that made us look rigorous for admitting it. **Same mechanism, opposite sign.**
- **The habit worth stealing:** once a week, go through the numbers you repeat and **sort them by who they flatter.** Check the ones that flatter your competitor first, because nobody else is going to.
- **[CLIP MOMENT]** *"Check the numbers that make you look worse. Everyone else is busy checking the ones that make you look better."*

### Outro CTA (about 45 seconds)

> Two questions, and they are free. **Who does this number flatter?** And **have I actually seen the source, or have I seen somebody describing the source?**
>
> We are at myspawn.me, storage is ninety-nine dollars a year, and the only thing I can tell you with confidence today is that it is less than half what one alternative charges annually, **and that I am saying "one alternative" instead of a name and a figure because I have not read their page myself.**

1. **Cold open, the first 40 seconds**, ending on *"nothing itches when a number is unflattering to you."*
2. **Segment 2's clip moment**, *"a second draft of the same mistake"*, over B-roll of the corrected paragraph.
3. **Segment 4's clip moment**, the sort-by-who-they-flatter line. **This is the one that travels.**

## 2. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM SCRIPT (about 8 minutes)

### "The Fertility Story That Is Not IVG"

**Freshness check:** **Never produced as an explainer.** Fertilo appears as **one segment inside the 20 August episode**, thirty-six days ago, framed as an IP-strategy story about a patent license. **The biology has never been explained here.** The 10 September IVG explainer is definitional and about a different technology entirely; today's Trend Radar sharpened the rail precisely because a single search answer returned both stories together.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend / hook used:** Today's sweep returned the IVG story and the Fertilo story in one answer, which is exactly how the two get conflated in public.

**[COLD OPEN, to camera]**
There is a fertility technology with a first live birth, a Phase 3 trial and a peer-reviewed paper behind it. And almost every time it gets mentioned alongside "eggs from stem cells," people conclude something that is not true. **The stem cells here are not becoming eggs. They are becoming the cells that sit around an egg.** That difference is the entire video.

**[SECTION 1: WHAT ACTUALLY MATURES AN EGG. About 2 minutes.]**
*[B-roll: simple animation, an oocyte surrounded by a shell of smaller cells.]*
An egg does not mature alone. Inside the ovary it is wrapped in somatic cells, principally **granulosa cells**, and maturation depends on constant chemical conversation between the egg and that surrounding tissue.
**That conversation is what traditional in vitro maturation is missing.** Take an immature egg out and try to mature it in a dish and you have removed the half of the system that was doing most of the talking. **That is the accepted biological rationale for why conventional IVM has historically underperformed**, and it is the problem this product is aimed at.

**[SECTION 2: WHAT THE COMPANY ACTUALLY MADE. About 2 minutes.]**
*[B-roll: iPSC colony; the Cell Stem Cell paper's title page on screen.]*
**Label first: this part is peer-reviewed.** *Cell Stem Cell* published **"Development of human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived ovarian support cells as a clinical-grade product for in vitro fertilization."**
The method: take induced pluripotent stem cells, drive them with **three transcription factors** into **granulosa-like cells**, and co-culture those with the retrieved egg. The company calls them **ovarian support cells**. **They are the audience, not the performer.** The egg is still the patient's own egg.
**[ON SCREEN: THE STEM CELLS BECOME THE SURROUNDINGS, NOT THE EGG.]**

**[SECTION 3: THE CLAIMS, SORTED BY WHO IS MAKING THEM. About 2 minutes.]**
*[B-roll: three labeled columns.]*
**Peer-reviewed:** the cells can be made to clinical grade and they improve maturation in vitro.
**Company-announced, and label it out loud:** the company reports replacing around **80 percent of hormone injections** and shortening a cycle from **10 to 14 days down to 2 or 3**, and reports **blastocyst formation of 86.7 percent against 60 percent, and euploid blastocysts at 77 percent against 30 percent, compared with standard IVM.** **These are company figures, from a 2025 announcement, compared against IVM rather than against conventional IVF.** That comparison choice matters and is not hidden by them, but it is usually dropped by everyone repeating it.
**Regulatory fact:** there is a **Phase 3 trial, NCT06858111.** A Phase 3 is a test, not a result.
**Reported:** a **first live birth using the technique**, announced in **December 2024**, delivery in Lima, Peru. **One birth is an existence proof, not an outcome rate.**

**[SECTION 4: THE LINE THAT KEEPS GETTING CROSSED. About 90 seconds.]**
*[B-roll: two labeled boxes, one crossed out.]*
**This is not in vitro gametogenesis.** IVG means making a gamete from a non-reproductive cell. **This matures an egg the patient already had.**
**And the rail this channel does not move: IVG has produced no mature human gametes.** Early-stage cells, yes. A functional human egg or sperm made from scratch, no.
**Why the conflation happens** is easy to see: both stories contain stem cells and eggs in the same sentence, and one company works in both areas. **Two true stories share four words and get merged.**

**[CLOSE, to camera. About 45 seconds.]**
The preservation point is narrow and I will keep it that way. **The technique improves what can be done with an egg that already exists.** It does not create one, and nothing here changes what a stored sample is or is not.
What it does illustrate is the pattern this channel keeps landing on. **The method got better. The biology did not change.** Our part is storage: hair and nails, an accredited third-party facility, ninety-nine dollars a year, no claim about what future methods will read from it. **Only that it will still be there to try.** myspawn.me.
