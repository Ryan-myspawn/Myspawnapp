## 1. PODCAST EPISODE PACK: "Nobody Broke In"

**Freshness check:** New subject and a new lane. **No episode in the log covers data security, export controls or research infrastructure.** Nearest neighbors are deliberately different: 18 September was **platform death policies** (who is allowed in after you die), 20 September was **acquisition as custody risk** (who is allowed to buy them), 22 September was **our own verification process**. **This is the first episode about what is allowed OUT.** Today's blog and longposts #88 and #91 carry the argument in short form; **this is the twenty-minute version and it is built on the institutional story rather than the consumer checklist.**
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend / hook used:** UK Biobank's phased reopening, beginning September 2026, after five months closed.

### Title options
1. **"Nobody Broke In"**
2. **"What Can Leave"**
3. **"The Airlock"**

*(Recommend 1. It states the surprise in three words and makes the listener ask the question the episode answers.)*

### Cold open, word for word, 85 seconds

> One of the most-used health research resources in the world spent five months this year closed to scientists. Not down for maintenance. Closed.
>
> In April, it found that data from its analysis platform was being offered for sale online. Deidentified, participant-level, and already exported. It stopped researcher access completely and it stayed stopped until this month.
>
> Now here is the part I want to spend twenty minutes on. When they published what they were rebuilding, it was not a bigger lock.
>
> When the platform reopens, researchers can go in, and compute, and download nothing at all. A manual output checking system arrives later this year. An automated one is targeted for early 2027. They are building an airlock.
>
> Think about what that means about the incident. **Nobody broke in.** The data had already been exported, by people who were allowed to have it, through a door that was open to them on purpose.
>
> That is a completely different theory of what a leak is. And it means the question almost none of us asks about anything we keep anywhere is not "who can get in."
>
> It is: what can leave, who approves it, and is there a record that it happened.
>
> I have spent a month telling you to ask the first question. Today I am going to argue I was asking half of it.

### Segment 1: what actually happened, carefully (4 to 5 min)

**Stance:** precise, unsensational, and fair to the institution.

- **April 2026.** Deidentified participant-level data **already exported from the Research Analysis Platform** found offered for sale online. **Researcher access paused.**
- **What I am not going to say on air, and why.** A figure of roughly 500,000 affected participants circulates widely. **That is security-press reporting, not the institution's own statement, and the episode works without it.** Likewise, **how the data got out has not been publicly established and I am not going to guess on a microphone.**
- **CLIP MOMENT:** *"I could give you a number here. It would get shared more. I am not going to, because it is not their number and I cannot stand behind it."*

### Segment 2: the fix, item by item (5 to 6 min)

**Read the list slowly. The list is the argument.**

- **No downloads at all at reopening**, phased from September 2026.
- **Manual Output Checking System**, limited capacity, later in 2026.
- **Automated Output Checking System**, targeted early 2027.
- **An airlock**, rolled out with the reopening.
- New project applications **suspended until late 2026**.
- **Every existing project extended by six months**, compute credits too.
- A **£200,000 independent security review**, commissioned and its recommendations accepted.
- Access on reopening limited to institutions **fully compliant on mandatory training, annual reports, and data deletion for projects that have ended.**

- **Talking point, and it is the one nobody covers:** that last condition is the hardest of the lot. **A project ends and the copy it was given does not go anywhere by itself.** Somebody has to delete it and somebody has to check. **An institution that never enforced that was not less secure on paper. It just had more copies of itself in the world than it could count.**
- **CLIP MOMENT:** *"Every item on that list is about the way out."*

### Segment 3: the consumer mirror image, and the reversal (5 to 6 min)

**Stance:** the same mechanism, opposite valence. This is the segment that earns the episode.

- For a company holding other people's data, **export is the risk.** For you, **export is a right**, and a stronger one than most people know.
- **California Privacy Rights Act**, in force since **1 January 2023**: electronic disclosures must be **"portable and, to the extent technically feasible, in a readily useable format"** you can move elsewhere **"without hindrance."** And the CPRA goes beyond the CCPA: **the business must transfer it directly to another entity if you ask.**
- Then the gap. **Google Takeout** writes photo dates, GPS and descriptions into a **companion .json beside each image** rather than into EXIF; **silently skips shared albums**; splits a 100GB library into roughly **19 archives** with **links expiring in about a week**; and runs **1.5 to 2 times** the reported library size because of year-versus-album duplication. **Apple's privacy export excludes iCloud Photos and Drive entirely**, gives **limited message metadata rather than messages**, takes **three to seven days** and keeps links live **14 days**. **Meta's Transfer Your Information** does genuine direct service-to-service transfer, which is the strong form of the right actually working.
- **CLIP MOMENT:** *"The law says without hindrance. What arrives is nineteen zip files and a link that dies in a week."*

### Segment 4: the turn, including on us (3 to 4 min)

- The listener's takeaway is one added question, asked of everyone: **what can leave, in what shape, who signs off, and what gets deleted when the reason for holding it ends.**
- **Turn it on MySpawn and do not soften it.** We store a DNA record. **We do not currently have a published page answering that question about ourselves.** A draft was written yesterday; it is not live. **Say that on air.**
- **Fairness beat, and it belongs at the end rather than the start:** they disclosed, they paused rather than carrying on, they commissioned an independent review and accepted it, they compensated every affected project with six months, and they published a staged plan with dates on it. **Most organizations manage about two of those.**

### Outro CTA

> MySpawn stores a DNA record for ninety-nine dollars a year. It is storage. Today's ask is not that you buy anything. It is that you request an export from one service you already use, on a normal day, while it costs you nothing, and find out what actually arrives. Then ask us the same question and hold us to the answer.

2. Segment 2, the list read straight, ending on *"Every item on that list is about the way out."*
3. Segment 3, *"The law says without hindrance. What arrives is nineteen zip files and a link that dies in a week."*

## 2. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM EXPLAINER (7 to 9 min): "Two Halves, Two Laws"

**Freshness check:** **No explainer in the log covers probate mechanics, estate documents or any legal instrument.** Every prior explainer is a biological or evidentiary mechanism: autophagy, partial reprogramming, genetic nurture, programmed DNA elimination, the blood-testis barrier, IVG definitions, cohort-versus-individual effects, evidence grading. **This is the first legal-mechanism explainer and the subject entered the archive yesterday.**
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55.
**Trend / hook used:** This week's research into what a will actually reaches.

**Thesis:** a family archive sits across **two different legal regimes**, and the standard estate tool reaches only one of them.

**[0:00-0:40] Cold open.** *B-roll: a hand setting a shoebox on a table, then a phone beside it.* **VO:** "These are both your family archive. One of them your will can assign in about twenty minutes. The other one it does not reach at all, and almost nobody finds that out until it matters."

**[0:40-2:00] Part one: there is no rule about who gets the archive.** *B-roll: slow pan over unlabeled boxes.* No birth-order law, no statutory default. The keeper self-selects, usually during a house clearance. **On-screen: NO DEFAULT KEEPER.**

**[2:00-4:00] Part two: the tool almost nobody uses.** *B-roll: a pen, a single sheet, handwriting a short list.* **Uniform Probate Code section 2-513**, "separate writing identifying devise of certain types of tangible personal property", adopted in most US states. Enacted for example as **Hawaii 560:2-513, Massachusetts 190B 2-513, New Mexico 45-2-513, Idaho 15-2-513.**
Conditions on screen as he reads them: **tangible personal property, not money** · **signed by you** · **items and recipients described with reasonable certainty** · **may be written before or after the will** · **may be altered afterwards.**
**The two that matter:** it can be changed **with a pen**, and it covers only property **the will has not already given specifically.**
**Drafting beat:** *"My photographs" is thin. "The photograph albums in the hall cupboard" is not. Write it as if the reader has never met your family, because eventually that is who reads it.*

**[4:00-6:00] Part three: the half it does not touch.** *B-roll: the phone again, screen dark, then a cloud-sync icon.* **RUFADAA**, adopted in nearly every state, governs **the data, not the device.** The phone is tangible property through normal probate. **The photographs inside it, and in the account it syncs to, are digital assets under a separate regime.** **And the default runs the opposite way to everyone's assumption: unless access is explicitly granted in the will or trust, the presumption is that your executor does not have it.**
**On-screen, held: SILENCE IS NOT PERMISSION.**

**[6:00-7:30] Part four: what to actually do.** Four steps, on screen as bullets: **say out loud who the keeper is** · **separate who stores it from who gets copies** · **write the signed list** · **write the second instruction for the digital half, explicitly.**

**[7:30-8:30] Close, preserve-now thesis without overpromising.** **VO:** "None of this is about us. We store one narrow thing and the document that governs it is the same kind of document. The point is that a family archive has two halves, they are governed by two different laws, and the one everybody writes down is the one that was already going to survive."
**Honest label on screen, held for four seconds: THIS IS STATE LAW AND IT VARIES. CHECK YOURS.**

**PRE-SHOOT CHECK:** **name no state as having adopted section 2-513 beyond the four verified in the script**, and **claim no adoption count**, because none was verifiable. Say "most US states" and stop.
