### Segment 1 — How the bag actually works (the no-pump insight)

### Title
**"Your Sperm Has an Editor (and It Has Opinions)"**

### Script

**[COLD OPEN — B-roll: time-lapse clock, then cell-division animation]**
Here's a sentence from a Nature paper that rearranged how I think about inheritance: some harmful mutations in sperm aren't there by accident. They're there because they WON. This is a video about the strangest natural-selection arena in your body, what a landmark study actually measured, and, before anyone panics, why the honest numbers are less scary than the headline and more interesting.

**[SECTION 1: THE ARENA — B-roll: testis stem-cell niche diagram]**
Your sperm are produced continuously by stem cells that divide for decades. Every division risks a typo. So sperm from a 55-year-old carries more mutations than sperm from a 25-year-old; that part was known. The new insight is that the stem cells COMPETE. A mutation that makes a stem cell divide a little more aggressively spreads through the testis over the years, even if that same mutation would be harmful in a child. Biologists call it selfish spermatogonial selection. The sperm factory has an editor, and the editor optimizes for the factory, not for the next generation.

**[SECTION 2: WHAT THE STUDY DID — B-roll: paper on screen, numbers highlighted]**
In October 2025, scientists at the Wellcome Sanger Institute and the TwinsUK cohort published the deepest look yet [PEER-REVIEWED LABEL: Nature, Oct 8, 2025]. Using a duplex method called NanoSeq, they sequenced 81 sperm samples from men aged 24 to 75. The clean findings: mutations accumulate at about 1.67 per year per genome copy. Forty genes showed significant positive selection, 31 of them newly identified. And the bottom line that made headlines: in middle-aged and older men, roughly 3 to 5 percent of sperm carry a disease-causing mutation in the protein-coding genome, a 2-to-3-fold amplification over what random error alone would produce.

**[SECTION 3: THE HONEST COUNTERWEIGHT — checklist on screen]**
Now the part the scary headlines skip, and the reason this video exists. One: 3 to 5 percent of sperm means 95-plus percent are not carrying such mutations, and a single sperm's defect must still survive fertilization, development, and everything else; most fathers at every age have healthy children. Two: this study measured sperm, not birth outcomes; the path from a mutation in a cell to a condition in a child is long and heavily filtered. Three: paternal age is one risk factor among many, and it's continuous, not a cliff. The study is a landmark of measurement, not a verdict on anyone's life plan. [LABEL: "rates, not destiny"]

**[SECTION 4: WHAT IT CHANGES — B-roll: research-pipeline graphic]**
What it genuinely changes is science's picture of inheritance: the genome your children draw from is not a static file, it's a living tissue being edited by competition, in a direction nobody chose. Expect this to reshape genetic counseling research, and expect a wave of products riding the fear. When you see them, apply this channel's standard audit: named data, absolute risks, peer review.

**[CLOSE — direct to camera, framing rail verbatim]**
And here's our disclosure, stated as plainly as we can: MySpawn stores DNA records, hair and nails in an accredited vault, $99 a year. We do not bank sperm, we do not offer anything reproductive, and nothing about this study is a reason to buy what we sell. If this topic touches your family planning, that's a conversation for a doctor, and fields like fertility preservation belong to companies that do that work. We covered this because it's the best measurement of a hidden clock anyone has published, and knowing how the clock works beats being sold to by people who hope you don't. myspawn.me.
