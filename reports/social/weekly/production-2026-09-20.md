## 0. EPISODE REVISION: "Nobody Did Anything Wrong" (produced 19 September, not yet shot)

**What changed.** Today's competitor dive established that **StoryFile did not stay dead.** It filed a voluntary **Chapter 11 petition on 5 May 2024** in White Plains, New York (case **7:2024bk22398**), declaring **$1.5M in assets against $10.5M in liabilities**, and then **emerged**: its assets were **acquired by Key 7 Investment Company**, with trade coverage dated **3 March 2025** reporting fresh leadership and new products. **In 2026 it is operating and expanding**, with reported deployments at Washington DC's **Museum of the Bible**, a forthcoming presence at the **Medal of Honor Museum**, and a trial with **Inflatable Films and Blue Card in New York City public schools** letting students talk to a Holocaust survivor. *(Court record for the filing; trade press for the emergence and deployments. **Why the episode cannot go as written.** Segment 2 lists StoryFile among **"four companies in about two years that have shut down, pivoted or paused."** Chapter 11 is a **reorganisation, not a closure**, and this one worked. "Filed for Chapter 11" is literally accurate. **Listing it among four failures without saying it survived is misleading by omission**, and this channel spent the week telling other people not to do exactly that.

**REPLACEMENT COPY FOR SEGMENT 2, word for word:**
> "I told you four companies. That was not quite right, and the correction is more interesting than the thing I got wrong.
>
> Three of them shut down, left the category or went quiet. The fourth filed for Chapter 11 in May 2024, with one and a half million in assets against ten and a half million in liabilities, and then came out the other side. An investor bought the assets. Today it is running in museums and going into schools.
>
> So the range of outcomes is not one thing. It is two. And I am not sure the second one is the comfortable one.
>
> Because when a company like this survives, your material survives **as an asset somebody purchased.** Nobody signed up with an investment company they had never heard of. They signed up with a small team who wanted to keep their father's voice. The obligation transferred. The relationship did not."

**NEW SEGMENT 2B, and it is the strongest thing in the pack (4-5 min):**
- **The question we have never asked in any asset:** everybody asks what happens if a company shuts down. **Almost nobody asks who is allowed to buy it.**
- A shutdown is visible. **A transfer is not.** Your files move, the terms may change, and the first you hear of it may be a rebrand email.
- **The practical version:** before you hand anything to a service, look for what its terms say about **assignment on acquisition or bankruptcy**. Most consumer terms permit transfer freely. **That clause is doing more work than any privacy policy on the same page.**
- **The turn on us, and it must stay in:** *"We use a third-party accredited biorepository. That is a real answer to 'what if MySpawn stops', and it is only half an answer to 'who could end up holding this', because a third party can be sold too. I do not have a complete answer. I have a partial one and the beginning of a document."*
- **[CLIP MOMENT]** "Everybody asks what happens if they shut down. Almost nobody asks who is allowed to buy them."

## 1. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM EXPLAINER (6-10 min)

### "Two Numbers That Are Both One Percent"

**Freshness check:** The 21 August explainer compared two sperm-count literatures; this is a different measure and a different job, because the subject is **a statistical distinction rather than a contested literature.** No fleet asset has covered cohort effects. **It uses no quote from anybody**, which matters, because the Reaction Fuel item that prompted it is blocked on an unverified date and this script does not depend on it.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** "Testosterone is dropping one percent a year" is circulating again, and there are two different one-percent findings that get collapsed into one.

**Script beats with B-roll:**

**[COLD OPEN: to camera, plain background.]**
> You have heard that men's testosterone is falling about one percent a year. That is true. There is also a completely different finding, also about one percent, that is also true, and almost everybody repeating the first one is actually thinking of the second. Untangling them takes four minutes and changes what you would do about it.

**[B-ROLL: a simple line going down, drawn on paper, top-down.]**
> **Number one: the individual decline.** As a man ages, his total testosterone tends to fall gradually, on the order of about one percent a year from somewhere around his thirties. **That is ageing. It happens inside one person over time.** *(Longitudinal ageing literature. Peer-reviewed, and the exact rate varies by study and by which measure you take.)*

**[B-ROLL: three separate dots at different heights, not a line.]**
> **Number two, and this is the interesting one: the cohort decline.** In 2007, Travison and colleagues published *"A Population-Level Decline in Serum Testosterone Levels in American Men"* in the ***Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism***. It drew on the **Massachusetts Male Aging Study**, with three separate data-collection waves over seventeen years: **1987 to 1989, 1995 to 1997, and 2002 to 2004.**
>
> **They found an age-matched decline of roughly 1.2% per year.** Age-matched. That means: a fifty-year-old man in 1988 had **higher** testosterone than a comparable fifty-year-old man in 1996. *(Peer-reviewed.)*

**[TO CAMERA.]**
> Read that again, because it is the whole video. **This is not men getting older. This is fifty-year-olds getting different.** Same age, later decade, lower number.

**[B-ROLL: a list of factors being crossed off.]**
> The obvious explanations were tested. The age-matched declines **remained essentially unchanged** after controlling for **chronic illness, general health, medications, smoking, body mass index, employment and marital status**. A companion paper the same year looked specifically at the relative contributions of ageing, health and lifestyle. **Whatever is driving the cohort effect, the usual suspects did not account for it.** *(Peer-reviewed.)*

**[B-ROLL: later paper on screen.]**
> Later work looked at the same question in national data. Nyante and colleagues examined trends in sex hormone concentrations in US males from **1988 to 1991 and 1999 to 2004**. **Read it yourself rather than taking my summary**, because this is exactly the kind of literature where the summary is where the error enters. *(Peer-reviewed.)*

**[TO CAMERA.]**
> **So why does the distinction matter practically?**
>
> If what you have heard is the first number, the individual one, the implied story is *you are running out of time*. That story sells supplements and clinics.
>
> If it is the second, the cohort one, the implied story is *something changed in the environment between 1988 and 2004 that nobody has pinned down*. That is a research problem, not a personal countdown, and **nothing you buy this week addresses it.**
>
> Both numbers are real. **They point in completely different directions, and the one being sold to you is usually the wrong one.**

**[HONEST LIMITS, to camera.]**
> What I am not telling you: that your own levels are fine, that they are not, or what to do about them. **I am not a doctor and this is not medical advice.** If you want to know your number, the test is cheap and a physician can interpret it. **A statistic about a population has never told anybody anything about themselves.**

**Preserve-now close, without overpromising:**
> **Storage has nothing to do with any of this.** We keep a genetic record. It is not a treatment, it confers no health benefit, no access to any trial, and it does not touch your testosterone or your fertility. **I am saying that plainly because this is the exact lane where companies like mine are tempted to imply otherwise.**
>
> The honest connection is the habit. **When a number is quoted at you, ask what it is a number about: one person over time, or a whole population across decades.** That question costs nothing and it works on us too.

## 2. EVERGREEN BANK (new format)

**Format: "One Object, Three Owners."** A single small object stays in frame the whole time while three different pairs of hands take it, one after another. No cuts away. **No format in the archive tracks a single object through changing custody**; recent evergreens checked individually and excluded: mirror-shot (12th), whiteboard (14th), wall-calendar (15th), Flop-Core (16th), retail-aisle-walk (17th), Read It Out Loud (18th), Cards Face Down (18th PM), Black Screen (19th), Overlay Audit (19th), Backwards From Five (19th), The Ladder (19th PM).

- **Hook (first 2 seconds):** "Watch the object, not the hands."
- **Beats:**
  1. A sealed envelope on a table. One pair of hands sets it down carefully.
  2. Second pair of hands takes it. No explanation. The envelope is unchanged.
  3. Third pair takes it. Still unchanged. Still sealed.
  4. VO, flat: "Nothing happened to the envelope. Everything happened to who is holding it."
  5. "When people worry about a company that stores things for them, they picture it closing. The commoner outcome is that it changes hands and nobody tells you in a way you notice."
  6. "So the question is not only what happens if they stop. It is who is allowed to take over, and whether the promise travels with the object."
  7. Close, hands withdraw, envelope alone in frame: "Ask that before you seal anything. Including with us."
- **Why it works for men 25-55:** it is a demonstration, not an argument, and the emotional register is procedural rather than sentimental.
- **Risk flag:** **no company named, no implication that any specific service has been sold.** The envelope must be plain, with nothing written on it.
