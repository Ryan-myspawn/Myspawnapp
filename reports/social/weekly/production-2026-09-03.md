### Title
**"You Have a Second Genome (and It Only Comes From Your Mother)"**

### Script

**[COLD OPEN — B-roll: cell animation, camera pushes past the nucleus]**
Everyone knows about the genome in the nucleus: 23 chromosome pairs, three billion letters, the big file. Almost nobody thinks about the other one. There's a second genome in nearly every cell you own, it's tiny, it's circular, it breaks all the inheritance rules you learned in school, and this summer it became front-page news without most people noticing. This is the mitochondrial genome, and here's your tour.

**[SECTION 1: THE SPECS — B-roll: circular DNA diagram next to a chromosome for scale]**
The mitochondrial genome is a loop of DNA about 16,569 letters long, carrying 37 genes (per the NIH's genetics reference; textbook-level biology throughout this section). Compare: the nuclear genome is three billion letters and roughly 20,000 genes. So why care about the rounding error? Location and copy number. This DNA lives inside your mitochondria, the structures that burn your food into usable energy, and you don't have one copy like the nuclear genome. You have hundreds to thousands of copies per cell, because each cell carries many mitochondria and each mitochondrion carries its own DNA.

**[SECTION 2: THE WEIRD RULES — B-roll: family-tree animation, maternal line highlighted]**
Rule one: it's maternal-only. Sperm mitochondria are essentially eliminated at fertilization, so your mitochondrial DNA is your mother's, and hers is her mother's, in an unbroken chain. Rule two: because there are thousands of copies, you can carry a MIX of versions in the same body. That mix has a name, heteroplasmy, and you heard it this summer if you followed the news from Newcastle: eight babies born after mitochondrial donation, where doctors moved parents' nuclear DNA into donor eggs with healthy mitochondria [NEJM, July 2025, peer-reviewed; the babies' residual mutation levels, undetectable to about 16 percent, are heteroplasmy percentages]. Now you know what that number actually measured.

**[SECTION 3: WHY IT SURVIVES WHEN EVERYTHING ELSE IS GONE — B-roll: hair shaft macro, old bone]**
Here's where it gets practical for this channel. When forensic scientists identify decades-old remains, or read DNA from a rootless hair shaft, they usually read mitochondrial DNA. Not because it's magic, but because of arithmetic: one nuclear copy per cell versus hundreds of mitochondrial copies means that when time destroys 99 percent of everything, "hundreds" still leaves survivors and "one" usually doesn't. It's the same reason ancient-remains labs so often start with mtDNA. Copy number is a preservation strategy that biology invented before we did.

**[SECTION 4: WHAT IT CAN AND CANNOT TELL YOU — honest-limits section]**
What mtDNA gives you: deep maternal-line ancestry, identity confirmation, and a window on a specific class of inherited disease. What it cannot give you: it's 37 genes, so no traits, no health panorama, no substitute for the nuclear file. Anyone selling mitochondrial ancestry as your whole story is selling a bookmark as a book. [LABEL ON SCREEN: "mtDNA = maternal line only; ~0.0005% of your DNA."]

**[CLOSE — direct to camera]**
So here's the preservation takeaway. Your body already runs a two-genome archive, and the copy-number trick is why part of your record can outlast the rest. When we store hair and nails at MySpawn, that redundancy is part of what's being banked: keratin-locked tissue carrying both signal types, in an accredited vault, $99 a year, storage only. No promises about what future tools read from it; the honest claim is just that a file with thousands of copies beats a file with one. Your mother's line already proved that. myspawn.me.
