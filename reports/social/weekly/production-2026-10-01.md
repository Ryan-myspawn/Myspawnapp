### SEGMENT 1: The three that flattered us (4 to 5 minutes)

**Stance:** describe the shape precisely, name nobody.

- **26 September.** A competitor's pricing was wrong in our own published article, **and the real defect was not the number: it was the explanation we had invented for the number.**

- **Marked clip moment:** *"Every single one of those made a competitor look more expensive. Not one of them made us look worse. That is not luck, that is a sampling problem."*
- **Talking point with the receipt:** **in each case the corrected figure was higher than the one we had printed.** The sentence that matters: **an archive is not a random sample of reality. It is a sample of what somebody bothered to check.**

### SEGMENT 2: Why a flattering error survives (4 to 5 minutes)

**Stance:** mechanism, not confession.

- **Nobody disputes a number that makes them look good.** If our article says a competitor charges less than they do, the competitor is not reading our article, and our reader has no way to know.
- **Compare that to a number that makes us look bad**, which gets challenged by the first customer who notices.
- **So the error rate is identical in both directions and the DISCOVERY rate is not.** **The errors that survive are the ones that flatter you.**
- **Worked example from our own week, and it is not about pricing at all.** On Monday a report in this operation said it was the **"sixth consecutive week"** of a broken data feed. **Counting the file's own entries, it was the fifth.** The number had been double-incremented twice. **Nobody was ever going to challenge the count of weeks something had been broken**, and it only got worse each week, which felt right.
- **Marked clip moment:** *"The easiest number in any file to get wrong is the one that only ever goes up and that nobody has a reason to dispute."*
- **And it happened again this week**, in the same operation, four days later: **a second run on one calendar day incremented a drought counter that a morning run had already set.** **Same defect, caught faster.**

### SEGMENT 3: The one that ran the other way (4 to 5 minutes)

**Stance:** this is the payoff and it must be told without triumph.

- **The instruction was written yesterday. The check ran today.** It was a scheduled look at the third provider named in that same published price row.
- **What it found: the provider may not be in our market at all.** Our article prints a **dollar** range and names three companies. Today's check returns that company describing itself as **the largest provider in a different country**, with its prices quoted **in a different currency.**
- **And here is the restraint, which is the actual lesson:** our article links a domain that suggests a separate local entity. **We cannot open either page from our systems.** **So we flagged it, and we did not correct it**, because calling it a correction would repeat the exact overclaim we corrected on Monday.
- **Marked clip moment:** *"The finding is worth less than the fact that we went looking for it. We asked for an error pointing the other way and got one in a day. That tells you how many are still in there."*
- **Honest limit, stated on camera:** **this is one candidate, not a proven error**, and three days from now it may turn out our article was right all along. **That outcome would not make the method wrong.**

### SEGMENT 4: Run it yourself (3 to 4 minutes)

**Stance:** give the listener the tool, with no product in it.

1. **Take any record you keep** where some entries make you look good and some do not: a CV, an expenses file, a performance review, a price list, a set of claims on a website.
2. **Sort the errors you have ever caught in it by who they flattered.** Not by size. **By direction.**
3. **If they are evenly split, your checking is working.** **If they lean one way, the lean is your blind spot and not your luck.**
4. **Then check three entries that currently flatter you**, chosen at random, and see what comes back.
- **Marked clip moment:** *"You do not need an auditor. You need to notice that all your mistakes point the same way."*
