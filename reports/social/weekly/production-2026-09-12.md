### SEGMENT 2 — The number the audience would normally demand (5-6 min)
**Receipts (all peer-reviewed, all on today's blog):**
- Karavani et al., *Cell*, 2019: selecting the top-scoring embryo out of five gives an expected gain of **~2.5 IQ points or ~2.5 cm of height**; widening to an unrealistic 50 embryos raises it only to ~4.5. https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(19)31210-3
- Same paper's real-world check: across 28 families with ten or more adult children, the highest-polygenic-scoring child **was usually not the tallest**.
- Variance in liability explained (eLife 2021): ~6% Crohn's, ~7% schizophrenia, ~9% type 2 diabetes. https://elifesciences.org/articles/64716
- Scores are validated on unrelated people and **shrink inside a family** — population stratification, assortative mating and genetic nurture do not transfer to sibling comparison, which is the only comparison PGT-P makes.
- Real-world IVF modelling: most cycles yield one usable embryo or none, so the selection question frequently never arises.

**Stance:** actual-science, delivered flat. The 2.5-point figure is the entire argument and needs no adjectives.
**CLIP MOMENT:** "Two and a half IQ points, best of five. If a supplement company showed you that effect size, you'd close the tab."

**CLIP MOMENT:** "A breakthrough list is a bet on the future. A professional society's opinion is a read on the present. Don't buy the second one with the first one's confidence."

# 2. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM EXPLAINER (8-9 min)
## "Partial Reprogramming, Explained: The Thing Longevity People Are Actually Excited About"

**Freshness check:** Never produced. The log's science explainers cover base editing, meiosis, the
second genome, sample-type stability, hachimoji DNA, ambient-vs-frozen storage, the paternal clock,
IVG definitions, polygenic-score method, accreditation, and cord-blood economics. **Cellular
reprogramming — the single hottest theme in longevity funding — has never been explained on the
channel.** Pegged to yesterday's MIT TR Innovators list.
**Primary audience: Men 25-55.**
**Trend-hook used:** MIT Technology Review's Innovators Under 35, published September 11, 2026 — nine
biotech honorees, and the headline entry is a partial-reprogramming therapy for vision loss now in
human testing.

**SCRIPT**

[B-ROLL: a photograph being un-creased by hand, flattening back toward smooth. Return to this image at the close.]

"In 2012 Shinya Yamanaka won a Nobel Prize for showing you can take an ordinary adult cell and push it all the way back to an embryonic-like state using four factors. That's reprogramming. Full reprogramming makes a cell forget what it was — which is useful for growing tissue, and catastrophic inside a living body, because a liver cell that forgets it's a liver cell is a tumour risk.

Partial reprogramming is the interesting version. The idea is that you apply those factors briefly — you push the cell part of the way back, so it resets age-associated markers, and then you stop before it forgets its job.

[B-ROLL: dial being turned a quarter turn and released, not swept to the end.]

Whether that actually reverses aging, or just resets the markers we happen to measure, is the open question. Hold onto that distinction — it's the one the marketing tends to lose.

**What is actually established.** [ON SCREEN: PEER-REVIEWED] In 2020, Yuancheng Lu and colleagues published work showing a reprogramming therapy restored vision in aged and injured mice. That's a real, peer-reviewed animal result. In 2024, scientists at Altos Labs published work showing targeted partial reprogramming of age-associated cell states extended lifespan in mice. Also peer-reviewed, also mice.

**What is in progress.** [ON SCREEN: IN HUMANS — NO PUBLISHED RESULT] Yesterday, MIT Technology Review's Innovators Under 35 list reported that a near-identical version of that vision therapy is now being tested in people with eye disease. That is genuinely significant. It is also, right now, a trial with no published outcome — and I want to be precise, because this is where honest coverage and hype diverge. 'In humans' is not 'works in humans.' A trial is a question, not an answer. The people running it would tell you the same thing.

**What is money, not results.** [ON SCREEN: FUNDING ≠ EVIDENCE] Reprogramming is the highest-conviction theme in longevity investing. NewLimit raised $435 million in June 2026 at around a $3.1 billion valuation, and its first human study is expected in **2027**. Retro Biosciences has raised over a billion dollars cumulatively and announced in August 2025 that AI models had made reprogramming roughly fifty times more efficient — an engineering-throughput claim, not a clinical one. Altos remains the best-capitalised name in the field.

Those numbers tell you what serious people believe. They do not tell you that it works. Valuation is not evidence, and I'd say that about my own company.

[B-ROLL: five index cards laid out, dates written on them: 2027 · 2029 · 2031 · 'unknown' · then a sixth card set slightly apart, reading 'today'.]

**So here's the honest state of play.** The best mouse data is real. The first human trials are running. The earliest additional human studies are a year or more out. Nobody in this field is promising you a treatment this decade, and the ones who are, are not the ones doing the work.

**And here's the part that concerns me and is the reason I'm making this video.** Every item on that shelf has a date on it, and none of the dates is now. Which means the rational move for anyone genuinely interested in this is boring: stay alive, stay healthy, and don't lose the inputs.

[B-ROLL: return to the photograph, now flat.]

Reprogramming, if it works, works on cells that are still there. Everything in this field operates on a living person. None of it is retroactive, and none of it reconstructs someone from a record.

That's the limit of my own pitch, so let me state it rather than imply it: preserving a DNA record does not get you a therapy. It is not a medical intervention, it does not put you in a trial, and anyone in my industry telling you a stored sample is a ticket to future medicine is selling you something I won't sell you. What a record does is much smaller and much more certain: the information about who you were, biologically, still exists later. That's it. Seven minutes at a kitchen table, $99 a year, stored at an accredited facility.

The clever people are working on the hard part. The record is the part that's finished the day you do it."

**Honest-labeling summary for the description:** Lu 2020 vision study — peer-reviewed, mice. Altos 2024 lifespan study — peer-reviewed, mice. Human eye trial — running, no published result. NewLimit/Retro/Altos figures — company and investor announcements, not clinical evidence. No claim is made that reprogramming works in humans.
