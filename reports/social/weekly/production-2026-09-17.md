### Segment 1 (4-5 min): What an unlabelled archive actually is

- The prank's mechanics, described without reproducing them. Old photo, vintage filter, plausible claim, no way to falsify it.
- **The honest test for the listener:** go and find one photograph in your house where you can name every person in it. Most people can do this for very few, and the number drops off a cliff two generations back.
- **Stance:** an unlabelled archive is not a record. It is an object that produces the *feeling* of a record. Those are not the same, and the difference only becomes visible at the moment somebody needs it.
- **Talking point:** the print often carries more information than the digital copy. Handwriting on the back of a photograph is routinely discarded at exactly the moment a family decides to scan the collection. **[CLIP MOMENT]**

### Segment 2 (5-6 min): What the people who do this professionally actually do

Receipts, because this segment is the useful one and it should be citable.

- **Start filenames with the date in YYYY-MM-DD order.** That single choice makes a collection sort chronologically on every system, forever, with no software.
- **Keep filenames short**, commonly guided at **under 25 characters**, and avoid spaces and special characters, which break transfers between systems.
- **Put meaning in metadata, not the filename.** Dates, places, captions, keywords and people belong in **EXIF and IPTC** fields, which travel invisibly with the file. The filename is for sorting. The metadata is for meaning. **Most people have this exactly backwards.**
- **Use the same word for the same thing every time.** Pick "Nana" or "Grandma" and never switch; consistency is what makes a collection searchable later.
- **3-2-1:** three copies, two kinds of media, one off-site. A single drive in a cupboard is one house fire from being nothing.
- *Sources: Digital Preservation Coalition guidance on file naming and formats; Penn State University Libraries personal digital archiving; University of Michigan Library digital archiving metadata. Institutional guidance, not a study.*

### Segment 3 (4-5 min): The turn, and it points at us

- **The same question applies to anyone holding anything of yours, including a DNA record.** How do you know the thing in the freezer, or the drawer, or the facility, is the thing you think it is?
- That question has a name in the industry: **chain of custody**. It is the difference between a sample and a labelled sample, and it is the entire reason accreditation schemes exist.
- **What I can say about us, precisely and no further:** MySpawn stores a DNA record and sealed video messages, **$99/year billed annually**, at a **GenVault-operated biorepository in New Jersey**. GenVault is **ISO and CAP accredited and FDA-registered**. **Those are GenVault's credentials, not ours, and FDA-registered means the facility is registered with the agency rather than approved or endorsed by it.**
- **What I am deliberately not going to do on air is describe our internal custody procedures in detail I have not verified line by line.** If I did that here it would be marketing dressed as disclosure, which is the exact failure this episode is about. The honest version is: ask us, in writing, and hold the answer against the four questions.
- **Stance:** a company that cannot tell you how it knows your sample is yours has the same problem as a photograph with no caption. It just costs more.

### Segment 4 (3-4 min): Forty, not four thousand

- Nobody labels four thousand photographs. The advice fails because it assumes a project.
- **Do forty.** A labelled forty beats an unlabelled four thousand, permanently, and the four thousand are still there if someone later wants them.
- The same logic runs through the Memory Vault: one sealed video against a chosen date beats an unsorted camera roll, not because the video is better footage, but because somebody said what it was and when it opens.
- Close: the work is never the scanner, the drive or the facility. **The work is the sentence that says what the thing is.**

### Outro CTA
No product ask. One instruction: **"Find one photograph tonight where you can name everyone. Then find one where you can't, and call the person who might know, while you still can."**
