## 1) PODCAST EPISODE PACK

**Title options:**

**COLD OPEN (word-for-word, ~85s):**
"In 2019 a journalist named James Vlahos started a company called HereAfter AI. The idea was genuinely lovely: it interviewed you about your life, saved your actual voice answering actual questions, and let your family ask you things later and hear you reply. Not a simulation. Your recordings. Thousands of people did it, and some of them did it because they were running out of time. In July of this year, HereAfter announced it was shutting down. As of last month the homepage doesn't load. The iOS app hasn't been updated since September of 2023. And if you want your father's voice back, the only known way to get it is to send an email to a company that is winding down and hope somebody is still reading that inbox. Nothing was lost to a fire, or a flood, or a hard drive failure. The audio files were fine the entire time. What failed was the institution holding them. Meanwhile the category HereAfter helped start is projected to be worth about five billion dollars this year. So today: what people are actually buying when they buy a piece of the digital afterlife, what the ethicists are worried about, and the line I think matters most, which is the difference between keeping a record and building a ghost."

**SEGMENT 1 — What actually happened to HereAfter's users.**
- Receipts: shutdown confirmed on the company's homepage, July 2026; as of August 2026 hereafter.ai does not load and the iOS app's last update was September 2023; retrieval reported to be by email only. Founded 2019 by James Vlahos. (Epistemic label: reported/documented from public sources and the company's own site; not a company statement to us.)
- Stance: no gloating, and no blaming the founders — this is a structural failure mode, not a moral one.
- Talking points: the gap between "we're shutting down" and "the site is gone" is where people lose things, and it is usually months long; export deadlines that arrive while you are not paying attention; why an app that stopped being updated in 2023 was already a warning; the specific cruelty of this category, where the thing you lose is a voice.
- **CLIP MOMENT:** "The recordings never degraded. The company did."

**SEGMENT 2 — The five-billion-dollar category.**
- Receipts: digital-afterlife services projected around **$5B in 2026**, spanning griefbots, AI voice memorials, holographic avatars and VR reconstructions; significant adoption in China and South Korea, including a widely-streamed Korean VR reconstruction of a deceased child.
- Stance: demand is real and not stupid. Grief is the oldest market there is.
- Talking points: three distinct products people conflate — (a) archival: your real recordings, kept; (b) synthetic: a model trained to talk like you; (c) interactive memorial: an avatar for others to visit. Very different failure modes, very different consent questions. Note that a growing academic literature is now examining griefbot ethics and responsible innovation.
- **CLIP MOMENT:** "Grief is the oldest market on earth. What's new is that it now ships software updates."

**SEGMENT 3 — The consent problem nobody solved.**
- Stance: this is where the category is genuinely unresolved, and we should say so rather than pretend our corner is above it.
- Talking points: the dead cannot consent to a new conversation; a model trained on someone's outputs will eventually say things they never said; who owns the voice, who can switch it off, who inherits the subscription; the terminally-ill case (a man recreating his voice deliberately, in advance, for his wife) is the *strongest* version because consent is explicit and prior — that distinction is doing all the ethical work.
- **CLIP MOMENT:** "A recording can only repeat what you said. A model will eventually say something you didn't."

**SEGMENT 4 — Records, not resurrection.**
- Stance: draw our own boundary in public, including the part that constrains us.
- Talking points: what we do — store a biological record and, in the Memory Vault, the actual videos and voice notes a member chose to record; what we do not do — simulate a person, generate speech in anyone's voice, or claim any of it brings anyone back; ECHO is deliberately a widget speaking as a *fictional descendant from 2126*, not a reconstruction of a real individual, and if we ever blur that we should be called on it; the four custody questions apply to us as hard as to anyone in this episode, and HereAfter is the reason they exist.
- CTA (one breath): "Ninety-nine a year, storage only. We keep records. We don't build ghosts."
- **CLIP MOMENT:** "If we ever start selling you a version of your father that talks back, cancel your subscription."

**OUTRO:** the three-product distinction restated; ask listeners whether they'd want a recording, a model, or neither.

**3 SHORTS CUT POINTS:** (1) cold open through "the only known way to get it is to send an email." (2) the recording-vs-model line from Segment 3. (3) the cancel-your-subscription close.

## 2) YOUTUBE LONG-FORM SCRIPT (6-10 min) — "IVG Doesn't Mean What You Think It Means"

**Freshness check:** the iPSC-vs-testicular-stem-cell distinction has never been explained in any format. Adjacent pieces (polygenic scores Aug 31, meiosis wall Sep 2) cover different mechanisms. This is the definitional piece that protects every other claim we make in the lane — and today's research showed our own standing phrasing is challengeable.
**Primary audience: Men 25-55** (IVG-for-men is a standing preferred rotation direction).
**Trend-hook used:** a May 13, 2026 company announcement most people missed, and the vocabulary problem it exposes.

[OPEN — desk, two petri dishes labelled with masking tape: "SKIN CELL" and "TESTICLE".]
"Two labs, two completely different starting points, and the press calls both of them the same thing. If you only learn one distinction in reproductive biotech this year, make it this one, because it's the difference between a technology that would change everything and a technology that helps a specific group of men."

[BLOCK 1 — IVG proper. B-roll: cell-reprogramming animation.]
"IVG, in vitro gametogenesis, in the strict sense means this: take an ordinary cell — skin, blood, whatever — reprogram it backwards into a stem cell, then coax it forward into a gamete. Egg or sperm, from a cell that was never going to be either. Here is the status, and it has not changed: researchers have made human primordial germ cell-like cells from iPSCs. Nobody has finished the job. **No lab has produced a mature, functional human gamete from an induced pluripotent stem cell.** That sentence is precise on purpose."

[BLOCK 2 — the other route. B-roll: the second dish.]
"Now the other dish. Some men produce no mature sperm but still have the stem cells that are supposed to make them. The goal there isn't to reprogram anything — it's to take those existing testicular stem cells and finish the maturation in a lab that the body didn't finish. That's in vitro spermatogenesis. Different starting material. Different problem. And in May of this year, a company called Paterna Biosciences announced it had done exactly that: functional human sperm grown outside the body from a testicular biopsy, used to create early test embryos as a safety check."

[BLOCK 3 — how to hold that claim. B-roll: a journal page, then an empty one.]
"Here is how much weight to put on it. **It is a company announcement. It has not been published in a peer-reviewed journal. No independent lab has replicated it. It is not cleared for clinical use.** That is not an accusation; it's the actual epistemic status, and the coverage I read said so too. If it holds up under peer review and replication, it is a major result for male-factor infertility. Until then, the honest word is 'claims'."

[BLOCK 4 — why the vocabulary matters. B-roll: two headlines side by side.]
"Why be this pedantic? Because the two routes imply completely different worlds. Reprogramming any cell into a gamete would eventually decouple reproduction from having usable gametes at all, with everything that follows. Maturing a man's own existing stem cells is a treatment for a specific medical condition. Same headline word. Wildly different futures. And when someone tells you 'IVG is here,' you now know exactly which question to ask: from what starting cell, and published where?"

[BLOCK 5 — our own boundary, stated as a constraint.]
"Now the part where I tell you this has nothing to do with what we sell. MySpawn stores DNA records — ninety-nine dollars a year, in an accredited biorepository, storage only. We do not work with gametes, we do not offer any reproductive service, and nothing in this video is a reason to buy anything from us. I cover it because our whole pitch is that the tools for reading and using biological information keep changing, and you cannot follow that story if the words are mush. When we ourselves have used sloppier phrasing here, we've now fixed it, and the corrected version is in our public research notes."
