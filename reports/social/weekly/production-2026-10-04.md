### SEGMENT 1: The question, and why it is the one customers actually meet (4 to 5 minutes)

**Verbatim quote, with source:** **"There should be better options for when people pass away."** Customer review, quoted in a published round-up. ([Keepsake Project, StoryWorth reviews](https://www.keepsakeproject.co/storyworth-reviews), via search index.) **No name is used and none is published in a way we would repeat.**

**Stance:** every service in this category sells a race against time and almost none of them publish what happens when the race is lost. **That is not villainy. It is a product gap that is uncomfortable to write marketing copy about**, which is exactly why it goes unwritten.

**Talking points.** The subscription-end policy at one well-known provider **is published and clear**: when a term ends, the customer gets a renewal-or-print email and **a few months to keep editing without losing access**. **That is a good answer to a different question.** The death question is a different trigger with a different person asking, and in most households the person asking will be someone who has never logged in.

### SEGMENT 3: The shape of it across the whole category (5 to 6 minutes)

**Stance:** the memoir case is one instance of a pattern we wrote up yesterday, and the pattern is that **ending a relationship with a company is never one action.**

**Talking points, carried from our own published article.** Cancelling is at least **three acts and usually four**: closing the account, deleting the data, destroying any physical material, and withdrawing research consent. **Several providers treat data deletion and sample destruction as separate processes**, and some retain limited information after deletion to meet legal obligations. **Published analyses of direct-to-consumer genetic testing policies found nearly 40 percent of companies had no specific testing policies at all, and of those that did, 29 percent had one addressing sample destruction**: many of those deferring to a laboratory's internal procedures rather than naming a date. ([Our write-up with all sources](/blog/what-happens-dna-sample-if-i-cancel).)

**Now apply the same lens to a memoir service.** Closing the account, retrieving the partial manuscript, printing what exists, and granting someone else the right to finish it **are four different acts too**, and the customer's family will want at least two of them in the same week.

**[CLIP MOMENT]** "Every one of these products has four exits and sells you one door."

## 2. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM EXPLAINER (7 to 9 min): "Who Signed For It"

**Freshness:** **"Handoff", "paper trail" and "who signed" return ZERO across the production log.** **Two adjacencies disclosed, and one of them becomes the hook.**
- **Longpost #106 (29 September, FIVE DAYS, inside the window)** is about **a sample's COLD CHAIN breaking.** **That is temperature.** This explainer is about **the chain of CUSTODY, which is paperwork.** **Rather than hope a viewer notices the difference, the explainer opens on it.**
- **The 7 September accreditation-audit explainer (twenty-seven days)** is about **certificates a facility holds.** Chain of custody is about **the record of who physically handled the thing.** Different object.

**Thesis close, no overpromising:** a chain of custody is the only part of long-term storage that a non-scientist can actually audit, and it is the part most likely to be missing.

### Script with B-roll

**[0:00-0:45] COLD OPEN.** [B-ROLL: a clipboard on a steel bench, nothing else in frame.] "Two different things in this industry get called a chain. One of them is temperature. The other one is paper. Only one of them has a name on it, and that is the one almost nobody asks about."

**[0:45-2:00] WHAT IT IS.** [B-ROLL: gloved hands signing a form, no faces.] A chain of custody is a continuous record of **who had the thing, when, and what they did to it**, from collection to storage. **It is not a certificate. It is a sequence.** Accreditation says a facility meets a standard. **A chain of custody says what happened to your specific item on a specific Tuesday.**

**[2:00-3:30] WHAT BREAKS IT.** [B-ROLL: a courier box on a loading bay.] The classic breaks are **a gap with no signature, a transfer with no timestamp, and a container that changes hands without changing the record.** **None of those are dramatic and none of them are visible later.** **A sample with a broken custody record is not necessarily a damaged sample. It is an unprovable one.**

**[3:30-5:00] WHY IT MATTERS MORE FOR DECADES THAN FOR DAYS.** [B-ROLL: a long rack of identical boxes, slow pan.] Clinical labs keep specimens for years under federal minimums, and accredited pathology labs often keep them far longer. **Over a multi-decade horizon the people change, the software changes, and the company may change owner.** **The record is the only thing that is supposed to survive all three.**

**[5:00-6:30] WHAT TO ASK, honestly framed.** [B-ROLL: a plain email draft on screen, no branding.] Three questions any provider should be able to answer in writing: **who signs at each handoff; whether you can request the custody record for your own item; and what happens to that record if the company is acquired.** **The third one is the one that gets vague answers**, and we wrote up the acquisition case separately. ([What happens when a DNA company shuts down](/blog/what-happens-dna-company-shuts-down).)

**[6:30-8:00] HONEST LIMITS, labeled.** [B-ROLL: static shot of the clipboard again.] **Nothing here is peer-reviewed science; it is operational practice, and practice varies by facility and by jurisdiction.** **We are describing what a good record looks like, not asserting what any particular provider does.** **No figure in this script is quoted from a company page we could not open.**

**[8:00-8:45] CLOSE.** "You cannot audit a freezer from your kitchen. You can audit a sentence. **Ask who signed for it, and keep the answer.**" **[ON SCREEN: myspawn.me - $99/year, billed annually]**
