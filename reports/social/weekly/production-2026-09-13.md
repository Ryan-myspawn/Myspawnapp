### COLD OPEN (word-for-word, ~85s)

"I want to start with two numbers that I think say something unflattering about the industry I work in.

In 2004, the CDC ran a survey. Ninety-six percent of Americans said knowing their family's health history was important. Thirty percent had actually sat down and collected one.

Twenty years later, same question, surveyed again at the start of 2024. Ninety-five percent still said it was important. The share who had actually done it had dropped to **fifteen percent**.

It halved. During the twenty years when spitting into a tube became a Christmas present.

Now, I can't prove causation from two surveys and I'm not going to pretend I can. But I have a strong suspicion, and it's uncomfortable for me, because I sell a thing you mail a sample to.

I think the test *felt* like it replaced the conversation.

You send off the tube, you get back a coloured dashboard with percentages on it, and some part of your brain files the job as done. It isn't done. The dashboard doesn't know that your father was fifty-two when they found the tumour. And that number — not the diagnosis, the *age* — is the thing that would actually change what a doctor does about you.

So today: what the free version buys you, why the paid version didn't replace it, and the seven questions. Nothing in this episode costs money, including from me."

### SEGMENT 1 — The two numbers, and what they're not (5-6 min)
**Receipts:**
- 2004 CDC HealthStyles Survey: **96.3%** said family health history was very or somewhat important; **29.8%** had actively collected one.
- Survey conducted **January-February 2024**: **94.8%** important; **15.2%** had collected one.
- *(Both figures as reported in the family-medicine literature on barriers to family health history collection — https://www.jabfm.org/content/39/1/157915. **Located via search index; open the primary article and confirm both figures before recording.** If the 2024 figure does not hold up, the episode still works on the 2004 number alone and the script should be re-cut rather than fudged.)*

**Stance:** state the limits out loud. Two surveys twenty years apart, different instruments, self-reported. This is a striking correlation and an honest hypothesis, **not** a demonstrated cause.
**CLIP MOMENT:** "The belief held. The behaviour halved. Something got in between them."

### SEGMENT 2 — What the free version actually buys (6-7 min)
**Receipts, all from professional guidance:**
- **Colorectal:** a first-degree relative diagnosed with colorectal cancer or advanced polyps **before 60**, or two or more first-degree relatives at any age, moves screening to **age 40, or ten years before the youngest affected relative's diagnosis, whichever comes first** (American College of Gastroenterology guidance, [summarised by AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2022/0300/p327.html)).
- **Hereditary breast/ovarian:** NCCN criteria route people to *BRCA1/BRCA2* testing largely on family pattern — who, how young, which side.
- **Lynch syndrome:** surveillance intervals key off the **earliest diagnosis in the family**.
- **Prostate:** referral criteria pair a first-degree relative with prostate cancer against close relatives with triple-negative or male breast cancer — a pattern invisible without a written record.

**Stance:** the history doesn't change biology, it changes the calendar. That is the entire mechanism and it is worth saying plainly three times.
**CLIP MOMENT:** "Fifteen-year head start on the cancer that is most survivable when you catch it early. Triggered by a fact you either know or you don't."

### SEGMENT 3 — Why the test didn't replace it (4-5 min)
**Receipts:**
- A Cleveland Clinic team led by Charis Eng compared thorough family-history assessment with a direct-to-consumer genomic screen across 44 people for breast, prostate and colorectal risk. Family history performed better, and **the consumer screen missed nine people at high colon-cancer risk** on family-history grounds. *(Small study, n=44, reported in mainstream press — indicative, not definitive; say so on air.)*
- The honest counterweight, and it goes in: *PLOS Genetics* found family history explains roughly **20-30% of heritability for common heritable conditions** like coronary artery disease — comparable with the best SNP models — but **under 4%** for less common conditions like Crohn's, where genetic markers clearly win ([peer-reviewed](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1002973)).

**Stance:** refuse the clean version. "Family history beats DNA tests" is not what the evidence says. What it says is: free and competitive for the common killers, weaker for rarer conditions, and usually the thing that gets you *referred* for the genetic test that matters.
**CLIP MOMENT:** "I'd love to tell you the cheap thing always beats the expensive thing. It doesn't. It just beats it where most people actually die."

### SEGMENT 4 — The seven questions, and the deadline (3-4 min)
**Talking points:** the seven questions; three generations; ages recorded even as approximations; one relative at a time rather than at the table; record the audio; put it where a clinician can see it and where **at least one other person knows it exists**.
**The deadline:** the people who remember are a finite resource, this is the only item on any legacy list with a real clock, and no company can reconstruct it afterwards — including mine.
**OUTRO CTA:** "No product this week. Write the list, make one call, and if you want the questions in text they're on the blog with the sources. If you get a good story out of the call, tell me — I'll read the best one on the next episode."

# 2. YOUTUBE LONG-FORM EXPLAINER (7-8 min)
## "Why a Genetic Score Gets Weaker Inside Your Own Family"

**Freshness check:** Never produced. The Aug 31 explainer covered **what a polygenic score is**; this
covers **why sibling comparison is the setting where it performs worst** — genetic nurture,
population stratification and assortative mating. Different mechanism, and it is the mechanism that
makes this week's embryo-screening argument actually work rather than just assert.
**Primary audience: Men 25-55** (optimizer).
**Trend-hook used:** this week's embryo-screening coverage and yesterday's blog; stated as
explanation of a live product category rather than as breaking news.

**SCRIPT**

[B-ROLL: a tape measure held against a doorframe with pencil marks — the classic height chart. Return to it at the close.]

"Here's a thing that sounds like a contradiction and isn't.

A polygenic score can genuinely predict differences **across a population** and be close to useless for choosing **between two siblings**. Same score. Same maths. The reason is not that the score is fake, and understanding why is the difference between reading these products well and being sold one.

**Start with how the scores are built.** [ON SCREEN: BUILT ON STRANGERS] You take hundreds of thousands of unrelated people, measure a trait, and find which genetic variants track with it. Add the variants up, weighted. That's the score. And across that population, it works — it really does sort people into different risk bands.

**Now here's the problem.** That population is made of strangers, and strangers differ in more than their genes. Three things ride along inside the score without belonging there.

**One: population stratification.** [ON SCREEN: ANCESTRY, NOT CAUSATION] Groups with different ancestry differ both in gene frequencies *and* in diet, income, environment and healthcare. A variant can look predictive simply because it is more common in a group that also differs environmentally. The maths cannot tell those apart on its own.

**Two: assortative mating.** [ON SCREEN: PEOPLE PICK PEOPLE] People do not pair randomly. They pair by education, height, background. Over generations that correlates variants that have no biological relationship to each other, and inflates how much the score appears to explain.

**Three, and this is the beautiful one: genetic nurture.** [ON SCREEN: YOUR PARENTS' GENES, YOUR ENVIRONMENT] Some of the predictive power of *your* genes is actually the effect of *your parents'* genes — on the home they built, the books in it, the neighbourhood they could afford. That is a real effect on you. It is just not an effect of the DNA in your cells. It is an effect of theirs, running through the world.

[B-ROLL: two nearly identical seedlings in two different pots, one on a windowsill, one in shade.]

**So what happens when you compare siblings?**

All three of those disappear. Same parents, so no stratification between them. Same parental pairing. Same house, same books, same neighbourhood — genetic nurture is held constant.

Everything the score was borrowing gets taken away, and what's left is only the part that was ever really about the individual's own genotype. Which is smaller. Sometimes much smaller.

[ON SCREEN: SIBLING COMPARISON = THE HARDEST TEST]

**And that is exactly the setting embryo screening operates in.** Not you versus the population. Embryo versus its own sibling embryo, same two parents. It is the one comparison where a polygenic score is weakest — structurally, not fixably.

**You can see it in the numbers.** [ON SCREEN: PEER-REVIEWED] The benchmark simulation, published in *Cell* in 2019, found that selecting the top-scoring embryo out of five yields an expected gain of about **2.5 IQ points** or **2.5 centimetres** of height. And when the same team checked it against reality — 28 families with ten or more adult children — **the highest-scoring child usually was not the tallest one.**

Ten kids. Real people, grown up, measurable. The score had every advantage and still did not pick the winner.

**What this does not mean.** It does not mean polygenic scores are junk. Across populations they stratify risk usefully — one peer-reviewed analysis put variance in liability explained at roughly 6% for Crohn's, 7% for schizophrenia, 9% for type 2 diabetes. Real signal. Researchers work on these for good reasons.

It means one specific thing: **a tool calibrated on strangers is being sold for a comparison between siblings, and that is the one job it is worst at.**

[B-ROLL: back to the doorframe and the pencil marks.]

**Where I sit, stated plainly.** We store DNA records. We assign no scores, we rank nothing, and we are not in the screening market — so take the argument on its evidence rather than on my motives, and note that I have no product that gets better if you believe me.

The record we keep contains no prediction at all. That's not modesty about the technology. It's that the honest thing to preserve is what someone *was*, not a guess about what they'll be."

**Honest-labeling summary for the description:** Karavani et al., *Cell* 2019 — peer-reviewed. *eLife* 2021 variance figures — peer-reviewed. Genetic nurture, stratification and assortative-mating effects — established in the peer-reviewed literature and summarised in *Human Reproduction Update*. No claim that polygenic scores are without value.
