**Short answer: the hospital does not keep it. The state does.** Almost every US baby gets a heel prick in the first days of life, and the blood goes on a card that is sent to a state public-health laboratory. Once the screening tests are run, what is left over is called a residual dried blood spot, and **how long the state keeps it varies enormously: from about 30 days in some programs to indefinitely in others.** New York holds specimens up to 10 years. Virginia keeps normal-result spots 6 months and abnormal-result spots 10 years. Texas destroys them within two years unless a parent affirmatively consents to longer, in which case they may be held up to 25 years. Minnesota may store them indefinitely unless a parent directs destruction. **New Jersey, which held them for 23 years, changed its policy in June 2024 to two years by default.** In most states a parent can ask for the spot to be returned or destroyed, and in most states nobody tells them that at the bedside.

## The screening is not the storage, and that is the whole confusion

Newborn screening is one of the genuinely successful public-health programs of the last century. A few drops of blood, dried on filter paper, screened for a panel of serious conditions that are treatable if caught in the first weeks and devastating if they are not. **Nothing in this article is an argument against the screen**, and anybody who uses it that way is misreading it.

The screen takes days. What happens to the card afterwards is a separate decision, made by a different part of the system, usually under a different statute, and it is the part parents are rarely told about. **The test is a test. The retention is storage.** Those are two different things and they are governed differently.

This distinction matters for the same reason it matters with any custodian of biological material: the question is never only "what did you do with it," it is "what are you still holding, for how long, and who decides." We ask readers to put that question to commercial labs in [how long labs keep your DNA sample](how-long-labs-keep-dna-sample) and in [what happens to my DNA sample if I cancel](what-happens-dna-sample-if-i-cancel). A state screening program is not a commercial lab, but the question has the same shape.

## How long does your state keep it?

There is no national rule. Retention is set state by state, which is why the honest answer to "how long do they keep it" is "look up your own state, because your neighbor's answer may be wrong for you."

| State | Residual spot retention |
|---|---|
| **Minnesota** | May be stored **indefinitely** under Minn. Stat. 144.125 unless the parent or guardian directs destruction ([MN Dept. of Health](https://www.health.state.mn.us/people/newbornscreening/program/retention.html)) |
| **Texas** | Destroyed within **two years**; if the parent consents on the Storage and Use form, stored up to **25 years** ([Texas DSHS](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/laboratory-services/programs-laboratories/newborn-screening-laboratory/newborn-screening-use-storage)) |
| **New York** | Up to **10 years**, access strictly controlled ([Wadsworth Center](https://wadsworth.org/programs/newborn/screening/families/specimen-usage)) |
| **Virginia** | **6 months** for normal results, **10 years** for abnormal results ([Virginia DH](https://www.vdh.virginia.gov/dried-blood-spot-newborn-screening/retention-of-residual-dried-blood-spots/)) |
| **New Jersey** | **Two years** by default since a June 2024 policy change, with the parent able to choose a longer or shorter period ([NJ DOH, 20 June 2024](https://www.nj.gov/health/news/2024/approved/20240620b.shtml)) |

**Read that table as a sample, not as a list.** Retention across US programs runs from roughly 30 days to indefinite, and the federal advisory briefing on residual specimens describes exactly that spread ([HRSA Advisory Committee briefing](https://www.hrsa.gov/sites/default/files/hrsa/advisory-committees/heritable-disorders/reports-recommendations/briefing-residual-dried-spot-specimens.pdf)). If your state is not above, the phrase to search is your state's name plus "newborn screening residual dried blood spot retention," and the page you want is the health department's own.

### The New Jersey number everybody quotes is out of date

If you have seen "New Jersey kept newborn blood for 23 years," that was accurate and it is now history. **The state announced a change in June 2024: identified newborn screening blood spots are retained for two years unless the parent or guardian opts for a longer or shorter period.** The 23-year figure still circulates widely, usually in coverage of the litigation that preceded the change. **It describes the old policy, and quoting it in the present tense is wrong.**

## What are the leftover spots used for?

Three broad categories, and they are not equally controversial.

1. **Program quality.** Calibrating and validating the tests themselves, which is why any spot is kept at all.
2. **Research**, usually de-identified, including work that genuinely could not be done any other way. Residual newborn spots are, for example, an unusual resource in childhood cancer research because they capture a sample from before the disease existed ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198413/)).
3. **Outside requests**, which is where the disputes live. Access rules vary by state and the controlled-access framing is standard; New York's provider-facing page on residual spot usage is a reasonable example of how a program documents it ([Wadsworth, residual blood spot usage](https://www.wadsworth.org/programs/newborn/screening/providers/residual-blood-spot-usage)).

**Labeled honestly: the research use is real and defensible, and the consent process around it is what has been litigated.** Those two statements are not in conflict, and an article that only tells you one of them is selling you something.

## The lawsuits, with their dates attached

**Michigan.** Four parents sued in 2018 in what became *Kanuszewski v. Michigan Department of Health and Human Services*. In **June 2022** the state agreed, as a partial settlement, to destroy **more than 3 million** dried blood spots ([WOOD TV](https://www.woodtv.com/news/michigan/michigan-to-destroy-some-blood-spots-in-fight-over-consent/)). The litigation continued past that, including a **2025 Sixth Circuit opinion** ([Sixth Circuit, 25a0168p-06](https://www.opn.ca6.uscourts.gov/opinions.pdf/25a0168p-06.pdf)). **We have not read that opinion in full and make no claim about its holding here.** The practical mechanism it concerns is simple: parents, or the children once they are adults, submit a form to the state lab asking for return or destruction.

**New Jersey.** The Institute for Justice filed a federal class action in **November 2023** on behalf of three parents ([IJ case page](https://ij.org/case/new-jersey-genetic-privacy/)). **US District Judge Georgette Castner partially dismissed the complaint in August 2025**, reasoning in part that it was not clear that interests in residual dried blood spots should be treated the same as real property. **Plaintiffs filed an amended complaint in October 2025** ([IJ press release](https://ij.org/press-release/new-jersey-parents-file-amended-complaint-in-challenge-to-states-baby-blood-retention-after-judge-partially-dismisses-case/); [Biometric Update, October 2025](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202510/parents-renew-fight-against-new-jerseys-storage-of-infant-biometric-identifiers)). **As of this sweep, no final ruling has been located.** The relief sought is informed consent, or return, or destruction.

**What the Michigan settlement is evidence of, and what it is not.** It is evidence that a state will destroy stored spots at scale when a court requires it. **It is not evidence that any particular state will do so on request without one.** Those are different claims and the difference is the whole practical question.

## How to find out, and how to ask

This is the part worth doing today, and it takes about twenty minutes.

1. **Find your state's newborn screening page.** Search your state name plus "newborn screening residual dried blood spot." Use the health department's own domain. A summary on a news site is not a policy.
2. **Write down three things:** the default retention period, whether storage beyond screening requires your consent or your objection, and whether research use is opt-in or opt-out. **Those three answers are the whole picture and most states publish all three.**
3. **Look for the form.** Many programs have a specific destruction or opt-out form. Some accept a written request. The form is usually not linked from the page a new parent sees.
4. **Ask in writing and keep the reply.** "Please confirm the current retention period for my child's residual dried blood spot, whether it has been used for any purpose beyond the screening panel, and the process for requesting its destruction." **A verbal answer at a hospital desk is not a record and the hospital is usually not the custodian anyway.**
5. **Know the deadline problem.** If your state's default is six months and your child is four, the question may already be moot. **Ask anyway, because the answer tells you what the program does, which is worth knowing before the next child.**

If you want the wider legal picture on who can hold genetic material and under what conditions, [genetic privacy laws](genetic-privacy-laws) covers the state statutes that have moved most recently.

## FAQ

### Do hospitals keep newborn blood samples?
**No, not usually.** The hospital collects the heel-prick card and sends it to a state public-health laboratory, which runs the screen and holds whatever is left. **The custodian you want to contact is the state newborn screening program, not the hospital.**

### How long does the state keep my baby's blood spot?
**It depends entirely on the state, and the range is from about 30 days to indefinitely.** Texas destroys within two years unless a parent consents to longer storage of up to 25 years. New York holds up to 10 years. Virginia keeps normal-result spots 6 months. Minnesota may hold indefinitely unless a parent directs destruction. New Jersey moved to a two-year default in June 2024.

### Can I ask for my child's newborn blood spot to be destroyed?
**In most states, yes.** Programs generally have a destruction or opt-out process, and in Michigan parents, or the children themselves once they are adults, may submit a form to the state lab requesting return or destruction. **Ask in writing and keep the reply.**

### Is the leftover blood spot used for research?
**Often, usually de-identified, and the rules vary by state.** Some programs require affirmative consent for storage and research use; others treat it as opt-out. Residual spots have real scientific value, including in childhood cancer research, and the consent process around that use is what has been litigated.

### Did New Jersey really keep newborn blood for 23 years?
**It did, and that policy changed.** The 23-year retention is what the November 2023 federal lawsuit was filed over. **In June 2024 the state announced a two-year default, with parents able to choose a longer or shorter period.** The 23-year figure is still widely quoted and is now historical.

### Does newborn screening itself give my child a DNA profile I can keep?
**No.** The screening panel tests for a defined list of conditions; it is not a sequencing service and it does not produce a record you own or can download. **If you want a biological record of your own, that is a separate decision you make deliberately, with a custodian whose retention terms you have read.**
