Most legacy advice assumes you have a family history to pass on. You write down what your grandfather died of, you tell your kids which screening to start early, you hand over the pedigree with the photographs. **For a lot of people that first step is not hard, it is impossible**, and no amount of good intention produces a document that was never written.

This is about what you can actually do when the history is a blank: what the law will and will not give you, what a clinician does with "I don't know", what testing substitutes for, and which of these has a clock on it.

## First, the thing nobody says out loud: the blank gets inherited

If you do not know your family medical history, **your children do not know half of theirs.** That is what turns a personal inconvenience into a legacy problem, and it is the honest reason to do work this tedious.

## What the law will give you, by category

**If you were adopted in the United States, the answer depends on which state you were born in, and the map changed again this year.**

**As of 1 July 2026, adult adopted people born in seventeen states can request and obtain their own original birth certificate with no discriminatory restriction** beyond an age requirement, usually 18, and a fee. [1] Virginia is the most recent addition, effective that date. Counts of fifteen were accurate as recently as 2024 and are still circulating.

**Partial access is the norm.** Utah is not unrestricted because a birth parent can restrict release by court order obtained before the adoptee turns 18, and **West Virginia and Wyoming require a court order.** A change in the law is also not always the change it sounds like: **North Carolina's new law, effective 1 January 2026, makes certified copies of the amended certificate easier to get and leaves sealed originals sealed.** [1]

**An original birth certificate is also not a medical history.** It gives you names, and names are the input to the search, not the output.

**If you were donor-conceived, the governing number is ten years, and it is a federal floor rather than a promise.** Under the FDA's rules for human cells and tissues, records for a particular product must be kept **at least ten years** after administration, or after distribution, disposition or expiration where that date is unknown. [2] The consequence is plain arithmetic: **a donor's medical information can lawfully be destroyed before the person conceived from it turns eighteen.**

**That floor is being challenged.** A donor-conceived adult petitioned the FDA in 2016 for longer retention, the agency denied it, and **a suit filed 22 July 2026 asks a court to require fifty years.** [2] It is early, with no ruling. **Do not plan around it.**

**One state has already moved.** Colorado's Donor-Conceived Persons Protection Act **took effect 1 January 2025.** Licensed banks, clinics and agencies must **keep donation records permanently** and **periodically ask donors to update their medical history and contact details**, with identity release to offspring at 18. It is **prospective**, so pre-2025 anonymous donations are not reopened. [3]

**Epistemic label: the Colorado detail comes from advocacy and law-firm summaries, not the statute or the final CDPHE rules, which we did not read.** The effective date is consistent across sources; the exact update interval is not something we can state.

## What a clinician actually does with "I don't know"

Less than you would hope, and the profession has started saying so in print.

**A cross-sectional mixed-methods survey of 204 US adults adopted in childhood, fielded June 2023 to January 2024 and published in Annals of Family Medicine in November 2025, found most said clinicians lacked knowledge about adoption, particularly about how limited family medical history affects care.** Most also reported adoption-related discrimination from clinicians, and a companion study found primary care physicians receive little training on it. [4]

**There is no dedicated clinical guideline for patients with no retrievable family history.** What exists is indirect, and the exact language matters because it is what your insurer reads:

**Cancer genetics guidelines treat unknown family structure as a reason your risk may be underestimated, not as a reason to test you.** The NCCN high-risk assessment guideline lists adoption among the factors to weigh and notes that people with unknown or limited family history may have an underestimated chance of carrying a familial pathogenic variant, its footnote applying at age 51 and over. [5]

**Payer policies define "limited family history" with a number, and adoptees are inside it.** At least two large policies define it as **fewer than two known first- or second-degree female relatives surviving beyond 45 years of age** on either or both sides. [5] That is the sentence to quote in an appeal.

**And the panel guidance points somewhere useful:** ASCO notes patients may be unaware of family history to inform gene selection, which supports a broader multigene panel rather than a targeted test. [5]

**The practical translation:** nobody hands you a protocol. You arrive with the definition, the footnote and the ages, and ask for the broader panel on the record. Our guide to [what a genetic counselor actually does](/blog/what-does-a-genetic-counselor-do) is the appointment to book, and it is the one place where the missing history is the reason to go rather than the reason not to bother.

## What genetic testing substitutes for, and what it does not

**People in this position reach for testing at much higher rates.** In a prospective cohort of 5,799 patients enrolled in an elective genomic testing program between August 2020 and April 2022, **197 reported being adopted**, and adoptees were substantially more likely than non-adoptees to rate lack of family health history as a very important reason for testing. [6] **A 2016 study of 1,607 consumer testing customers, 80 of them adopted, put the odds ratio for citing limited family history as a strong motivation at about 10.1.** [7]

**And the same literature says the test is not the thing they are missing.** A 2024 Journal of Genetic Counseling study found many adoptees treat testing as a substitute for family history even though it cannot fully stand in for one; a 2023 UK study found they may not understand the limits of direct-to-consumer tests when buying them. [7]

**Why the substitution fails.** A family history carries **ages at onset**, **patterns across generations**, and **everything polygenic, environmental and behavioural** that no panel reports. A test covers a defined list of variants, and a negative panel means that panel found nothing, not that there is nothing. **A blank history plus a negative panel is not an all-clear. It is two kinds of not knowing stacked on each other.**

**There is a second-order problem specific to this group.** With no relatives to compare against, **you also have no family context for a variant of uncertain significance**, and nobody is obliged to tell you if the classification later changes, which we covered in [will anyone tell you if your genetic test result changes](/blog/genetic-test-result-reanalysis). **For someone with a full pedigree a reclassification is an update. For someone without one it was the whole basis of the conclusion.**

## A sequence, in order of how fast the clock runs

**1. Request what is time-limited first.** Donor records have a ten-year floor and the clock may already be running. **Anything held by a third party under a retention schedule goes first.** Our piece on [how long labs keep your DNA sample](/blog/how-long-labs-keep-dna-sample) covers the same logic for clinical samples.

**2. File for the original birth certificate if your birth state allows it.** Seventeen states, age 18, a fee. If yours needs a court order, find out what that involves before deciding it is impossible.

**3. Write down the history you do have, however thin.** Your own diagnoses with ages, your children's, anything a relative mentioned, labelled as hearsay. **A thin documented history beats a rich remembered one**, and the [family health history guide](/blog/family-health-history-guide) covers the structure.

**4. Book the appointment with the limited-family-history definition in hand.** Ask for the broader panel and ask why if refused.

**5. Decide what you are preserving, and be clear it is material rather than answers.** People do this first and should do it last.

### How long are sperm and egg donor medical records kept?

### Does a DNA test replace a family health history?
**No, and the research on exactly this group is consistent about it.** A family history carries ages at onset, multi-generation patterns and all the polygenic and environmental risk no panel reports. **A blank history plus a negative panel is two kinds of not knowing, not an all-clear.** Studies from 2016, 2021, 2023 and 2024 find adoptees far more likely to seek testing for this reason and liable to over-read what it delivers. ### What should I tell my doctor if I don't know my family history?
**Arrive with the payer language rather than the apology.** At least two large hereditary-cancer policies define "limited family history" as **fewer than two known first- or second-degree female relatives surviving beyond 45 years of age** on either or both sides, and adoptees are explicitly inside it. ASCO supports a **broader multigene panel** when family history cannot guide gene selection. **Ask for it, and ask for any refusal in writing.** [5]

### Are clinicians trained for this?
**Largely not, and the profession has published that finding.** A survey of 204 US adults adopted in childhood, fielded June 2023 to January 2024 and published in Annals of Family Medicine in November 2025, found most said clinicians lacked knowledge about adoption and about how limited family medical history affects care; most also reported adoption-related discrimination from clinicians. **There is no dedicated clinical guideline for patients with no retrievable family history.** [4]
