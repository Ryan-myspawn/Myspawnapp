# What Does It Mean When a Study Is Retracted?

**It means the paper should no longer be relied on, it does not mean anybody has been accused of fraud, and it is one of at least three different notices that get loosely called the same thing.** A correction fixes an error. An expression of concern warns you that something may be wrong while somebody finds out. A retraction withdraws the finding. They carry different weight, they arrive at different speeds, and the gap between them is where most of the confusion in science reporting lives.

Here is what each one actually is, how long they take, and the two-minute check you can run on any paper somebody quotes at you.

## Three notices, and they are not interchangeable

**A correction** amends a specific error in a paper that otherwise stands. The findings survive.

**An expression of concern** is the one almost nobody can define. Per the Committee on Publication Ethics, which writes the guidance most journals follow, it is a notice alerting readers to **major and credible concerns about a publication's reliability that do not meet the criteria for retraction**, or where **conclusive evidence cannot be obtained for some time**. It can be an **interim** notice while an investigation runs, or it can be the **final outcome**. COPE also holds that an expression of concern should carry its own DOI, so that it is citable and findable rather than buried.

Read that twice, because it is doing something unusual: it is a published statement that the journal does not yet know. That is a more honest posture than most institutions manage, and it is also why it is so easy to misreport. An expression of concern is not a soft retraction. Sometimes it is followed by one. Sometimes it is lifted. Sometimes it just sits there.

**A retraction** withdraws the paper's findings. COPE's retraction guidelines are a separate document and deliberately do not cover expressions of concern or corrections. Its 2025 update also describes **retraction with replacement**, intended for articles with serious errors that change the findings without invalidating the underlying scholarship.

**And a retraction is not a verdict on a person.** Papers are retracted for fabrication and for honest error, for image duplication and for consent paperwork, for plagiarism and for a lab discovering its own mistake. The notice tells you the paper is unreliable. It does not, by itself, tell you why.

There is also a fourth thing, and it is a standards problem rather than an editorial one. **NISO RP-45-2024, the CREC recommended practice**, covers how retractions, removals and expressions of concern are recorded and displayed in metadata so that databases and reference managers can carry the flag. It states plainly that it **does not address why** a paper was retracted, because COPE already does that. So the ecosystem has one body deciding what a notice means and another deciding how it travels, and the second half is only two years old.

## The lag is the part that should worry you

A retraction is not instant. The literature on how long it takes is small, field-specific, and consistent about one thing: it is slower than you would guess.

- In a citation analysis of **128 retracted systematic reviews**, the median time from publication to retraction was **15 months**.
- In a study of **460 retracted genetics articles**, the median was **3.2 years**.
- In a study of **621 retracted chemistry papers**, the median split sharply by reason: **1.3 years for plagiarism** against **4.8 years for fabrication or falsification**.

*(All three are peer-reviewed bibliometric analyses, read via search index.)*

That last split is the useful one and it is counterintuitive. **The fastest retractions are for the offence that is easiest to prove with software. The slowest are for the offence that does the most damage.** Plagiarism is detectable by matching text. Fabricated data has to be investigated by humans who are often colleagues of the person being investigated.

So when somebody tells you a finding is "established," the honest follow-up is not whether it has been retracted. It is how long it has been out, and whether anybody with the means to check has had a reason to.

## Retraction does not stop the citations

This is the finding that changed how we read reference lists.

- Of the citations to those **128 retracted systematic reviews**, **580 of them, about 60.8 percent, appeared in articles published after the review was retracted.**
- In the genetics study, pre-retraction citations ran about **3.3 times** post-retraction ones, meaning roughly **23 percent** of citations arrived after the notice. **One in four of those retracted articles received at least half its citations after retraction.**
- In chemistry, the post-retraction citation share was **42 percent for plagiarism cases** against **21.5 percent for fabrication and falsification**.
- Even Wikipedia, which has volunteers actively hunting this, showed a **median time to correction of 3.68 years, about 1,344 days**, for citations to retracted papers.

**One honest caveat the studies raise themselves:** a citation appearing shortly after a retraction is not necessarily careless, because a paper typically takes a year or more from submission to publication. Some of those citations were written before the notice existed. That caveat does not survive the three-year and four-year tails.

**What this means for you as a reader is simple and slightly bleak: a retracted paper does not disappear. It keeps being cited, keeps appearing in review articles, keeps getting quoted in press releases, and the flag does not follow it reliably.**

## Nobody agrees how many retractions there are

The headline number you will see is that **more than 10,000 articles were retracted worldwide in 2023**, described as a record. *(University library guidance, reported.)*

Beneath it, the registers disagree.

The **Retraction Watch Database**, acquired by **Crossref in 2023** and now updated publicly, held **just shy of 55,000 entries at the end of 2024**, with a later page citing **more than 63,000**. Crossref's own count of retraction notices has historically run much lower than Retraction Watch's. **A 2026 analysis found the two registers agree on only 72.42 percent of retracted DOIs.**

**That disagreement is not a scandal, it is a definition problem**, and it is the same shape as every measurement problem this blog writes about. One register counts publisher notices. Another counts investigated cases. Mass retractions, where a publisher withdraws hundreds of papers at once, land in one year and distort it.

**So treat any single retraction count the way you would treat any single market-size estimate: ask which register, and what it counts.**

## The two-minute check

You do not need a subscription or a librarian.

1. **Search the paper's title or DOI in the Retraction Watch Database.** It is free, hosted by Crossref and updated continuously. A hit tells you a notice exists.
2. **Open the paper on the journal's own site, not a PDF somebody sent you.** Notices are attached to the publisher's version. A PDF in an email, a preprint server copy or a file on a supplement company's site may carry no flag at all.
3. **Read the notice itself, not the word.** It will say whether it is a correction, an expression of concern or a retraction, and usually why. An expression of concern that says an investigation is ongoing means something different from a retraction that says the data could not be produced.
4. **Check the date of the notice against the date of whatever is quoting the paper.** If an article from last month cites a paper retracted in 2023, that tells you something about the article.

For a worked example of what this looks like in practice, our write-up on [plasmalogen supplement evidence](/blog/plasmalogen-supplement-evidence) traces one human study from a January 2026 expression of concern by *Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology*, citing concerns about compliance with local legislation, through to a subsequent retraction after the journal's integrity team investigated. **That is the full sequence in one case: concern first, investigation, then withdrawal. It is also a case where the popular coverage carried the original finding and not the notice.**

### Is a retracted study always fraud?

No. Papers are retracted for fabrication and for honest error, for image problems, plagiarism, consent and ethics paperwork, and sometimes because the authors themselves found the mistake. A retraction says the findings should not be relied on. It is not by itself an accusation against a person.

### What is the difference between an expression of concern and a retraction?

An expression of concern is a notice that there are major and credible concerns about a paper's reliability that either do not meet the criteria for retraction, or cannot be settled yet. It can be interim or final. A retraction withdraws the findings. One is a warning that the journal does not yet know; the other is a conclusion.

### How long does it take for a bad paper to be retracted?

Longer than most people assume, and it varies by field and reason. Published medians include 15 months for retracted systematic reviews, 3.2 years in a genetics sample, and in chemistry 1.3 years for plagiarism against 4.8 years for fabrication or falsification.

### Do people stop citing a paper after it is retracted?

No. In one analysis of retracted systematic reviews, about 60.8 percent of citations came after retraction. In a genetics sample about 23 percent did, and one in four of those papers got at least half its citations post-retraction. Some of that is publication lag, but not the multi-year tails.

### How can I check whether a study has been retracted?

Search the title or DOI in the free Retraction Watch Database, now hosted by Crossref, then open the paper on the journal's own website and read the notice attached to it. Flags often do not travel with PDFs, preprint copies or files hosted by companies selling a related product.
