# What Happens If a Lab Loses Its Accreditation?

**Freshness check:** No item here is from the last 14 days and the article claims none. The newest dated source is the Federal Register notice re-approving CAP as a CLIA accreditation organisation, 29 March 2021. **The reason to publish now is structural rather than topical: accreditation is a status that can change, and almost everything written about it, including our own, describes it as though it were permanent.**
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55, plus 40-60 readers checking a custody arrangement.
**Trend-hook used:** None.

**Accreditation is not a permanent property of a laboratory. It is a status, it can be suspended or withdrawn, and the accreditation body publishes that status so you can check it. If a lab is suspended, it generally may not issue reports or certificates as an accredited lab, and it may not use the accreditor's logo, but your physical sample does not move and the suspension is intended to be temporary. If accreditation is withdrawn, work already properly done before the withdrawal date is usually still regarded as valid; work after it is not backed by accreditation.** This article is only about losing it. **It does not explain what ISO 20387, CAP accreditation or "FDA-registered" each certify, because that is covered in [biobank accreditation explained](/blog/biobank-accreditation-explained).**

**Scope and honesty note up front: almost nothing published addresses accreditation sanctions for biobanks specifically. The material below comes from clinical-laboratory regulation and from accreditation bodies' general sanction policies, and each source is labelled where it sits.**

## Suspension and withdrawal are two different things

Accreditation bodies generally operate two sanctions, and the distinction is the one most worth knowing.

**Suspension is temporary.** The United Kingdom Accreditation Service describes suspension and withdrawal as the two main sanction types, with **withdrawal typically used when a suspended body has failed to address the issues that caused the suspension adequately or in a timely manner**. [1] The American accreditation body A2LA describes suspension as a temporary measure lasting until specific conditions for reinstatement are met. [2]

**So withdrawal is usually not a first response. It is what suspension turns into when nothing gets fixed.**

## What a suspended lab may not do

This is more restrictive than most people expect, and it is about paperwork rather than about work.

**Finland's accreditation body FINAS states that during a suspension an accredited body is not allowed to issue certificates and reports as an accredited body.** [3] South Africa's equivalent is blunter still: a suspended organisation cannot issue certificates, reports or any other materials displaying the accreditor's logo. [4]

**Read that carefully. The lab may still physically operate. What it loses is the right to describe its output as accredited output.** For a storage facility, where the service is custody rather than testing, that distinction matters: **the freezers do not switch off because a certificate is suspended.**

## What happens to work already done

**For certification bodies, the withdrawal date is the hinge.** Certificates issued before that date by a then-accredited body are generally treated as valid for their remaining term, **provided the audits behind them were properly conducted**. Certificates issued after the withdrawal date are not backed by accreditation. [5] One source notes that a suspension, as opposed to a withdrawal, does not generally put existing certificates at risk, **though this varies by jurisdiction**. [5]

**Applied to stored samples, the practical reading is that a custody record made while the facility was accredited does not retroactively stop having been made under accreditation.** **LABEL: that is an inference from how certification bodies treat certificates, not a rule published for biobanks, and we are flagging it as such rather than presenting it as settled.**

## The timelines, and which of them we actually trust

**This section is where the sourcing matters most, so the reliability of each number is stated with it.**

**TRUSTWORTHY, because it is federal regulation.** Under **42 CFR 493.1844**, a laboratory has **60 days from the notice of sanction** to request a hearing before an administrative law judge on CLIA certificate sanctions, and **suspension, limitation or revocation of an accreditation certificate is an initial determination and is appealable**. [6]

**TRUSTWORTHY, because it is the Federal Register.** The notice re-approving the College of American Pathologists as a CLIA accreditation organisation, dated **29 March 2021**, confirms that **CAP will deny, suspend or revoke accreditation and report that action to CMS within 30 days**, and that CAP provides an appeals process for laboratories denied, suspended or revoked. [7] **It does not publish the internal deadlines.**

**NOT TRUSTWORTHY, and we are telling you rather than quietly using it.** A widely-circulated copy of a CAP accreditation manual, hosted on a document-sharing site rather than by CAP, states a **45-day** window from notice of suspension to satisfy the accreditation committee, a **30-day** appeal window, and a **six-month** wait before reapplying. **Those figures are plausible and they may well be correct. They are not verified, the document is not an official CAP publication, and we are not going to repeat them as fact.** **If those deadlines matter to you, get them from CAP's own manual.**

**For comparison only: the US National Voluntary Laboratory Accreditation Program, under 15 CFR 285.13, allows 30 days from receipt of a proposed denial or revocation letter to appeal to the Director of NIST.** [8] **Different programme, different rules, included to show the range rather than to be borrowed.**

## The part you can actually use: the status is public

**Under ISO/IEC 17011, accreditation bodies are required to make the status of an accreditation publicly available.** The German accreditation body DAkkS cites exactly this requirement for publishing certificate status. [9] **Accreditation databases list suspended and withdrawn status, and UKAS publishes a page of organisations currently under sanction.** [1]

**This is the single most useful fact in the article, and it costs nothing to act on.** A company's own website will tell you it is accredited. **The accreditation body's register will tell you whether that is still true today.** Those are different sources with different incentives, and only one of them updates when something goes wrong.

**A reduction in scope is worth watching for too, not just outright loss.** The FDA's laboratory accreditation framework requires the accreditation body to notify the laboratory of any **reduction of scope** or withdrawal, including the grounds and the effective date. [10] **A lab can remain accredited while quietly no longer being accredited for the thing you care about.**

## FAQ

### Does my sample get destroyed if the lab loses accreditation?
**Nothing in the sanction rules requires a stored sample to be destroyed.** Accreditation sanctions govern what a facility may claim about its output, not what it holds. A suspended facility generally may not issue reports or certificates as an accredited body and may not use the accreditor's logo, **but the storage itself continues.** **What a closure or insolvency does is a separate question, covered in our article on DNA companies shutting down.**

### Is suspension the same as losing accreditation?
No. **Suspension is temporary and lasts until specified conditions for reinstatement are met.** Withdrawal is typically what happens when a suspended body **fails to fix the underlying issues adequately or in time.** Treat a suspension as a serious warning rather than an ending.

### Can I check whether a lab is currently accredited?
Yes, and this is the most useful thing in this article. **ISO/IEC 17011 requires accreditation bodies to make accreditation status publicly available**, accreditation databases list suspended and withdrawn status, and some accreditors publish a dedicated page of organisations under sanction. **Check the accreditor's register, not the company's own website.**

### How long does a lab have to appeal?
**It depends entirely on which programme, and published answers differ.** Under 42 CFR 493.1844 a laboratory has **60 days from the notice of sanction** to request an administrative law judge hearing on CLIA certificate sanctions. Under 15 CFR 285.13, NVLAP allows **30 days from receipt** of a proposed denial or revocation letter to appeal to the Director of NIST. **Figures circulating for CAP's internal windows come from an unofficial copy of its manual and we do not repeat them as fact.**

### Does accreditation cover everything the lab does?
No, and this is the trap. **Accreditation has a scope, and a facility can keep its accreditation while having that scope reduced.** The FDA's laboratory accreditation framework requires the accreditation body to notify a laboratory of any reduction of scope, with grounds and an effective date. **Check what the certificate actually covers, not just whether one exists.**

### Is there a rule written specifically for biobanks losing accreditation?
**We could not find one.** The published material addresses clinical laboratories and certification bodies generally. **ISO 20387 is the international biobanking standard, but we found nothing setting out biobank-specific sanction procedures, and we would rather say that than imply a rule exists.**
