# Epigenetic Age Tests: What They Actually Measure

**An epigenetic age test reads chemical tags on your DNA, mostly methyl groups at specific sites, and runs them through a formula trained to output a number in years. The number is real in the sense that the chemistry is real. What it is not is a stable property of you.** A 2026 analysis in *Aging Cell* found that these clocks are highly consistent when the same sample is run twice, and considerably less consistent when the same person is sampled under different everyday conditions. **Precision and stability are different things, and the test result you paid for carries only the first one.**

That distinction is the whole article, and most coverage skips it.

## What an epigenetic clock actually measures

DNA methylation is a chemical mark attached at particular positions in the genome, and the pattern changes with age in ways that are predictable across large populations. **A clock is a statistical model, not a reading.** Researchers measure methylation at hundreds of thousands of sites in thousands of people whose ages are known, find the sites that move most reliably with age, and fit a formula. Feed a new sample in, get a number out.

**This matters for how much weight the number deserves.** A clock is only as good as the population it was trained on and the question it was trained to answer. Two clocks can read the same sample and disagree, and neither is malfunctioning. They were built to predict different things.

## Four clocks, four different questions

**The first-generation clocks were trained to predict chronological age.** Horvath's 2013 multi-tissue clock is the best known. It does what it was built to do, and in one longitudinal cohort analysis, the Horvath clock was the only one of the set tested that showed no association with mortality when measured once at baseline. **A clock that predicts your birthday accurately is not thereby telling you anything about your health.**

**The second generation changed the training target.** PhenoAge and GrimAge were trained against clinical markers and mortality rather than calendar age. GrimAge was built specifically to predict mortality and healthspan, and in a 2025 analysis reported secondhand through research coverage, it outperformed the other clocks tested on mortality prediction. **LABEL: that comparison reached us through a secondary report, not the paper itself.** Faster acceleration on PhenoAge and GrimAge has been associated with reduced ability to perform daily activities and with cognitive dysfunction.

**DunedinPACE is a different kind of instrument again.** It estimates a rate, the pace at which you are ageing per calendar year, rather than an age. It is weaker than GrimAge at predicting death and tracks physiological decline.

**And a 2024 cohort study of 699 adults followed for up to 24 years found something more useful than any single reading:** faster longitudinal change across several clocks was significantly associated with higher mortality, independent of baseline age. **The direction of travel carried information the snapshot did not.**

## The 2026 reliability finding that should change how you read your result

This is the part worth sitting with.

The *Aging Cell* analysis separated **technical reliability**, meaning does the same sample give the same answer twice, from **biological reliability**, meaning does the same person give the same answer across ordinary life. **The clocks scored well on the first and unevenly on the second, and the paper reported that technical reproducibility did not predict biological reliability.**

**Pooled across four independent studies, most clocks landed in the moderate-to-good range, roughly 0.4 to 0.7 on an intraclass correlation. None reached excellent.** Estimates also moved measurably with laboratory handling: when replicates differed only by position on the processing slide, several clocks fell below the good threshold.

**Principal-component versions of the clocks did better on both measures.** PCPhenoAge, PCGrimAge, PCHorvath1 and Systems Age were the reproducible ones. First-generation clocks and Dunedin-based measures were less stable.

**Read that back against the product you are being sold.** If a result can move with a meal, a stressful week, an air-quality spell or where your sample sat on a slide, then a three-year improvement after six months of effort is not obviously an improvement. It may be noise wearing the costume of a result.

## What the consumer market does not publish

A 2026 review in *Biogerontology* noted an absence of independent peer-reviewed validation for commercial clocks, and in some cases no published correlation or mean-absolute-error figures at all. A separate ethics paper observed that several direct-to-consumer epigenetic testing companies market a biological age while running **first-generation clocks**, which are the ones trained on calendar age rather than health outcomes.

**One commercial comparison page offers a specific test-retest precision figure in years. This article will not repeat the number, because it traces to no primary study we could reach.** A precise-sounding figure with no source behind it is worse than no figure, because it travels further.

It is also worth knowing what regulatory status these tests generally carry, which is to say not much. **Being sold legally is not the same as being cleared, and cleared is not the same as approved.** We took that ladder apart in [FDA clearance versus approval](/blog/fda-clearance-vs-approval), and the broader question of what makes a genetic test trustworthy in [what a validated genetic test actually means](/blog/genetic-test-validated-meaning).

## Can you run one of these on hair or nail clippings?

Short answer: **sometimes on nails, rarely on hair shafts, and not as a general-purpose clock.**

**Nails are the better of the two.** One study extracted DNA from 433 nail samples taken from 113 people, with yields ranging from 2 nanograms to 2,295 nanograms, and built age estimation on a small panel of fifteen CpG sites across four markers, a design chosen precisely because the material is limited. The authors argued nails resist decay comparatively well. **LABEL: that work reached us as a preprint, not a journal publication.**

**Hair is harder, and the reason is structural.** DNA in hair is extensively degraded by the keratinisation process itself. One plucked-hair study analysing ten loci succeeded in 31 of 49 individuals, and the analysis was restricted to hairs with a root still carrying intact cells. **A hair shaft without a root is a poor candidate.** A 2025 paper reported methylation-sensitive fragmentation patterns in rootless hair that might one day allow methylation to be inferred without bisulfite conversion, which is an early-stage finding and nothing to plan around.

## What to do if you want a number anyway

**Ask which clock.** If the answer is a first-generation clock, the product is estimating your calendar age, which you already know.

**Ask for the validation.** Correlation against what, mean absolute error of what size, published where. **No answer is an answer.**

**Do not act on a single reading.** The cohort evidence that carried real signal was about change over time, measured repeatedly, in the same lab, by the same assay.

**Treat an improvement smaller than the test's own instability as nothing at all.** On the published reliability ranges, that bar is higher than most people assume.

**And keep the measurement separate from the intervention.** A clock that moves is not evidence that a supplement works; we went through what that standard of evidence looks like in [the plasmalogen supplement evidence](/blog/plasmalogen-supplement-evidence), and the related question of what researchers are actually resetting in [cellular reprogramming explained](/blog/cellular-reprogramming-explained).

## FAQ

### Are epigenetic age tests accurate?

They are reproducible more than they are stable. A 2026 *Aging Cell* analysis found clocks give consistent answers when the same sample is run twice, but vary across ordinary conditions in the same person, with pooled reliability in the moderate-to-good range and none reaching excellent.

### Which epigenetic clock is best?

It depends what you want predicted. First-generation clocks like Horvath's estimate chronological age. GrimAge and PhenoAge were trained against mortality and clinical markers and track health outcomes better. DunedinPACE estimates a rate of ageing rather than an age.

### Can my biological age go down?

A reading can go down. Whether that represents a biological change is a separate question, because the measurement moves with ordinary conditions and with laboratory handling. A change smaller than the test's own variability is not evidence of anything.

### Are consumer epigenetic age tests FDA approved?

Generally no. These are typically sold as wellness products rather than cleared or approved medical devices, and a 2026 review noted an absence of independent peer-reviewed validation for several commercial clocks.

### Can you test epigenetic age from hair or nails?

In research settings, small targeted panels have been run on nail material, and on plucked hairs with intact roots, with frequent failures. Hair shafts without roots are poor material. This is not how consumer tests are run, which generally use blood or saliva.

### Does MySpawn measure biological age?

No. MySpawn stores a physical DNA record and runs no tests of any kind. We do not operate epigenetic clocks and make no claim that a stored sample will support one in future.
