# Does Cremation Destroy DNA?

**Yes, almost always.** Cremation runs at roughly **1,400 to 1,800 degrees Fahrenheit**, around **1,000 degrees Celsius** in many descriptions, and DNA is a molecule that comes apart far below that. Soft tissue is gone entirely. Most bone structure is gone. **Occasionally, dense fragments such as teeth or parts of a femur survive partially**, and in rare cases a degraded profile can be recovered from them, more often mitochondrial than nuclear. **But that is a forensic long shot, not a plan.** If you want a readable copy of someone's genome, it has to exist before the cremation, not after it.

## The temperature is the entire answer

There is no clever workaround here and it is worth being blunt about that.

DNA is held together by chemical bonds that break with heat, water and time. **Cooking destroys it. Fire destroys it faster.** A cremation chamber is not a fire in the ordinary sense; it is a sustained industrial furnace running for a period of hours at temperatures that reduce bone to brittle fragments, which are then processed mechanically into what a family receives.

**Reported figures put the process at roughly 1,400 to 1,800 Fahrenheit, with some descriptions citing up to 1,000 Celsius and above.** At that range, the question is not whether DNA degrades. It is whether any molecule long enough to type survives anywhere in the chamber.

The honest summary from the forensic literature, as reported, is that **obtaining reliable DNA from intensively burned human remains is extremely difficult.** Not impossible. Extremely difficult.

## What sometimes survives, and why it is not something to rely on

Three situations account for most of the exceptions.

**Density.** Teeth and the thickest parts of long bones are the most heat-resistant tissue in the body. **If anything survives, it is usually there.**

**Shielding.** A fragment positioned so that it is not directly exposed to the flame can experience a lower effective temperature than the chamber reading suggests.

**Incompleteness.** Not every cremation runs identically. **Where the process was shorter or cooler than standard, more material survives.**

**Here is why none of that is a strategy.** You do not control which fragments survive, you do not know in advance whether any did, and **you find out by paying a laboratory to try and be told that it failed.** Planning around an exception you cannot verify or influence is not planning.

## Mitochondrial DNA lasts longer, and answers a narrower question

When nuclear DNA is destroyed, laboratories often turn to **mitochondrial DNA**, and it is worth understanding why that helps and how much.

Mitochondrial DNA sits in the cell's mitochondria rather than the nucleus. It is a **short circular genome** and each cell carries **many copies of it**, against two copies of the nuclear genome. **More copies and a smaller target means a better chance that something readable survives damage.** The same copy-number logic is why nail clippings and hair hold up better than people expect, which we walked through in [the best DNA sample type to preserve](/blog/best-dna-sample-type).

**The catch is what it can tell you.** Mitochondrial DNA is inherited down the maternal line and is shared by everyone in that line. **It can support a relationship question. It is not a personal genome**, it does not carry most of what people mean when they say "my DNA", and it cannot substitute for a nuclear sample in the way a family usually hopes.

## FAQ

### Can you get DNA from cremated ashes?
Usually no. The temperatures involved destroy genetic material. In a minority of cases, degraded DNA can be recovered from dense surviving fragments such as teeth or parts of a long bone, more often mitochondrial than nuclear, and success is not predictable in advance.

### How hot does cremation get?
Reported figures put the process at roughly 1,400 to 1,800 degrees Fahrenheit, with some descriptions citing up to 1,000 degrees Celsius and above. DNA degrades far below that range.

### Is mitochondrial DNA recoverable after cremation?
It is the more likely of the two to survive, because the mitochondrial genome is short and present in many copies per cell. It is inherited down the maternal line and shared by relatives, so it can support a relationship question but is not a personal genome.

### Does burial preserve DNA better than cremation?
Almost always yes, in the narrow sense that burial does not apply extreme heat. But buried remains still degrade through moisture, microbes and enzymes, usually far faster than a properly stabilized stored sample. Neither is a preservation method.

### Should I collect a DNA sample before a cremation?
If nothing was preserved and a death has already occurred, speaking to the funeral director before the procedure is the last point at which anything is possible. The far better version of this decision is made years earlier, from a living person who consented.

### Can a funeral home collect DNA for me?
Some offer it, and the offer is usually made at-need, at the point of arrangement. The information is accurate. Consider the timing separately from the fact, and be cautious about signing anything in that week.
