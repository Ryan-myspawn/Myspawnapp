## What all four do well — and the honest caveats

**StoryWorth** invented the category's rhythm: one emailed question a week, a bound book at the end. The caveat is the workload sits on the storyteller — if dad doesn't like writing, the book quietly dies in month two.

**Remento** fixes the typing problem: he talks, AI drafts, the family gets both the audio and a readable book. Caveat: the written voice is partly the machine's.

**Storii** solves the problem nobody else touches — the relative with no smartphone, no Wi-Fi, no inclination to check email. Scheduled phone calls, recorded and transcribed. Caveat: output is an archive, not a designed book.

**Tell Mel** goes furthest on ease: the AI conducts an actual conversation. Caveat: an AI interviewer means an AI's choice of follow-up questions — charming to some storytellers, uncanny to others.

Any of these makes a genuinely good gift. ([More in our legacy-tier gift guide](/blog/meaningful-gifts-for-men-legacy).)

## The gap none of them fill

Here's the thing we'd want said plainly if we were the buyer: **every service above preserves narrative — what someone says about their life.** Three other layers routinely get lost, and no memoir subscription holds them:

1. **The delivery.** A transcript keeps the fish story; it loses the pause before the punchline. If the point is *him*, thirty seconds of deliberate video beats a chapter. This is what MySpawn's Memory Vault is for: video messages sealed to milestone days — an 18th birthday, a graduation — held independently of any platform's fate. (Not a memoir service; a time-capsule one. Different job.)
2. **The access.** Books survive on shelves; digital archives survive only if someone can legally reach them — [platform tools and estate law decide that, not good intentions](/blog/digital-legacy-what-happens).
3. **The source file.** Stories describe a person; biology *is* the person, at the record level. A hair-and-nails sample in accredited storage — [keratin is the medium built for decades](/blog/best-dna-sample-type) — keeps the one archive no interview can reconstruct. That's MySpawn's actual product: $99/year, first 1,000 members lock the founding rate, stored by GenVault (independent CAP-accredited, ISO 9001/20387, FDA-registered biorepository, New Jersey). Storage only, said plainly: nobody can promise what future science does with any sample; the promise is custody.

Our honest recommendation is a stack, not a swap: **pick one memoir service by how your storyteller talks, and put the layers it can't hold — video and the biological record — somewhere built for them.** ([Full how-to here](/blog/how-to-preserve-your-dna).)

## FAQ

**What is the best StoryWorth alternative?**
Remento (~$99/yr) if your storyteller prefers speaking to typing; Storii ($9.99/mo) for relatives without smartphones or email; Tell Mel ($19-39/mo) if an AI-guided phone conversation suits them better than solo prompts.

**Is StoryWorth worth it in 2026?**
If the storyteller genuinely likes writing, yes — $59-199/yr for a weekly-question rhythm and a hardcover book remains the category standard. Its books stall when typing feels like homework.

**Is there a StoryWorth option for someone without a smartphone?**
Yes — Storii works entirely over ordinary phone calls, recording and transcribing scheduled conversations.

**What do memoir services not preserve?**
Delivery (voice, timing, face), long-term digital access rights, and the biological record. Those need video time-capsules, estate/platform setup, and DNA storage respectively.

**Can I combine a memoir service with MySpawn?**
That's the sensible stack: a memoir service for the narrative, MySpawn for milestone video messages and the stored biological record ($99/year). They do different jobs.

**How much do family memory services cost in 2026?**
Roughly $59-199 per year for book-based services (StoryWorth, Remento) and $10-39 per month for call-based ones (Storii, Tell Mel).
