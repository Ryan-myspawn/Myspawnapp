# What Happens to Your DNA When a Company Shuts Down?

*Published: August 21, 2026 · Lane (d) trust and custody · myspawn.me/blog*

When a DNA company shuts down or goes bankrupt, your genetic data is treated as a business asset. It can be sold to the highest bidder through bankruptcy court, transferred to a new owner you never chose, or, in the best case, protected by settlement terms that attorneys general fight for after the fact. That is not a hypothetical. It happened to 15 million people when 23andMe filed for Chapter 11 in March 2025. The good news: you can protect yourself, and the difference comes down to two things most people never check, physical custody and accreditation.

## The 23andMe case: the largest genetic custody lesson in history

Here is the timeline, because the details matter more than the headlines:

- **October 2023:** a credential-stuffing attack exposed personal and genetic profile data tied to roughly **6.9 million users** ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/09/16/security_in_brief/)).
- **2024:** 23andMe agreed to a class action settlement of up to **$30 million** over the breach ([ClassAction.org](https://www.classaction.org/blog/23andme-data-breach-settlement-30m-deal-covers-millions-whose-info-was-stolen)).
- **March 23, 2025:** 23andMe Holding Co. filed for **Chapter 11 bankruptcy**. Its database of about 15 million customers was suddenly an asset on the block.
- **July 2025:** after a bidding war that briefly saw Regeneron winning at $256 million, the company's assets sold for about **$305 million to TTAM Research Institute**, a nonprofit led by 23andMe cofounder Anne Wojcicki ([Recording Law](https://www.recordinglaw.com/news/23andme-data-breach-settlement-approved/)).
- **July 2026:** a coalition of 43 state attorneys general settled bankruptcy claims, binding the new owner to enhanced security, independent oversight, and permanent consumer deletion rights ([Tennessee AG](https://www.tn.gov/attorneygeneral/news/2026/7/14/pr26-29.html)). New York separately secured **$18 million** for failing to protect customers' genetic data ([NY AG](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-secures-18-million-23andme-failing-protect-customers)).
- **July 7, 2026:** the bankruptcy court approved a **$46.75 million** distribution to roughly 7 million breach claimants, about $32.5 million of it for the consolidated class claims ([Recording Law](https://www.recordinglaw.com/news/23andme-data-breach-settlement-approved/), [23andMe Data Settlement](https://www.23andmedatasettlement.com/)).

Read that timeline again from the customer's side. Your genome was breached, then auctioned, and the protections arrived years later, through litigation, after the fact.

## In bankruptcy, your genetic data is an asset

United States bankruptcy law exists to maximize value for creditors. Customer databases are property of the bankruptcy estate, and unless a company's privacy policy or a court says otherwise, they can be sold. Public interest groups urged investigations of the 23andMe sale precisely because the buyer acquired the DNA database itself ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/former-23andmes-ceo-acquires-dna-database-through-nonprofit-public-citizen-urges-california-ag-to-investigate/)). Legal scholars now describe consumer genetic privacy as "precarious" for exactly this reason ([Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adz7229)).

A privacy policy is a promise from a company. When the company dies, the promise is only as strong as whatever a court decides next.

## The law that does not cover this

Many people assume a federal law protects their genetic data. The main one, **GINA** (the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008), only bars health insurers and employers from using genetic information against you. It says nothing about life insurance, long-term care insurance, data sales, or what happens in a bankruptcy. State laws are a patchwork: some states now grant deletion rights for genetic data, most do not, and none of them prevent a court-supervised asset sale. The 2026 multistate settlement had to write protections into a private agreement because no statute supplied them ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/privacy--consent--and-national-security-after-the-23andme-bankruptcy)).

## Data and samples are two different fates

Here is the distinction that decides everything: **a database entry and a physical sample are not the same thing, and they fail differently.**

- **Data companies** (testing apps, ancestry databases) hold a digital file derived from your spit kit. When they fail, the file is an asset, and you saw above where assets go. This is also the pattern in adjacent industries: when the AI memoir service HereAfter AI shut down in 2026, users were told to email support to retrieve recordings of their deceased relatives, with no published deadline or guarantee ([Afterlife AI](https://www.afterlife.ai/hereafter-ai-shutting-down)).
- **Accredited biorepositories** hold the physical material itself under laboratory standards written for hospitals and research institutions: **CAP accreditation** (College of American Pathologists), **ISO 20387** (the international biobanking standard), and **FDA registration**. These facilities operate under chain-of-custody rules, documented transfer procedures, and inspection regimes that exist independently of any one company's marketing site.

An accredited facility can still have a parent company fail. The difference is that a physical sample under a custody chain has somewhere lawful and documented to go, and a regulator watching the transfer. A row in a startup's database has neither by default.

This matters more every year because the science that could one day read or use preserved material keeps advancing, from whole-genome sequencing costs collapsing to early-stage work on lab-made reproductive cells (we explain the honest state of that field in [What is IVG?](https://myspawn.me/blog/what-is-ivg-in-vitro-gametogenesis)). A sample that no longer exists, or sits in an unaccountable warehouse, benefits from none of it.

## Five questions to ask before you trust anyone with your DNA

1. **Who physically holds my sample, and are they accredited?** Look for CAP, ISO 20387, and FDA registration by name. If the answer is "our secure facility" with no accreditations listed, that is your answer.
2. **Is the storage provider independent of the brand that sold me the kit?** Independence means a failed marketing company does not drag the samples down with it. (This is how MySpawn is built: the $99 kit is ours, but samples are held by GenVault, an independent, CAP-accredited, ISO 9001 and ISO 20387, FDA-registered biorepository in New Jersey. If MySpawn vanished tomorrow, your sample would still sit in an accredited facility with a documented custody chain.)
3. **What exactly is stored, data or material?** A file can be copied and sold invisibly. Physical material has to be moved, logged, and accounted for.
4. **Can I destroy it, and how?** A real custodian gives you a documented destruction option, not a settings toggle that may or may not purge backups.
5. **What is the written continuity plan?** Ask what happens to samples if the company is acquired or dissolved. A serious answer names procedures. A vague answer is a warning.

## FAQ

**Can a bankrupt DNA company really sell my genetic data?**
Yes. Customer data is generally property of the bankruptcy estate and can be sold under court supervision, as the 2025 sale of 23andMe's 15-million-person database showed. Protections, where they exist, come from settlement terms, state laws, and court conditions, not from an automatic federal shield.

**Does GINA protect my DNA if a company closes?**
No. GINA only restricts health insurers and employers. It does not cover data sales, bankruptcy transfers, life insurance, or long-term care insurance.

**Is my physical DNA sample safer than my DNA data?**
Generally yes, if it is held by an accredited biorepository. Physical samples under CAP or ISO 20387 custody follow documented chain-of-custody and transfer rules. Digital profiles can be copied, breached, or sold as ordinary business assets.

**How do I delete my 23andMe data after the bankruptcy?**
Log in to your account, download any data you want to keep, then use Settings, then "23andMe Data," then "Permanently Delete Data." The 2026 attorneys-general settlement binds the new owner to honor deletion rights.

**What accreditations should a DNA storage company have?**
The facility that physically holds samples should hold CAP accreditation and ISO 20387 (biobanking), and be FDA-registered. Ask specifically about the storage facility, which may be a different entity than the brand selling the kit.
