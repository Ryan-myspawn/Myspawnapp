# Polygenic Embryo Screening: What the Scores Can and Cannot Tell You

**Polygenic embryo screening (PGT-P) scores IVF embryos on statistical risk for complex traits — heart disease, diabetes, schizophrenia, and at some companies height and IQ — by adding up thousands of tiny genetic effects. The scores are real statistics, but they are population statistics applied to one embryo, and the realistic benefit of choosing between siblings is small: the best-known peer-reviewed simulation put it at roughly 2.5 IQ points or 2.5 centimetres when selecting from five embryos. In December 2025 the American Society for Reproductive Medicine concluded the technology is not ready for clinical use. Here is what the numbers actually support.**

## What polygenic embryo screening actually is

Most genetic testing during IVF looks for single, large-effect problems: a specific mutation that causes cystic fibrosis, or a missing or extra chromosome. That is PGT-M and PGT-A, and it is well established.

PGT-P is different in kind. Complex traits — height, blood pressure, diabetes risk, cognitive test scores — are not caused by one gene. They emerge from interactions among hundreds or thousands of variants, each contributing a sliver. A polygenic score adds those slivers into a single number, then ranks the available embryos by it.

The first clinical application arrived in 2019 from Genomic Prediction. Orchid followed with whole-genome sequencing of embryos, and in 2025 Herasight and Nucleus Genomics entered with substantially broader claims, including intelligence ([MIT Technology Review, January 12, 2026](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/01/12/1130011/embryo-scoring-genetic-testing-2026-breakthrough-technology/)). MIT Technology Review named embryo scoring one of its 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2026 — worth reading carefully, because that is an *editorial selection of what will matter*, not a clinical endorsement, and the same article calls the technology controversial.

## What the scores can tell you

It is worth being fair to the science before criticising its application, because the underlying statistics are not fake.

Polygenic scores genuinely stratify risk across populations. Take a large group of people, sort them by polygenic score for coronary artery disease, and the top and bottom of that distribution really do have different rates of disease. For some conditions the signal is meaningful: one [peer-reviewed eLife analysis](https://elifesciences.org/articles/64716) estimated the proportion of variance in liability explained at roughly 6% for Crohn's disease, 7% for schizophrenia, and 9% for type 2 diabetes.

That is a real finding, and it is why serious researchers work on polygenic scores at all. The problem is not that the numbers are invented. The problem is what happens when you take a population statistic and point it at one embryo.

## The five limits that matter

### 1. Siblings are genetically similar, so there is little to choose between

This is the constraint most marketing skips. You are not selecting from the whole human range. You are selecting among embryos from the same two parents, who share roughly half their DNA with each other. The spread of polygenic scores within one sibship is narrow by definition.

The landmark simulation here is [Karavani et al., *Cell*, 2019](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(19)31210-3), which is peer-reviewed and still the reference point. Selecting the top-scoring embryo out of five produced an expected gain of about **2.5 centimetres of height or about 2.5 IQ points**. Widening to an unrealistic batch of 50 embryos raised it only to roughly 4.5. The gain rises very slowly with the number of embryos.

The same team then checked the prediction against reality using 28 families with ten or more adult children. In most of those large families, the child with the highest polygenic score for height **was not the tallest one**.

### 2. "Variance explained" is not a prediction about your child

Nine percent of variance explained means the score accounts for nine percent of the differences across a population. It does not mean the score is ninety-one percent likely to be wrong, and it does not mean it is nine percent right about any individual. It means most of what determines the outcome is not in the number you were handed.

For an individual embryo, a polygenic score is a probability shift, not a forecast. A company can be entirely honest about its statistics and still leave a parent believing they have been told something about a person.

### 3. Scores shrink when you use them inside a family

Polygenic scores are usually validated on unrelated people, and that inflates them. Some of the apparent predictive power comes from [population stratification, assortative mating, and indirect parental effects](https://academic.oup.com/humupd/article/30/5/529/7684172) — the last of these often called "genetic nurture," where a parent's genotype shapes the environment they build rather than the child's biology directly.

None of that transfers when you compare embryos from the *same parents*, because the parents and the environment are held constant. Sibling comparison is exactly the setting where polygenic scores are weakest, and it is exactly the setting PGT-P operates in.

### 4. Accuracy falls outside European-ancestry datasets

Polygenic scores are built from genome-wide association studies, and those studies remain heavily skewed toward people of European ancestry. Performance is [substantially worse in non-European populations](https://academic.oup.com/humupd/article/30/5/529/7684172), and varies across socio-economic groups too. A score sold as a universal product does not perform universally.

### 5. Real IVF does not hand you a shelf of embryos to pick from

Simulations often assume a set number of viable embryos and that the chosen one becomes a baby. [Modelling using real-world IVF patient data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13131720/) shows how much that assumption flatters the technology: many transfers do not produce a live birth, and a great many cycles yield one usable embryo or none. If you have one embryo, there is nothing to select. The screening question never arises.

## Where the professional bodies stand

In December 2025, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine's Ethics and Practice Committees concluded that **PGT-P is not ready for clinical practice and should not be offered as a reproductive service at this time**, citing both predictive uncertainty and substantial ethical concerns. Their position is that any use should occur in research settings under institutional review board oversight until those concerns are addressed ([ASRM Ethics Committee opinion, 2026](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/use-of-preimplantation-genetic-testing-for-polygenic-disorders-pgt-p-an-ethics-committee-opinion-2026/); [Healio Q&A, January 2026](https://www.healio.com/news/womens-health-ob-gyn/20260109/qa-polygenic-embryo-screening-not-ready-for-clinical-practice-asrm-ethics-group-says)).

That is a notable gap: a technology on a breakthrough list in January 2026, and a professional-society recommendation against clinical use from a month earlier. Both things are true at once. A technology can be genuinely important, well funded, and widely covered, and still not be ready to make a decision with.

Coverage has also been pointed about trait screening specifically, particularly claims around intelligence ([NPR, May 6, 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/05/06/nx-s1-5704317/genetic-embryo-screening)). Disease-risk screening and IQ screening get discussed as one product; scientifically they are not remotely equivalent, and the IQ claims are the weakest part of the category.

## If you are actually considering it

A few practical things, offered without a position on your decision:

- **Ask what the absolute numbers are, not the relative ones.** "40% risk reduction" on a condition with a 1% baseline is a shift from 1% to 0.6%. Ask for it in percentage points.
- **Ask how many embryos you are likely to have.** With one or two, the exercise is mostly theoretical.
- **Ask which ancestry the score was validated in**, and whether it matches yours.
- **Ask what the score does *not* cover** — polygenic risk is one input alongside family history, environment, and chance.
- **Separate disease screening from trait screening** in your own head, even if a company sells them together.
- **Talk to a genetic counsellor who is not employed by the testing company.** This is the single highest-value step, and it is the one most often skipped.

## FAQ

### Is polygenic embryo screening legal in the US?
There is no federal ban. It is offered commercially by several companies. But legality is not the same as clinical endorsement — ASRM's Ethics and Practice Committees concluded in December 2025 that it is not ready for clinical use and should be confined to research settings with IRB oversight.

### How accurate is polygenic embryo screening for IQ?
Far less accurate than marketing implies. The peer-reviewed simulation benchmark found an expected gain of about 2.5 IQ points when selecting the top embryo of five, and real-family data showed the highest-scoring sibling frequently is not the highest-scoring adult. Cognitive polygenic scores are also particularly vulnerable to the genetic-nurture and population-stratification problems described above.

### What is the difference between PGT-A, PGT-M, and PGT-P?
PGT-A screens for chromosomal abnormalities (wrong number of chromosomes). PGT-M screens for a specific known single-gene disorder in the family. PGT-P scores complex, many-gene traits statistically. The first two look for a defined thing; the third estimates a probability.

### Does a low polygenic risk score mean my child won't get the disease?
No. A polygenic score shifts a probability. Most of the variation in almost every complex condition is not captured by the score, and environment and chance remain substantial. A low score is not protection and a high score is not a diagnosis.

### Can polygenic scores predict eye colour or height reliably?
Some physical traits with simpler genetic architecture predict better than behavioural ones. Height is among the better-predicted complex traits and *still* only yields about 2.5 cm of expected gain across five embryos, because sibling embryos are so genetically similar.

### Is storing my DNA the same as embryo screening?
No. Screening selects between embryos; preservation keeps a record of an existing person's DNA. They are different technologies aimed at different questions, and preservation involves no selection, no ranking, and no reproductive decision.
