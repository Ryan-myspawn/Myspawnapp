If you're searching for a 23andMe alternative in 2026, here's the direct answer: for ancestry and relative-matching, AncestryDNA is the biggest database in the category (27M+ samples, ~$99); MyHeritage is the budget option; and 23andMe itself still operates under new ownership. But the reason you're searching is probably not features. It's that you watched a DNA company go bankrupt with 15 million people's genetic data on its books. So before comparing report quality, ask the question that actually matters: **who has custody of your sample and your data, and what happens to them when the company fails?** This article gives you that checklist.

## What actually happened to 23andMe (the 90-second version)

The timeline matters, because it's the case study every "alternative" should be measured against.

- **October 2023:** a credential-stuffing attack exposed data linked to about **6.9 million customers** — nearly half the customer base with connected features.
- **March 2025:** 23andMe filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Its most valuable asset was the thing customers thought was theirs: the genetic database.
- **2025:** the assets — including consumer data — were sold to TTAM Research Institute, a nonprofit formed by 23andMe's own co-founder. Customers' data changed legal hands through a bankruptcy court.
- **July 14, 2026:** 42 state attorneys general announced a **$150 million settlement** over the breach. Because the money comes out of a finite bankruptcy estate, the states' recovery is capped at **$18 million**, and the customer class settlement — **$46.75 million**, reduced by $14.29 million already paid — works out to an estimated **~$100 statutory payment** for most affected customers, with larger sums reserved for documented extraordinary harm. (Sources: Massachusetts AG and Pennsylvania AG announcements, July 2026; court approval reported the same month.)

Read that last line again. Roughly a hundred dollars, three years after the breach, filtered through bankruptcy accounting. The lesson isn't "23andMe was uniquely bad." The lesson is structural: **when a data company fails, your genetic information is an asset in someone else's insolvency.**

## The two different jobs people mean by "alternative"

Most "best 23andMe alternatives" lists compare testing features. That's fair — but searchers are actually shopping for two different things, and conflating them is how people end up disappointed.

### Job 1: Analysis — "tell me about my DNA"

Ancestry percentages, relative matching, trait and health-risk reports. This is what 23andMe, AncestryDNA, and MyHeritage sell. If this is your job-to-be-done, the 2026 landscape is straightforward:

- **AncestryDNA (~$99):** the largest matching database in the category — the practical choice for genealogy and finding relatives, with controls to switch DNA matching off. No health-risk reports.
- **23andMe (under TTAM ownership):** still the most detailed consumer health/trait reporting; you'll have to decide how much the 2023-2026 history weighs against that.
- **MyHeritage:** cheapest entry point, smaller database, coarser estimates.

One honest note that "alternative" lists rarely include: **every one of these requires uploading your genome into a company database.** The feature you're comparing is the analysis; the risk you're accepting is the custody.

### Job 2: Preservation — "keep my DNA safe for the long term"

This is the quieter job, and no testing company is built for it. Testing firms are data businesses: your sample is consumed, your genome becomes rows in their database, and their incentives revolve around monetizing aggregate data. Preservation is a custody business: the point is that your **physical biological sample** exists decades from now, held by an institution whose only job is keeping it, regardless of what happens to any brand. Knowing which job you're hiring for changes which questions matter. For analysis, compare databases and reports. For preservation — and increasingly, for analysis too — ask the custody questions below.

## The custody checklist: 5 questions to ask any DNA company in 2026

We published a version of this framework when [23andMe's shutdown mechanics were still news](/blog/what-happens-dna-company-shuts-down); the July settlement makes it worth restating as the buying checklist.

### 1. Who physically holds my sample — the brand or an accredited custodian?
If the answer is "the company itself," the sample's fate is tied to the company's fate. Independent, accredited biorepositories (look for CAP accreditation, ISO 20387 — the biobanking standard — and FDA registration) exist precisely to separate custody from any one brand's survival.

### 2. What does the contract say happens in bankruptcy?
Not the privacy policy — the terms. 23andMe's customers learned that a database can be sold as an estate asset with the court's blessing. Ask directly: is my data/sample an asset that can be transferred to a buyer? Under what conditions can I retrieve or destroy it first?

### 3. Can I delete or retrieve on demand — and is that audited?
A deletion button is marketing; a documented chain of custody with confirmation is an operational fact. Ask which one you're getting.

### 4. What's actually stored — data, sample, or both?
Data-only means your privacy exposure is total but your material is gone (the sample is consumed in testing). Sample-preservation means the physical material still exists and any future use stays your call. These are opposite risk profiles; know which you're buying.

### 5. What law actually protects me?
The federal Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) blocks health insurers and employers from using genetic data — but **not** life, disability, or long-term-care insurers. State genetic-privacy laws vary widely. If a company's privacy pitch leans on "we comply with applicable law," remember how much law doesn't apply.

## So what should you actually do?

**If you want ancestry answers:** use a testing service with your eyes open — AncestryDNA if matching matters most — and treat the upload as a permanent disclosure decision, because as a practical matter it is.

**If you want your DNA to exist, safely, long-term:** that's preservation, not testing. Keep the record in accredited third-party custody, on terms where nobody's bankruptcy can auction it.

**If you want both:** do them separately, deliberately. There is no single product that maximizes analysis and custody at once, and anyone claiming otherwise is hand-waving one of the two.

The 23andMe story isn't a reason to fear DNA services. It's a reason to buy them the way you'd buy any long-horizon service: read the custody terms first, and assume the brand on the box will not outlive your need for what's inside it.

## FAQ

**Is 23andMe safe to use now?**
23andMe operates under TTAM Research Institute, a nonprofit formed by its co-founder, after the 2025 bankruptcy sale. The service functions, and the settlement resolved state claims. "Safe" depends on your risk tolerance: the structural issue — your genome living in a company database — is unchanged at any testing company.

**What is the best 23andMe alternative in 2026?**
For ancestry and relative matching, AncestryDNA (largest database, ~$99, matching can be disabled). For budget testing, MyHeritage. For long-term preservation of your DNA rather than analysis of it, use a dedicated preservation service with independent accredited storage — a different product category.

**How much are 23andMe breach victims getting paid?**
Under the settlements approved in July 2026, most affected customers who filed claims receive an estimated $100 statutory payment; documented extraordinary-harm claims can reach up to $10,000, and health-information claims about $165, plus genetic-monitoring services. States' own recovery was capped at $18 million by the bankruptcy estate.

**Can a company legally sell my DNA data in bankruptcy?**
23andMe's database was sold through bankruptcy proceedings in 2025, with conditions negotiated in court. Whether and how that can happen to you depends on the specific company's terms and your state's law — which is why the contract's bankruptcy language is checklist item #2.

**Does deleting my 23andMe account remove my data?**
You can request deletion, and the multistate settlement strengthened consumer data rights. But deletion applies prospectively to their systems; it cannot recall data already exposed in 2023, and some information may be retained where law requires.

**Is DNA preservation a replacement for DNA testing?**
No — they're different jobs. Testing analyzes your genome and gives you reports; preservation keeps your physical sample in custody for the decades ahead. Preservation doesn't tell you your ancestry, and testing doesn't keep your sample. Choose by which job you need done; some people sensibly do both.
