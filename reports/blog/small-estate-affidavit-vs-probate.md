# Small Estate Affidavit vs Probate: What It Is, and When It Actually Wins

**A small estate affidavit is a sworn statement that lets you collect a dead person's property without opening a probate case.** You sign it, hand it to whoever holds the asset, and they release it to you. It works only under a dollar ceiling set by your state, only after a waiting period, and only for the kinds of property the statute names. The ceilings differ by more than three to one between states, they moved in 2025 and again in 2026, and the thing nobody tells you is that signing one transfers the dead person's unsecured debts onto you, up to the value of what you collected.

## It is a receipt, not a court case

The confusion starts with the word "affidavit." In most states this is not a filing. It is a document you present to a bank, a transfer agent, a credit union or an employer holding a final paycheck, and the institution's own risk policy decides whether to honor it.

**California is the clearest example.** The declaration under Probate Code section 13100 is not filed with a court at all. You deliver it to the holder of the asset. There is no judge, no hearing, no docket number and no order. Our sources disagree about whether the Judicial Council publishes an optional form for the declaration itself; what they agree on is that the Council publishes **form DE-300, "Maximum Values for Small Estate Set-Aside and Dispositions," revised 28 April 2025**, which is the chart carrying the current dollar limits.

**New York and Texas work the other way.** New York's route is a filing with the Surrogate's Court in the county where the person lived, on **form Small Estate - Aff SE-3A**, an affidavit for voluntary administration under Article 13 of the Surrogate's Court Procedure Act. Texas requires a filing in the probate court, and **approval sits in the court's discretion**: the statute does not oblige a judge to grant it.

That split matters more than the dollar figure. An affidavit you hand to a bank can be refused by that bank for its own reasons, and you have no order to wave at it. A court-approved affidavit is harder to get and much harder to argue with afterwards.

## Four states, four different answers

**California.** Personal property up to **$208,850** for deaths on or after 1 April 2025, up from $184,500 for deaths between April 2022 and March 2025. The limit is fixed by the **date of death, not the date you file**, which is the single most common error in this area. A separate route under section 13151 covers a primary residence up to **$750,000** by petition rather than affidavit, and real property that is not a primary residence runs through a different affidavit under section 13200. One source puts that figure at $69,625; only one source carried it, and we are not treating it as established.

**Texas.** Non-exempt estate property up to **$75,000**, excluding a homestead passing to a surviving spouse or minor children and certain exempt personal property, under Chapter 205 of the Estates Code. Available **only if there is no will**. The affidavit must be sworn by two disinterested witnesses and by each distributee, and must list all known assets and liabilities. It generally does not transfer title to real property, with a narrow statutory exception for a qualifying homestead.

**Florida.** Summary administration up to **$150,000** in non-exempt value as of 1 July 2026, double the previous $75,000, under CS/HB 1337, enacted as Chapter 2026-57 and approved on 29 April 2026. Homestead and exempt personal property under section 732.402 do not count toward the ceiling. The alternative route is unchanged: an estate also qualifies if the person **died more than two years ago**, at any value. The same bill raised the intestate disposition limit for personal property from $10,000 to $20,000.

**New York.** Personal property with a gross value of **$50,000 or less**, excluding property set off for a surviving spouse or children under EPTL 5-3.1(a), under SCPA section 1301.

**One trap worth naming.** New York has a second document also called a small estates affidavit, under SCPA 1310, published by the Office of the State Comptroller. It is for claiming unclaimed funds held by a debtor and it is **not** the voluntary administration route. Two different instruments, almost the same name.

## The waiting period is where plans break

**California: 40 days from death**, for both the section 13100 declaration and the section 13151 residence petition. **Texas: 30 days**, and the affidavit cannot be filed earlier. **Florida and New York**, on our sources, impose no equivalent minimum wait for the simplified route, though Florida's two-year alternative is a waiting period of a different kind.

Forty days is longer than it sounds when a mortgage payment, a car note or a tuition bill is due. This is the practical reason to care about where documents live before anybody dies, which is the same problem we wrote about in [what belongs in a safe deposit box, and what does not](/blog/safe-deposit-box-after-death): the simplified route is fast only relative to probate, and it is still measured in weeks.

## What you are actually signing

Here is the part the free-template sites leave out.

**Under California Probate Code section 13109, the person who collects property by declaration is personally liable for the dead person's unsecured debts, capped at the value received** measured as fair market value less liens. Collect $50,000 against $80,000 of debt and your exposure is the $50,000, not nothing. **Section 13110 adds liability to anyone with a superior right to the property by will or intestate succession, and treble the property's value where the transfer was fraudulently secured.** Our sources disagree on the limitations period: some describe a three-year window, another argues section 13109 claims run under Code of Civil Procedure section 366.2 instead. We could not resolve that and are not going to pretend otherwise.

**And it is sworn.** Undervaluing an estate to squeeze under a ceiling, overstating your right to the property, or stating that no probate proceeding exists when one does, is perjury on its face before it is anything else.

That reframes the whole comparison. Probate is slow, public and expensive, and in exchange it gives you a creditor claims period that closes, a judge's order, and a clean break. The affidavit is fast and cheap, and in exchange **you keep the risk personally, for years.** For an estate that is genuinely simple and genuinely solvent, that trade is usually worth it. For an estate with business debts, medical bills, a disputed heir or an asset you cannot value confidently, it is a bad trade dressed as a shortcut.

## When it beats probate

It wins when all of these are true: the estate is under your state's current ceiling for the relevant date; you know the debts and they are comfortably smaller than the assets; every person with a claim to the property agrees in writing; and no real estate is involved beyond what your state's route specifically covers.

It loses, quietly, when the figure in a chart you found is last year's. **Texas is the proof.** An archived 2015 version of Chapter 205 still shows **$50,000** where the current figure is $75,000. The old text is indexed, it looks authoritative, and it is wrong by a third. Florida's old $75,000 is still sitting in hundreds of live pages. Verify the number against your state's own statute for your own date of death, not against a table.

## FAQ

### Do I need a lawyer to file a small estate affidavit?

No state on our list requires one. Whether you should is a different question. If the estate is under the ceiling, the debts are known and small, and the heirs agree, people routinely do this themselves. If any of those three is shaky, the personal liability you are accepting is worth an hour of professional time.

### What if the estate is slightly over the limit?

Then the simplified route is not available, and shaving the valuation to fit is the one move guaranteed to turn a paperwork problem into a legal one. Check whether your state excludes categories you are wrongly counting: homestead in Texas and Florida, and property set off for a spouse or children in New York, sit outside the ceiling.

### Does a small estate affidavit transfer real estate?

Usually not. Texas generally excludes real property other than a qualifying homestead. California routes a primary residence through a separate petition under section 13151 with a $750,000 ceiling, and other real property through a different affidavit entirely. Assume real estate is a separate process unless your state's statute says otherwise.

### Does the dollar limit depend on when the person died or when I file?

In California it is the date of death, which is why the $184,500 and $208,850 figures both still apply to different estates. Florida's practitioners are openly split on whether the new $150,000 ceiling reaches estates where death preceded 1 July 2026. Nobody should assume the filing date governs.

### Can a bank refuse a valid small estate affidavit?

Where the affidavit is not a court filing, as in California, yes: the institution applies its own policy, and a missing successor or an unclear valuation gives it a reason to decline. Where a court has approved the affidavit, refusal is much harder. That difference is a good reason to ask an institution what it requires before you draft anything.
