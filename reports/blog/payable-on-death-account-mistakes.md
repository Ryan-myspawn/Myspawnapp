# Payable on Death Accounts: Four Ways They Go Wrong

**Freshness check:** The September/October 2026 American Bar Association report of a new Uniform Transfers to Minors Act raising the custodial termination age to 25 is the newest item here, and state adoption is explicitly unconfirmed. The FDIC trust-account rule effective 1 April 2024 is the controlling number for coverage.
**Primary audience:** Men 25-55, plus 40-60 estate-planning readers.
**Trend-hook used:** None. This is standing search intent, and no news peg is claimed.

**A payable on death account passes your bank balance to the person you named on the form, without probate, as soon as the bank has a death certificate. The form takes five minutes and it works. What goes wrong is almost never the form itself: it is the four details underneath it, which are the number of beneficiaries, what happens if one of them dies first, what happens if one of them is a child, and how much of the money is actually insured.** This article covers those four. It does not re-explain that a beneficiary designation outranks your will, because that rule is covered in our [transfer on death deed article](/blog/transfer-on-death-deed-what-it-moves) and in [who inherits your DNA testing account](/blog/who-inherits-your-dna-testing-account).

**One disclosure before anything else: this is general information, not legal advice, and bank rules and state statutes differ enough that the only authoritative answers to these questions are your bank's account agreement and your own state's code.**

## What a POD account actually is

A payable on death account, sometimes written as POD or ITF ("in trust for"), is an ordinary bank account with a named beneficiary attached. **While you are alive, the beneficiary has no rights to the money at all.** You can spend it, move it, change the beneficiary, or close the account, and you do not need their permission or their knowledge.

When you die, the named person brings identification and a certified death certificate to the bank and the balance is paid to them directly. **It does not pass through your will and it does not wait for probate**, which is the entire point and the reason these accounts are recommended so often. If you want the broader picture of what probate costs in time, we covered that in [how long does probate take](/blog/how-long-does-probate-take).

## Mistake 1: Vague shares, or no shares at all

Most banks allow more than one POD beneficiary, and several consumer guides state there is no fixed limit on how many you can name. **The default treatment, where the bank allows it, is an equal split among the surviving beneficiaries, and you can usually specify unequal percentages instead.**

**The practical trap is that institutions generally want exact percentages rather than descriptive instructions.** "Split between my two children" is not a percentage. "Half to my brother and the rest divided among his kids" is not a percentage either. A form filled in with intentions rather than numbers is a form the bank has to interpret, and banks interpret conservatively.

**Write numbers that add to 100.**

## Mistake 2: Assuming the survivor gets it all

This one varies by state and it is the single most commonly misunderstood item on the list.

**If you name two POD beneficiaries and one dies before you, what happens to their share depends on your state and on your account agreement.** Some sources describe states where the surviving beneficiary simply takes the whole account. **Texas runs the other way: where multiple POD payees survive, there is no right of survivorship between them unless the account agreement expressly creates one.** [1]

**Those two outcomes are opposite, and both are reported as current.** We are not going to resolve that disagreement here, because the honest answer is that it is a state-law and contract question rather than a general rule, and an article that averaged them would be inventing a rule nobody legislated.

**The fix does not require you to know which rule your state follows: name contingent beneficiaries.** A POD designation whose only named beneficiary has died, with no backup named, does exactly what the account was set up to avoid, which is fall into the estate and go through probate.

## Mistake 3: Naming a child directly

**Banks will not hand a lump sum to a minor.** The consistent position across the consumer and law-firm guidance is that funds cannot be distributed directly to anyone under 18, and that if a minor is the named beneficiary, a court will generally appoint a guardian or conservator to hold the money until they reach adulthood.

So the account avoids probate and lands in a guardianship proceeding instead. **You have swapped one court process for another.**

**The standard fix is to name a custodian or a trustee rather than the child alone**, typically through a custodial arrangement under your state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act, or through a trust if the sums are large enough to justify one.

**Here the dates matter and they are genuinely unsettled.** The long-standing position is that UTMA custodianship ends at an age set by state law, **most commonly 21, with the real range across states running roughly 18 to 25**, and some states letting the person who created the account choose a later age. California is frequently cited as defaulting to 18 while permitting 25 to be specified. **Published state-by-state tables disagree with each other on individual states, including two tables from the same publisher**, which is why no table is reproduced here. [2]

**And the ground is moving. An American Bar Association article dated September/October 2026 reports a new Uniform Transfers to Minors Act that raises the custodial termination age to 25.** [3] **LABEL: that is a uniform act, which is a model law. It governs nothing until an individual state adopts it, and these sources do not confirm which states have. Treat 25 as a direction of travel, not as your state's current answer.**

**The point that survives all of that uncertainty: a child named directly on a POD form receives the entire balance the moment they hit whatever age applies, in cash, with no conditions on it.** If that outcome bothers you, the POD form is the wrong instrument and you want a trust.

## Mistake 4: Assuming all of it is insured

This is the mistake with a hard number attached, and it is the one most people have never heard of.

**Since 1 April 2024 the FDIC calculates deposit insurance on trust accounts, which includes POD and ITF accounts, as $250,000 multiplied by the number of eligible beneficiaries, capped at $1,250,000 per owner per bank.** [4] Naming a sixth beneficiary does not buy a sixth tranche of coverage.

**Two details inside that rule catch people out.** First, **only primary beneficiaries count toward the multiplier; contingent beneficiaries do not.** Second, the cap applies across **all** of your trust-type accounts at the same institution, not per account.

**Before the rule changed, revocable trust coverage was not capped at five beneficiaries, so some depositors who were fully insured under the old regime are not fully insured now.** Reporting at the time noted that trusts holding more than $1.25 million at a single bank could lose coverage, while the FDIC expected most depositors, who hold less than that, to see no change at all.

**A parallel rule for credit unions was proposed by the NCUA. One 2026-available source from 2024 describes it as not yet finalised, and we have not confirmed its current status, so do not assume your credit union applies identical arithmetic.**

## FAQ

### Can a payable on death account have more than one beneficiary?
Yes. Most banks allow several, and consumer guidance suggests no fixed upper limit. The default where permitted is an equal split among survivors, and you can usually set unequal percentages instead. **Institutions generally want exact percentages, so write numbers rather than instructions.**

### What happens if a POD beneficiary dies before me?
It depends on your state and your account agreement, and the published answers genuinely conflict. Some sources describe the surviving beneficiary taking the whole account, while **Texas provides that there is no right of survivorship between multiple POD payees unless the account agreement expressly creates one.** **Naming contingent beneficiaries removes the question.** If no named beneficiary survives you and no backup exists, the money typically falls into the estate and goes through probate.

### Can I name my child as a POD beneficiary?
You can name them, but **a bank will not pay a lump sum directly to someone under 18.** A court will generally appoint a guardian or conservator to hold it until they reach adulthood. Naming a custodian under your state's UTMA, or a trustee, is the usual alternative.

### At what age does a child actually get the money?
Whatever age your state sets, **most commonly 21, with the range across states running roughly 18 to 25**, and some states allowing a later age to be chosen when the account is created. **Published state tables disagree, including tables from the same publisher, so check your own state's statute.** An ABA article dated September/October 2026 reports a new uniform act raising the termination age to 25, **but a uniform act binds nobody until a state adopts it.**

### How much of a POD account is FDIC insured?
**Since 1 April 2024, $250,000 times the number of eligible primary beneficiaries, capped at $1,250,000 per owner per bank.** Contingent beneficiaries do not count, and the cap applies across all your trust-type accounts at that institution. **A credit union may differ: the NCUA's parallel rule was proposed and we have not confirmed it was finalised.**

### Does a POD beneficiary have any rights while I am alive?
No. You keep full control. You can spend the balance to zero, change the beneficiary, or close the account, without notifying the person named.
