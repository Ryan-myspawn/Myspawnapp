Here's the short answer up front: **it depends almost entirely on storage conditions, and the range is enormous.** In a bone buried at about 13°C, DNA has a measured half-life of roughly **521 years**. In Siberian permafrost, researchers have sequenced DNA from mammoth teeth **more than a million years old**. And in frozen Greenland sediment, fragments **two million years old** were recovered and read in 2022, the oldest DNA ever found. Left warm, wet, and exposed, the same molecule can become unreadable in years. Kept dry, dark, oxygen-free, and stable, it can outlast every institution that exists today.

That gap between "years" and "millions of years" is not luck. It's chemistry you can control. Let's walk through what the science actually says.

## The 521-year half-life, explained

In 2012, a team led by Morten Allentoft analyzed 158 radiocarbon-dated leg bones of the moa, an extinct New Zealand bird, all recovered from sites with nearly identical conditions at about 13.1°C. Because the bones spanned 600 to 8,000 years of age under matched conditions, the team could finally isolate time itself as a variable, and DNA decay behaved like radioactive decay: exponential, with a **half-life of 521 years** for a 242-base-pair mitochondrial fragment ([Allentoft et al., Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23055061/); [Scientific American's summary](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/dinos-dna-demise-genetic-material-512-year-half-life/)).

Two details from that study matter more than the headline number:

- **Temperature dominates.** The same math projects that at −5°C, the very last bonds in a DNA strand wouldn't break for roughly **6.8 million years**, though sequences would stop being readable long before that. Cold multiplies survival by orders of magnitude.
- **Age explained only ~40% of the variation** in DNA content between bones. The other 60% came down to everything else: water, microbes, oxygen, soil chemistry. Translation: *conditions matter more than time.*

That second point is the entire premise of modern biobanking.

The Greenland result deserves a beat of respect: the DNA wasn't even inside a fossil. It was loose genetic material shed into sediment by mastodons, caribou, and poplar trees, locked onto frozen mineral grains for two million years, and it was still recoverable enough to reconstruct an entire lost ecosystem ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/worlds-oldest-dna-discovered-revealing-ancient-arctic-forest-full-of-mastodons/)).

And no, this doesn't get us dinosaurs. At 66+ million years, every bond is long gone; the 6.8-million-year theoretical ceiling is why *Jurassic Park* stays fiction ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/moa-bones-reveal-dna-half-life-but-jurassic-park-remains-fiction-10067)).

## What actually destroys DNA

Four things do nearly all the damage:

1. **Water.** Hydrolysis constantly clips bases off the backbone. Wet DNA is dying DNA.
2. **Oxygen.** Oxidation chews at the bases themselves.
3. **Heat.** Every reaction above runs faster when warm. That's the whole half-life story.
4. **Life.** Microbes and enzymes (nucleases) treat DNA as food.

Remove those four and the molecule is startlingly durable. That's why the ancient-DNA records all share a profile — cold *or* dry, sealed away from oxygen and biology — and why the ones that combine several factors go deepest into the past.

## Does DNA need a freezer? What ambient-temperature studies show

Here's the counterintuitive part for anyone whose mental model is "freezer = safe": **dry beats cold.** Frozen storage works because it slows water chemistry, but freezers fail, and freeze-thaw cycles are themselves destructive. The professional alternative is removing the water and oxygen entirely.

Encapsulation studies bear this out. DNA dried and sealed in stainless-steel capsules under inert, anhydrous atmosphere (the DNAshell approach) has been through accelerated-aging tests at up to 140°C, and an Institut Pasteur-affiliated team found the stored DNA remained suitable for next-generation sequencing, with room-temperature degradation rates **too low to reliably measure** ([Institut Pasteur, 2019](https://research.pasteur.fr/en/publication/dnashell-protects-dna-stored-at-room-temperature-for-downstream-next-generation-sequencing-studies/); [PLOS One, 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0259868)). Arrhenius extrapolations from that work point to ambient-temperature stability measured in centuries to millennia, not years.

## "It lasts" only matters if someone can read it later

Durability is half the question. The other half: will a readable sample still be *worth* reading in 50 years? The trend line says the value goes up, not down.

On August 6, 2026, a Johns Hopkins-led team published the first complete **diploid** human genome benchmark in *Cell* — both parental copies, telomere to telomere, with 701 million base pairs that no previous reference could read (via search index — verify before posting). That's the third time in 23 years that "the genome is finished" got more finished: 2003's draft missed 8%, 2022's T2T closed the gaps for one composite copy, and 2026 finally did both copies. Every one of those upgrades made *existing, well-stored samples* more informative without anyone touching them.

A sample stored this year will be read by instruments that don't exist yet. The only requirement is that the molecule survives the wait, which, as the moa and the mammoths establish, is a solved problem when the storage is right.

## FAQ

**How long does DNA last after death?**
In an exposed body, usable DNA can degrade within years to decades. Protected in bone or teeth at cool temperatures, it follows the ~521-year half-life curve and remains recoverable for thousands of years.

**Can DNA really survive a million years?**
Yes, under exceptional conditions. Mammoth teeth over a million years old have yielded sequenced genomes, and 2-million-year-old environmental DNA was recovered from frozen Greenland sediment.

**How long does DNA last at room temperature?**
Unprotected: poorly, since humidity and oxygen do constant damage. Dried and sealed under inert atmosphere: accelerated-aging studies project centuries or more, with measured degradation too low to quantify.

**Can dinosaur DNA ever be recovered?**
No. Even at ideal cold temperatures, the last DNA bonds break after roughly 6.8 million years. Dinosaurs are 66+ million years out of range.

**Is a freezer the best way to store DNA at home?**
No. Home freezers cycle, fail, and cause freeze-thaw damage. Professional long-horizon storage uses dried, sealed, ambient-temperature archival systems with accreditation and monitoring.

**Will a DNA sample stored today still be useful in 50 years?**
More useful, most likely. Sequencing has improved through three "finished genome" milestones since 2003; each upgrade extracted more from well-stored samples that hadn't changed at all.
