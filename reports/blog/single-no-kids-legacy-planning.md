# Single, No Kids, and Planning Anyway: A Checklist That Isn't About Inheritance

**If you are single and have no children, the most urgent planning question is not who gets your money. It is who is legally allowed to speak for you if you cannot speak for yourself. Most US states have a default surrogate hierarchy — usually spouse or partner, then adult child, then parent, then sibling — and in 38 states the highest-ranked person available and willing becomes your decision-maker automatically. If nobody on that list exists, the fallback is not a friend. It is a court-appointed guardian. Three documents, signed in an afternoon, take that decision away from a judge and give it to a person you chose.**

This is the article we would want a friend to read, and it is not a product pitch. Most of what follows costs nothing but an appointment. We are a DNA preservation company; that is one small line near the end, and you can stop reading before it.

## The default exists. It just runs out.

When a patient cannot make decisions and has not named anyone, clinicians do not simply guess. Most states authorise **default surrogate decision-makers**, and the typical priority runs spouse or domestic partner, then adult child, then parent, then sibling, then more distant relatives, and in a growing number of statutes a close friend or someone with a "meaningful relationship" ([ABA Commission on Law and Aging, *BIFOCAL*](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_aging/publications/bifocal/vol44/bifocal-vol-44-issue3/recent-updates-to-default-surrogate-statutes/); [Merck Manual](https://www.merckmanuals.com/home/special-subjects/legal-and-ethical-issues/default-surrogate-decision-making)). In **38 states** the statute directs that the highest-ranked person available and willing becomes the surrogate.

Read that hierarchy again as a single person without children. The first two rungs do not exist. If your parents have died and you have no siblings — or you have siblings you would not want deciding — the ladder runs out.

**What happens then has a name.** Clinicians call patients with no available surrogate "unbefriended," and they are far more likely to end up with a **court-appointed guardian or conservator**. Where it is unclear who should decide, hospitals escalate to ethics committees or legal counsel. The process is slow, it is not designed around your preferences, and the person who ends up deciding may be a professional guardian who has never met you.

This is not a rare edge case. In AARP research on solo agers, **39% said they fear a court appointing someone to make decisions for them, against 27% of other older adults** — a fear that is, unusually, well calibrated to the actual risk. *(AARP research as reported; figures worth confirming against the primary report before citing them elsewhere.)*

## Three documents, and what each one actually stops

None of these is about who inherits. They are about the years before that.

### 1. Healthcare proxy (also called a medical power of attorney)

Names the person allowed to make medical decisions when you cannot. **This is the one that replaces the court.** Without it the default hierarchy applies, and when the hierarchy is empty, a judge fills the gap.

Pair it with a **HIPAA authorisation**, which is a separate and often-forgotten piece of paper: your proxy cannot make decisions about information nobody will release to them.

### 2. Durable power of attorney for finances

Names who can pay your mortgage, manage accounts and deal with insurers if you are incapacitated. "Durable" is the word that matters — it means the authority survives your incapacity, which is precisely when you need it. A non-durable POA stops working at the moment it becomes useful.

### 3. A will with a named executor — and a backup

For a single person with no children, **the executor choice is often harder than the beneficiary choice.** You are asking someone to do months of administrative work. Name a second choice. Consider whether a bank's trust department or a professional fiduciary is more realistic than a friend who will be sixty-eight when it happens.

**Add, if there is nobody obvious:** many states let you nominate your own guardian in advance, so that if a guardianship ever is opened the court starts from your preference rather than a list. It is worth asking about by name.

## The genuinely hard part: there may be no obvious person

The paperwork is the easy half. The hard half is that these documents require you to write down a name, and a lot of people stall there indefinitely.

A few things that help:

- **Separate the roles.** The person you trust with medical judgement does not have to be the person who is good with money, and neither has to be your executor. Splitting them widens the pool considerably.
- **Proximity beats affection for the medical role.** A decision-maker four time zones away is a worse choice than a sensible friend twenty minutes from your hospital.
- **Ask directly, and tell them where the documents are.** An unopened envelope in a drawer is not a plan. The most common failure of any personal archive is not decay — it is that the one person who knew it existed is not reachable.
- **Professionals exist for this.** Fiduciaries, trust companies and some elder-law practices serve exactly this role for a fee. Paying someone is not a failure of your social life; it is the same logic as hiring an accountant.
- **Revisit every few years.** People move, fall out, get ill, and die. A proxy named at 38 may be unreachable at 61.

## How common this actually is

Solo aging is a large and growing category rather than a footnote — roughly **22 million** US adults were described as solo agers in 2021, and the number has risen since as childlessness and later-life divorce have both increased *(reported figure; treat the exact number as approximate)*. NPR covered the demographic and its policy implications in **July 2026**, noting that changes designed to help solo agers would help many other people too.

The connection-level numbers are the ones that should prompt action: AARP research found **55% of solo agers had no one with whom to discuss medical decisions**, and **nearly 39% had nobody to call if they were sick and confined to bed** *(as reported; verify against the primary report)*.

If you are 35 and reading this thinking it is a problem for later — the documents above are the cheapest they will ever be to set up, and the failure mode they prevent is an accident, not old age.

Everything above concerns decisions and assets. There is a separate category that no legal instrument covers: **the record of who you were**.

## FAQ

### Who makes medical decisions for me if I'm single with no children?
If you have not named a healthcare proxy, most states apply a default surrogate hierarchy — typically spouse or partner, adult child, parent, sibling, then more distant relatives or in some states a close friend. If nobody on that list is available and willing, the likely outcome is a court-appointed guardian.

### What is an "unbefriended" patient?
Clinical and legal shorthand for someone who lacks decision-making capacity, has no advance directive, and has no available surrogate. These patients are substantially more likely to have a guardian or conservator appointed by a court.

### What's the difference between a healthcare proxy and a living will?
A healthcare proxy names a *person* to decide. A living will (advance directive) records your *instructions* about specific treatments. They do different jobs and most people should have both, because no document anticipates every situation, but a person can.

### Can I name a friend instead of a relative?
Generally yes, if you name them in a healthcare proxy — that is the point of the document. The default statutes are a fallback for when you have named nobody, and only a growing minority of them reach friends at all. Naming removes the guesswork.

### Who should be my executor if I have no close family?
Split the roles, name a backup, and consider a professional fiduciary or a bank trust department. Executorship is months of administrative work, so the realistic question is not who loves you most but who will actually be able and willing to do it.

### Is this legal advice?
No. Surrogate statutes, POA requirements and guardianship nomination rules vary substantially by state, and this is a general explainer. An estate or elder-law attorney in your state can do all three documents in one appointment.
