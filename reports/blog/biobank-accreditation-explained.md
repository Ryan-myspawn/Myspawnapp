# Biobank Accreditation Explained: What ISO 20387, CAP and "FDA-Registered" Each Actually Certify

**Biobank accreditation means an independent body has assessed a facility's competence to store biological material — its quality system, staff, equipment, storage conditions and record-keeping. The three credentials you will see quoted are ISO 20387 (an international biobanking standard published in 2018), CAP's Biorepository Accreditation Program (launched 2012 by the College of American Pathologists), and "FDA-registered," which is the weakest of the three and the most misunderstood: FDA registration means a facility has told the FDA it exists. It is explicitly not FDA approval or endorsement. And none of the three tells you the one thing you probably want to know — how long a specific company will keep your sample, and what happens to it if that company fails.**

We quote two of these credentials in our own marketing, which is exactly why this article explains what they do *not* cover as carefully as what they do. If that makes our own claims sound narrower, good — narrower is accurate.

## The three credentials, one at a time

### ISO 20387 — the international biobanking standard

ISO 20387:2018 is the general requirements standard for biobanking, and its stated objective is to promote confidence in biobanking. It applies to organisations banking biological material from humans, animals, fungi, plants and microorganisms.

An accreditation assessment against it is genuinely broad. Independent assessors examine the **quality management system, personnel qualifications and competence, material handling, the storage environment and equipment, and reporting** ([ANAB](https://anab.ansi.org/accreditation/iso-20387-biobanking-accreditation/); [A2LA](https://a2la.org/accreditation/iso-20387-biobanking/)). It covers the whole specimen lifecycle — acquisition, processing, storage, distribution and **disposal** — and includes risk-management requirements. The standard also requires biobanks to produce objective evidence that the material they hold is of comparable quality, through mechanisms such as proficiency testing and certified reference materials.

**What it is:** a serious, audited competence standard covering how a facility operates.
**What it is not:** a statement about any individual customer's contract.

### CAP Biorepository Accreditation Program

The College of American Pathologists launched its Biorepository Accreditation Program in **2012**, six years before ISO 20387 was published. It is the other main pathway, and the two are frequently held together rather than one replacing the other — a peer-reviewed comparison of the two options was published in *Archives of Pathology & Laboratory Medicine* in 2025 ([abstract](https://meridian.allenpress.com/aplm/article/149/6/497/503638/Two-Accreditation-Options-for-Biorepositories)).

CAP accreditation is inspection-based and rooted in laboratory-quality practice. For a US consumer, seeing CAP alongside ISO 20387 is meaningful: it means two different bodies, with different traditions, have both looked at the facility.

### "FDA-registered" — read this part carefully

This is the credential most likely to be misread, including in good faith.

**FDA registration is not FDA approval.** The FDA's position is explicit: assignment of a registration number means only that the facility is registered, and it **does not convey FDA approval or endorsement of the facility or its products**. FDA does not approve establishments at all. A registered facility receives a number the agency uses to identify the firm for inspections, import screening and other regulatory activity.

The clean way to hold the distinction: **registration applies to establishments; approval, clearance or authorisation applies to products.** The FDA issues warning letters over false "FDA-registered" and "FDA-approved" claims, which tells you how routinely the phrase is stretched.

So "FDA-registered" is a real fact and a low bar. It tells you a facility is on a list and is inspectable. It tells you nothing about quality, and nobody at the FDA has evaluated the storage of your sample.

### Two others you will see, and what they are

- **ISO 9001** is a general quality-management standard. It is not biobanking-specific. It says an organisation runs a documented, auditable quality system — useful context, not a storage credential.
- **CLIA** certifies laboratories performing diagnostic testing on human specimens. It is about *testing*, not *storage*. A company that tests and a company that stores need different things, and CLIA on a storage pitch is a category error worth noticing.

## The question accreditation does not answer

Here is the gap, and it is the reason we wrote this.

Accreditation certifies **the facility's competence**. It does not publish **the terms of your particular arrangement.** Those are two different questions, and only one of them is on the certificate.

A perfectly accredited biorepository can hold a sample under a contract that permits disposal after a set number of years, or on missed payment, or on the winding-up of the company that placed it there. ISO 20387 requires documented processes for disposal; it does not require that a given customer's retention period be long, generous, or even disclosed on a website. The standard makes the *process* auditable. The *policy* is commercial.

This is not hypothetical or unusual. When we looked across the adjacent industry recently — companies holding biological material for consumers — the answer to "how long do you keep it, and what happens if you shut down" generally did not appear on any public page. It resolved into a terms-of-service document. A policy you can only reach inside a terms document was written for a court, not for you.

So the accreditation logos are necessary and not sufficient. They answer "is this facility competent?" You still have to ask "what did I actually agree to?" separately, and of a different part of the company.

## How to check any of this yourself

1. **Ask which body issued the accreditation**, not just which standard. ISO 20387 is assessed by accreditation bodies such as ANAB or A2LA; a claim of "ISO 20387 compliant" without a named accrediting body is a different and weaker claim than "accredited."
2. **Ask whether the accreditation covers the specific site** holding your material. Multi-site organisations do not automatically carry one site's accreditation everywhere.
3. **Treat "FDA-registered" as a fact, not a rating.** If a company leans on it heavily, that is informative.
4. **Ask the retention question separately and in writing:** how long, on what terms, what happens on non-payment, and what happens on insolvency or acquisition.
5. **Time how long that takes you to find.** If the answer only exists inside a terms document, that is a design choice someone made.

## FAQ

### Is "FDA-registered" the same as FDA-approved?
No, and the difference is large. Registration means a facility has notified the FDA that it exists and is subject to inspection. It conveys no approval or endorsement of the facility or its products. Approval, clearance and authorisation apply to products, not establishments.

### Which is better, ISO 20387 or CAP accreditation?
Neither supersedes the other. CAP's biorepository program launched in 2012 and ISO 20387 was published in 2018, and they are complementary pathways assessed by different bodies. Holding both is stronger than holding either, because two independent assessors have looked.

### Does accreditation mean my sample is guaranteed for decades?
No. Accreditation assesses the facility's competence and processes, including how material is handled, stored and eventually disposed of. The duration of *your* storage and what happens to it on closure, acquisition or non-payment are set by your contract, not by the standard.

### Is CLIA certification relevant to DNA storage?
Not directly. CLIA covers laboratories performing diagnostic testing on human specimens. Storage and testing are different activities, and CLIA cited as a storage credential is a category error.

### What does "ISO 20387 compliant" mean if there is no accrediting body named?
It means the company says it follows the standard. Accreditation means an independent body assessed it and issued a decision. The two are not equivalent, and the wording usually tells you which one is being claimed.

### What is the single best question to ask a storage company?
"How long do you keep it, and what happens to it if you shut down?" Then notice how long the answer takes to find and where it lives.
