# What Happens to Prepaid Services If the Company Shuts Down?

*Published: November 5, 2026 · Lane (d) trust and custody · myspawn.me/blog*

If a company you prepaid goes out of business, you usually become an unsecured creditor. That means you join a queue behind lenders, landlords and tax authorities, and you are paid last, if at all. A narrow exception in federal bankruptcy law gives consumers a limited priority for deposits on goods and services they never received, capped at a few thousand dollars. A handful of industries are forced by state law to hold prepayments in escrow or trust. Most industries are not. Knowing which category you are in is the whole difference between a claim and a refund.

This article is about the money. A separate question, what happens to the biological sample or the data itself, is answered in [What Happens to Your DNA When a Company Shuts Down?](/blog/what-happens-dna-company-shuts-down). **They are different questions with different answers, and conflating them is how people end up surprised twice.**

## Prepayment makes you a lender, not a customer

The moment you pay in advance for something you have not received, you have extended credit. You are owed a service. The company owes you performance rather than money, but in an insolvency that obligation is converted into a dollar claim and ranked with every other claim against the estate.

Ranking is the thing to understand. Secured creditors, those holding collateral, are paid first. Then certain priority claims. Then general unsecured creditors, which is where most consumers sit. **In many liquidations, general unsecured creditors recover a small fraction of what they are owed, and sometimes nothing.**

This is not a scandal. It is the design. The system exists to divide a shortfall, and somebody has to be at the back.

## The consumer priority, and how narrow it is

US bankruptcy law does carve out a limited consumer protection. Section 507(a)(7) of the Bankruptcy Code gives priority to individual claims arising from deposits made for the purchase, lease or rental of property, or the purchase of services, for personal, family or household use ([BDO summary](https://www.bdo.com/insights/industries/retail-consumer-products/treatment-of-customer-deposits-after-bankruptcy); [Cozen O'Connor analysis](https://www.cozen.com/news-resources/publications/2021/what-constitutes-a-consumer-deposit-for-priority-treatment-under-the-bankruptcy-code)).

Three limits matter more than the existence of the rule.

**It is capped.** Claims notices in a 2025 retail bankruptcy state the figure as **$3,800 for cases filed on or after 1 April 2025**, with **$3,350** applying to cases filed earlier ([Pirch claims information](https://pirchbankruptcy.dinsmore.com/Home/ClaimPage)). *Background we could not verify in these sources: the Code adjusts its dollar figures on a three-year cycle, which would place the next revision in 2028. Treat the cycle as context, not as a cited fact.*

**It applies only to what was not delivered.** The priority covers the undelivered benefit. Money spent on services you already received is not a deposit awaiting performance.

**It may not cover the instrument you are holding.** A Delaware bankruptcy court held that payment for gift cards does not meet the statutory definition of a deposit, which removed gift card holders from priority treatment ([Seyfarth legal update](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/delaware-bankruptcy-court-rules-gift-cards-not-eligible-for-priority-treatment.html)). *Label: this is one court's decision reported by a law firm, not a nationwide rule.*

So the protection is real, and it is measured in thousands rather than in whatever you actually prepaid.

## Some industries must ring-fence your money. Most do not.

The more useful question is not what bankruptcy law does afterwards. It is whether your money was ever allowed to be spent in the first place.

Several states require specific industries to place prepayments into escrow or trust, which keeps the money legally separate from the company's own funds.

- **Health clubs.** New York requires that all moneys received for health club services before the club is fully operating be placed in escrow at a bank or trust company doing business in the state, with an alternative showing of financial responsibility available to the operator ([NY General Business Law article 30, section 622](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/GBS/622)). New Hampshire requires registration with the attorney general and escrow of fees collected before a club begins operating ([NH RSA 358-I](https://www.gencourt.state.nh.us/rsa/html/XXXI/358-I/358-I-mrg.htm)).
- **Funeral preneed.** Missouri requires sellers to place the full amount of payments on a trust-funded preneed contract into the trust within sixty days of receipt, with limited early release ([Missouri preneed legislation](https://house.mo.gov/billtracking/bills091/bills/SB001.htm)). Indiana regulates prepaid services and merchandise through trusts and escrows, permitting withdrawal as value is delivered ([Indiana Code 30-2-13](https://codes.findlaw.com/in/title-30-trusts-and-fiduciaries/in-code-sect-30-2-13-12-1/)). Georgia requires annual financial reporting on preneed trust and escrow accounts to the Secretary of State ([Georgia Code 10-14-7.1](https://codes.findlaw.com/ga/title-10-commerce-and-trade/ga-code-sect-10-14-7-1/)).

*Labelling, because it matters here: these are state statutes and we read them through a search index rather than at a state's own site. Several results were historical versions or bills rather than enacted current law. Check the current code before relying on any one of them.*

**The pattern is what to take away, not the citations.** Legislatures ring-fence prepayments in industries where consumers were visibly harmed: gyms that closed after selling lifetime memberships, funeral sellers holding money for decades. **DNA storage is in none of those categories in any state we could find.** No statute compels a genetic storage company to escrow your money.

## The question to ask any company holding something of yours

Four questions, in order of how much they tell you.

1. **How far ahead have I paid?** This is the size of your exposure, and it is the only number you fully control.
2. **Is the money held separately, and by whom?** Ask for the name of the institution. "Held separately" with no name is a sentence, not an arrangement.
3. **Who physically holds the thing itself, and are they the same company?** If a third party holds your property under its own contract, that party's obligations do not automatically vanish with the company that sold you the service. Our article on [chain of custody](/blog/chain-of-custody-explained) covers why the paperwork answering this matters more than the assurance.
4. **What is the documented route out?** Not whether one exists in principle. What it is called, who signs it, and how long it takes.

If a company cannot answer question four in writing, that is information. [When a biobank loses its accreditation](/blog/biobank-loses-accreditation) and [when a fertility clinic closes](/blog/fertility-clinic-closes-embryos) are the two cases where this question gets asked too late, every time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I get my money back if a company I prepaid goes bankrupt?
Usually not in full. You become a creditor with a claim against the estate rather than a customer with a refund. A limited consumer priority may apply to the part of the service you never received, capped at a few thousand dollars, and general unsecured creditors are paid last.

### Is a prepaid service the same as a gift card in bankruptcy?
Not necessarily. A Delaware bankruptcy court held that payment for gift cards did not meet the statutory definition of a deposit, which would exclude gift card holders from priority treatment. That is one court's decision rather than a nationwide rule, and the instrument you hold can change the answer.

### Should I pay annually or buy a multi-year plan?
A multi-year prepayment is a larger unsecured exposure to a company's survival. Discounts for prepaying are real, and so is the trade. The question is whether the discount compensates you for being further forward in a queue you cannot see.

### Does my credit card protect me?
Chargeback rights exist but are time-limited, and the windows are generally measured in months rather than years. A prepayment made long before a company fails is often outside them. Treat a chargeback as a short-term remedy, not as insurance.

### Is DNA storage covered by any escrow law?
We could not find a state that requires genetic storage companies to escrow prepayments the way health clubs and funeral preneed sellers are required to. If your provider holds money in trust, it is doing so by choice, and you should ask where.

### What should I ask before prepaying anything?
How far ahead you are paying, whether the money is held separately and by whom, who physically holds the thing itself, and what the documented route out is. The fourth question is the one that gets avoided.

## What this comes down to

Prepaying is lending. Most of the time that is fine, because most companies do not fail. The point is not to be afraid of it. **The point is to know the size of the loan you are making, and to prefer the companies that can tell you what happens if they are not there.**
