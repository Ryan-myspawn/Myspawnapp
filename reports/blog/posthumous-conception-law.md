The direct answer: **paperwork signed while you're alive decides almost everything, and geography decides the rest.** In the US, whether a stored reproductive sample can be used after death — and whether a child conceived that way can inherit — turns on three things: written consent you gave in advance, the state you die in, and increasingly, a clock: model law recognizes parentage only if the embryo is in utero within 36 months, or the child born within 45 months, of the death. There is no single federal rule. The Supreme Court confirmed exactly that in a 9-0 decision about twins conceived eighteen months after their father died.

*(This article is general information, not legal advice; posthumous-reproduction law varies by state and changes.)*

## The case that defined the field

Robert Capato was diagnosed with esophageal cancer shortly after marrying Karen in 1999 and banked sperm before chemotherapy — the medically responsible move. He died in Florida in March 2002. In September 2003, eighteen months later, Karen gave birth to twins conceived by IVF from the banked sample and applied for Social Security survivor benefits for them.

In May 2012 the Supreme Court ruled unanimously, in *Astrue v. Capato*, that the twins were not entitled to benefits — not because they weren't his children in any human sense, but because the statute defers to **state intestacy law**, and Florida's didn't recognize children conceived after a parent's death ([decision summary](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2012/5/supreme-court-decides-astrue-v-capato); [case page](https://law.cornell.edu/supct/cert/11-159); [Washington Post coverage](https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/court-twins-conceived-posthumously-cant-receive-benefits/2012/05/21/gIQArQoagU_story.html)).

Same twins, different state — possibly a different answer. That's the field in one sentence.

## The model law trying to fix it

The Uniform Parentage Act (2017 revision) is the template states increasingly draw on ([ABA overview](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/real_property_trust_estate/resources/journal/2024-winter/posthumous-pregnancy-uniform-law/)). Its core rules:

- **Consent is the hinge.** A deceased person is a legal parent of a posthumously conceived child only with written consent to posthumous reproduction — or intent proven by clear and convincing evidence.
- **The clock.** Parentage generally requires the embryo in utero within **36 months** of death, or birth within **45 months** — limits originating in the 2008 Uniform Probate Code so estates can actually close.
- **Adoption varies further** — and states adopt, modify, or ignore these provisions unevenly, which is why the Capato problem persists ([UNLV law review analysis](https://scholars.law.unlv.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1722&context=nlj)).

## What actually controls your stored samples

Practically, control flows through four documents, in roughly this order:

1. **The storage facility's consent form.** Fertility clinics and sperm banks resolved this the hard way: what happens to samples at death is now decided by forms signed at deposit — use by a named partner, destruction, or donation. If you stored anything years ago, you may not remember what you signed. That form is currently the law of your sample.
2. **Explicit posthumous-use consent.** Under UPA-style law, the difference between "my widow can use this" and "the sample is destroyed" is a signature. Silence is not consent — and in some states, silence means no.
3. **Your will and estate documents** — which should name the samples, the authorized person, and the intent, in language your attorney matches to your state.
4. **State statute defaults** — the fallback nobody should rely on, per Capato.

The pattern is the same one we documented for [digital assets](/blog/digital-legacy-what-happens): the instrument you filled out in advance beats the will, and the will beats the default. **Custody is a document, not a feeling.**

## The checklist, if you have (or plan) stored samples

- Find and reread your storage consent form; request a copy if you can't.
- If you intend posthumous use to be possible, execute **explicit written consent** in the form your state recognizes — ask a family/estate attorney, not the clinic's front desk.
- If you intend the opposite, say that in writing too; ambiguity is litigation.
- Name the samples in your estate documents; note the 36/45-month reality for anything reproductive ([fertility-preservation cost and logistics context here](/blog/sperm-freezing-cost)).

## FAQ

**Can sperm be used after death in the US?**
Sometimes — it depends on written consent, the storage facility's forms, and state law. Without explicit consent, many states and clinics will not permit use.

**Do posthumously conceived children inherit?**
Only if state law recognizes them — often requiring prior written consent and conception/birth within time limits (commonly 36 months to implantation or 45 months to birth under model law). *Astrue v. Capato* (2012) confirmed benefits follow state intestacy rules.

**What is the 36-month rule?**
Under the Uniform Parentage Act (2017), parentage for a posthumously conceived child generally requires the embryo be in utero within 36 months of the death (or birth within 45 months), so estates can close.

**Does a will control my stored sperm or eggs?**
Partially. The storage facility's signed consent form usually controls first; a will should still name the samples and your intent, in state-appropriate language.

**Does any of this apply to stored DNA records like hair or nails?**
The parentage clocks don't — a DNA record is information, not reproductive tissue. The custody questions (who can claim it, what directive controls it) apply fully and should be answered in writing.

**Is posthumous conception legal everywhere in the US?**
No state bans it outright, but recognition of parentage and inheritance varies widely; several states follow UPA-style consent and time rules, others have no clear statute.
