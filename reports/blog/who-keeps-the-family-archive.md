# Who Ends Up With the Family Archive, and Why Nobody Actually Decides

**Short answer: in most families, nobody decides. The person who ends up with the boxes of photographs, the letters and the loose negatives is whoever happened to care, usually at the worst possible moment, during a house clearance after a death. There is no legal default that assigns the family archive to a particular child. There is, however, a legal tool built for exactly this problem, it takes about twenty minutes, and most people have never heard of it. There is also a second half of the archive it does not reach at all.**

This covers who gets the family photographs and papers when someone dies, the separate-writing mechanism that assigns them without rewriting a will, and why the digital half is governed by completely different law.

## The short answer: the keeper self-selects

In most families there is one person interested enough in the old material to keep a box or two, and that person becomes the family curator by default. Nobody appoints them. Often nobody even discusses it.

A family without a de facto curator will usually not think to plan for the archive at all, so the question only gets asked when the house has to be emptied and someone has to decide, that week, what goes in a car and what goes in a skip.

The popular version of this story, that the youngest sibling inherits the family history, is not supported by anything. There is no birth-order rule in US inheritance law and no evidence of a birth-order pattern in who keeps archives. The role goes to whoever cares, not to whoever was born last.

## The statistic everyone quotes, and the date nobody mentions

If you read around this subject you will meet one number repeatedly: **personal possessions are five times more likely than money to be the greatest source of family conflict during a legacy transfer.**

It is a genuine finding and it is worth knowing. It is also worth knowing where it comes from, because almost nobody says. It is from the **Allianz American Legacies Study, conducted by Age Wave with Allianz Life Insurance Company of North America and Harris Interactive, surveying 2,627 boomers and elders. It was published in 2005.**

**Epistemic label, stated because this site does that: it is an industry-commissioned survey, not peer-reviewed research, and it is more than twenty years old.** It is quoted constantly on estate-planning blogs with no date attached, which makes it read like a current figure. Treat it as a well-known older survey that matches what probate attorneys report anecdotally, not as a 2026 measurement.

The underlying observation holds up on its own logic anyway. Money is divisible; a photograph album is not. Two siblings can split a bank account without either losing anything. They cannot split the only print of their grandmother at nineteen.

## Some societies did have a rule, and it was the youngest

**Ultimogeniture** is an inheritance system in which the youngest child inherits rather than the eldest. Among the **Khasi people of Meghalaya in India**, matrilineal ultimogeniture is traditional: inheritance passes to the youngest daughter, who is also expected to care for aging parents and unmarried siblings. A form of it existed historically in parts of England under the name Borough English.

**Label: anthropological and historical background, not US law.** The point is narrow. Systems that thought about this problem attached the archive to a person by rule, and attached a duty of care alongside it. Modern US practice attaches it to nobody and expects the family to work it out.

## The legal tool built for this, which almost nobody uses

Here is the part that is actually actionable.

Most US states have adopted a provision derived from the **Uniform Probate Code, Section 2-513, "Separate writing identifying devise of certain types of tangible personal property."** You can see it enacted in state code as, for example, Hawaii 560:2-513, Massachusetts 190B 2-513, New Mexico 45-2-513 and Idaho 15-2-513.

In plain terms: **your will can point to a separate handwritten or typed list that says who gets which objects.** That list is legally effective even though it is not part of the will itself.

### What the list has to do

Per the statute's own language:

- It disposes of **tangible personal property**, and explicitly **not money**.
- It must be **signed by the person making the will**.
- It must **describe the items and the recipients with reasonable certainty**.
- It may be written **before or after the will is executed**.
- It **may be altered after it is prepared**.
- It can only cover property **not otherwise specifically disposed of by the will**.

### Why this beats putting the list in the will

The last two points are the whole value. A will is a formal document; changing it means executing a new one or a codicil, usually with an attorney and witnesses. **A separate writing can be updated with a pen.** You can add a line when something changes and sign it.

For a family archive, that flexibility is not a convenience, it is the difference between a document that stays accurate and one that describes a house you moved out of in 2011.

**Practical note: this is a state-law question.** Adoption and details vary, including whether a state recognizes the mechanism at all and how strictly "reasonable certainty" is read. Check your own state or ask an attorney rather than assuming the model code applies where you live.

## The half of the archive this does not reach

Now the part that catches people, and it is the reason a family can do everything right and still lose half the material.

**A personal property memorandum covers tangible personal property. Most of a modern family archive is not tangible.**

### The device and the data are different things

Nearly every US state has enacted the **Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA)**, and the distinction it draws is the one that matters here. **The act governs the data, not the device.** A phone or a laptop is ordinary tangible property that passes through normal probate. **The photographs on it, and the ones in the cloud account it syncs to, are digital assets under a separate legal regime.**

So a line in your separate writing reading "my photographs to my daughter" reaches the shoebox in the wardrobe. It does not, on its own, reach the forty thousand images in the cloud account.

### Permission has to be explicit

RUFADAA's default runs the opposite way to most people's assumption. **Unless access is explicitly granted in the will or trust, the presumption is that the fiduciary does not have it.** Silence is not permission. This is also why the platform-level tools matter: we covered what each major platform's legacy setting can and cannot do in [What Happens to Your Accounts When You Die](/blog/platform-legacy-settings-explained), and the short version is that they vary enormously and none of them is a substitute for saying so in your documents.

## How to actually decide, before it gets decided for you

A workable sequence, in rough order of how much it costs you:

1. **Name the keeper out loud**, in a conversation before a document. The most common failure is that four siblings each assume one of the others has it in hand.
2. **Separate "who stores it" from "who gets copies."** Most archive conflicts are about access, not custody, and scanning resolves a surprising number before they start.
3. **Write the separate list.** Tangible items, signed, described with reasonable certainty. Keep it with the will.
4. **Write a second instruction for the digital half**, and make the authorization explicit rather than implied. This is the step almost everyone skips.
5. **Set the platform-level settings too**, because a document your executor holds and a switch inside the account are different mechanisms and you want both.
6. **Say what it is, not just where it is.** An unlabeled box of photographs of people nobody can name is not an archive, it is a storage problem. Naming who is in the picture is the cheapest preservation work available and the only part nobody can reconstruct later. On what is worth passing on at all, see [What to Leave Your Grandchildren](/blog/what-to-leave-grandchildren).

## Frequently asked questions

### Who legally gets family photos when a parent dies?

Whoever the will says. If the will is silent, family photographs are simply part of the residuary estate and are divided like any other tangible personal property, which in practice means the family negotiates or the executor decides. **There is no separate legal category for sentimental items and no birth-order rule.**

### Can I leave a list of who gets what without rewriting my will?

In most US states, yes. A provision based on Uniform Probate Code Section 2-513 lets your will refer to a separate signed writing that disposes of tangible personal property. It can be written before or after the will and altered later. **It cannot dispose of money, and it cannot cover anything the will already gives to someone specifically.** Whether your state has adopted it, and how it reads there, is worth checking.

### Does a personal property memorandum cover digital photos?

Generally no. Digital photographs and cloud accounts are digital assets governed by RUFADAA in nearly every state, which treats the data separately from the device it sits on. **You need explicit authorization for digital assets, because the default presumption is that a fiduciary does not have access.**

### How do you divide family photos between siblings fairly?

Stop treating it as a division problem. Scan the collection so every sibling can hold a copy, then decide separately who stores the originals. Where objects genuinely cannot be duplicated, structured selection, taking turns in a set order that reverses each round, is what estate attorneys most often recommend.

### Is the youngest child responsible for the family history?

No. That belief appears to come from a mix of cultural tradition and the practical fact that the youngest is often the last living sibling, but **it has no basis in US inheritance law.** Some societies did formalize youngest-child inheritance, but that is historical and cultural, not legal precedent here.
