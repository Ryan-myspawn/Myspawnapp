# What a Chain of Custody Actually Proves

**Direct answer:** A chain of custody is a **record**, not a guarantee. It is a chronological log of who had control of a sample, when, and what they did with it, kept so that anyone can later reconstruct the sample's whole handling history. It does not stop a sample being mishandled. It makes mishandling **findable**, and it makes an unbroken history **demonstrable**. Those are different things from "your sample is safe", and companies that use the phrase as a reassurance, including us, should be asked which one they mean.

## The definition, and the word doing the work

The clearest formal wording comes from the biobanking standard. **A draft revision of ISO 20387, circulated as ISO/DIS 20387:2025, defines chain of custody as a chronological record that tracks control of and responsibility for biological material and associated data at each process step, and adds that the chain of custody "can help assess the integrity" of that material and data.**

**Two labels before you lean on that.** First, **that is a DRAFT**, circulated for comment with voting scheduled to close on 21 January 2026, so the final published wording may differ. **Second, we could not confirm the wording used in the published 2018 edition**, which is the version a facility accredited today is most likely working to. We are telling you what we could verify and what we could not.

Now look at the verb. **"Can help assess."** Not "ensures". Not "protects". The standard's own language treats the chain of custody as **evidence about integrity rather than integrity itself**, and that distinction is the entire subject of this article.

## What a custody log actually contains

In forensic practice, the record chronologically documents the procurement, custody, control, transfer, analysis, disposition, management and storage of an item of evidence. The purpose is to show that the item was handled only by approved people and was not left accessible for tampering before analysis.

A working laboratory custody log typically captures, for each event:

- **Which sample.** A unique identifier that travels with the material rather than with its container.
- **Who had it.** The named custodian, not the department.
- **What they did.** Received, moved, aliquoted, analysed, returned, destroyed.
- **When, to the minute.**
- **Where it went.** The physical location, not just the building.
- **Under what procedure.** The SOP or protocol version in force at the time.
- **Anything that went wrong.** Deviations, excursions, environmental conditions outside the specified range.
- **An audit trail**, so later edits to the record are themselves visible.

That last line is the one people skip, and it is load-bearing. **A log that can be quietly edited afterwards is a story, not a record.**

### Sample tracking is not chain of custody, and the difference costs money

Most laboratories have **sample tracking**: a system that can tell you where a given sample is right now. That is inventory. **A chain of custody is accountability for every handling, storage and transfer event, including who performed the action and when it was recorded.**

Put plainly: tracking answers "where is it?" Custody answers "who has had it, in what order, and what did each of them do?" **A facility can have excellent tracking and no meaningful custody record, and from the outside the two look identical on a marketing page.** If you are comparing providers, this is the single most useful question you can ask, and it is also the one that is least often answered precisely. Our piece on [what it means when a genetic test is "validated"](/blog/genetic-test-validated-meaning) makes the same move in a different domain: ask which specific claim the reassuring word is standing in for.

## What it proves, and what it does not

**What it proves.** That the sample's handling history can be reconstructed. That every transfer has a named person attached. That a gap, if one exists, has a date and a location. That nobody can later claim the sample was somewhere it was not without contradicting a contemporaneous record.

**What it does not prove.** That the sample is intact. That storage conditions were correct. That the material is still usable. **A perfect chain of custody can document, in immaculate detail, the complete ruin of a sample.** It would tell you exactly who had it when the freezer failed, which is genuinely valuable and is not the same as the sample surviving.

**It also does not prove that the record is true.** It proves that a record exists and that it is internally consistent and auditable. The people writing it are the people being recorded. **That is why the audit trail and independent assessment matter: the chain of custody is checked by somebody else as part of accreditation, and the value of the chain is only as good as the honesty of that assessment.** Our article on [what happens when a biobank loses accreditation](/blog/biobank-loses-accreditation) covers what that outside check is and where its status is published.

## Four questions to ask any custodian

1. **"Is your chain of custody a record of events, or a tracking system that shows current location?"** If the answer conflates them, you have learned something.
2. **"Is the custody record auditable? Can an entry be edited without the edit being visible?"**
3. **"Does the record capture deviations and environmental excursions, or only successful transfers?"** A log of only the good days is a brochure.
4. **"Who outside your company reviews it, and on what cycle?"** This is the accreditation question, and the answer should be a named body with a published register entry.

None of those requires technical knowledge to ask, and all four are hard to answer vaguely without the vagueness showing.

## FAQ

**Is a chain of custody a legal requirement for DNA storage?**
Not generally for private, non-forensic storage. It is a requirement in forensic and legal contexts, where evidence must be admissible, and it is addressed in biobanking standards as part of demonstrating traceability. **A consumer storage provider may keep one as good practice rather than because a statute compels it, so ask rather than assume.**

**What breaks a chain of custody?**
Any gap where the record cannot say who had control. A transfer with no receiving signature, a period with no documented custodian, an edited entry with no audit trail. **A break does not automatically mean the sample is compromised. It means you can no longer demonstrate that it was not.**

**Does a chain of custody mean my sample is safe?**
No, and this is the most common misreading. It is a record of handling, not a guarantee of condition. The relevant standard's own draft language says it "can help assess" integrity, which is a claim about evidence rather than about outcome.

**Is sample tracking the same thing?**
No. Tracking tells you where a sample is now. A chain of custody documents accountability for each handling, storage and transfer event, including who did it and when it was recorded. **A provider can have one without the other.**

**Can I see my own chain of custody record?**
Ask. There is no universal right to it in consumer storage, and practice varies. **A provider that cannot produce a sample's handling history on request is telling you something about how that history is kept.**

**Does an accreditation certificate prove the chain of custody is sound?**
It proves an outside body assessed the facility against a standard, within a defined scope, as of a date. **Check the scope and the current status on the accreditor's own register, because a facility can remain accredited while the scope narrows past the thing you care about.**
