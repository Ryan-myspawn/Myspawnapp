# What Happens If a Cryogenic Storage Tank Fails?

**Primary audience:** Men 25-55
**Trend / hook used:** none claimed, and that is honest. **This is not a news article.** The incidents below are from 2018, 2019, 2021 and 2023, and they are here because a reader deciding where to put biological material this month needs to know how these systems actually fail, not what happened last week.

**If a cryogenic storage tank fails, the material inside it warms up and is usually destroyed, and the thing that decides how bad it gets is not the tank. It is whether anybody finds out in time. Tanks fail in two main ways: the vacuum insulation degrades, or the liquid nitrogen runs out. Both are survivable if caught. The reason they often are not caught is that temperature, the thing most alarms measure, is a late signal: in one bench study an intact tank lost ten percent of its liquid in about four to five days, but took roughly six weeks to warm to minus 185 degrees Celsius. By the time the thermometer moves, most of the nitrogen is already gone.**

## The two failure modes, and why one of them is invisible

**Vacuum loss.** A cryogenic dewar keeps its contents cold the way a thermos does, with a vacuum between two walls. That vacuum can degrade, and clinic-focused sources note that heavy handling makes it more likely. A tank losing vacuum does not look different from the outside.

**Liquid nitrogen depletion.** Nitrogen boils off continuously by design. The tank is topped up on a schedule. Miss the schedule, or lose the automatic fill, and the level falls.

**Here is the part that matters, and it is a measurement problem rather than an engineering one.** A **2019 study in the *Journal of Assisted Reproduction and Genetics*** compared a weight-based tank alarm against a temperature-based one. **An intact tank lost about ten percent of its liquid nitrogen in roughly 4.2 to 4.9 days. Warming to minus 185 degrees Celsius took 37.8 to 43.7 days.** *(Peer-reviewed, bench study.)*

**Read those two numbers next to each other.** The liquid starts going in days. The temperature does not report it for weeks, and then it rises fast, because once only a few centimetres of nitrogen remain at the bottom, there is nothing left to hold the temperature down. **A temperature alarm is a smoke detector that waits for the roof to catch.**

## What actually happened, in three documented cases

**A note before the list, and it is not a formality. This is not an article about fertility clinics being careless.** Embryologists work with irreplaceable material under real constraints, and the best-documented failures are documented precisely because those fields take them seriously enough to litigate, investigate and publish. **A sector that hides its failures produces no case studies at all.**

### San Francisco, March 2018

A cryogenic tank failed at a San Francisco fertility centre. **Our sources disagree on the scale and we are printing the range rather than picking one: one account says roughly 3,500 frozen eggs and embryos, another says approximately 4,000 belonging to about 600 clients.** More than 550 legal actions followed.

**In June 2021 a federal jury found the tank's manufacturer, Chart Inc., 90 percent responsible and awarded five patients just under $15 million**, reported precisely as $14.975 million. **A trade publication reports the manufacturer later reserved more than $300 million against related claims.** *(The verdict is reported by named outlets; the reserve figure is from trade press and is labelled as such. The characterisation of the cause as a manufacturing defect comes from plaintiff-side material and should be read in that light.)*

### Cleveland, the same period

An earlier US tank failure in Cleveland is described in our sources as having affected the eggs or embryos of **approximately 700 women**.

**This one carries a warning rather than a citation. Exactly one source in our sweep mentions it, it gives no institution by name, and we are not going to supply one from memory.** **The figure is single-sourced and the institution is unconfirmed.** We include it only because a second large failure in the same period is a material fact about how often this happens, and omitting it would make the picture look rarer than it is.

### A biobank, 2023

Over the 2023 Christmas holidays a freezer failure at a major research institute caused a warm-up across **16 cryogenic tanks**. **The institution's own published estimate of what was destroyed: 34,400 biobank samples, 3,800 samples from animal models, 2,600 from cell lines and 6,300 from manipulated or edited cell lines.**

**Its internal review is the most useful sentence in this whole article: the failure "did not occur due to any single event or source of error, but was the result of multiple issues."** An external review was to follow, led by a biobank professional from another institution.

**Three labels on that one, all of which matter.** **It is not American**, and nothing in it should be read as a claim about US institutions or US law; it is cited as an engineering and governance fact. **The counts are the institution's own estimate**, and are written here as an estimate. **And the institution published them itself.** A sector learns only from organisations that do that, and an article that treats self-disclosure as an indictment teaches exactly the wrong lesson.

## The alarm is usually the real story

**In two US fertility-clinic incidents, both facilities had remote alarm systems.** In at least one, **the alarm may have been switched off, and the clinic's own letter said nobody knew who had disabled it or for how long.** In one case the facility already knew the tank needed preventative maintenance and was troubleshooting it with the manufacturer.

**And the scale of quiet failure is worse than the headline events suggest.** A cord blood bank reported that temperature excursions had gone **undetected in seven of its twenty-two storage tanks.** Not a catastrophe. Just a third of the estate drifting without anybody knowing.

**The regulator-documented version of the same design flaw is not about samples at all.** In a **2021 Georgia incident investigated by the US Chemical Safety Board, a failed liquid-nitrogen level-control system allowed an overflow and six workers died.** The Board's findings named the **single point of failure** and the **absence of atmospheric oxygen monitoring and alarms**. *(We cite it once, carefully, because it is the clearest official statement that a safety system depending on one sensor is not a safety system. It is a worker-safety case, not a sample-loss case, and it is not being used as a metaphor.)*

**The pattern across all of them is the same: the tank was the smallest problem.** The problems were a late-reporting sensor, an alarm nobody could prove was on, a maintenance ticket that was open rather than closed, and a review that found multiple causes rather than one.

## The questions worth asking any facility, cryogenic or not

These are short, they are answerable in writing, and a facility that cannot answer them has told you something.

1. **What triggers your alarm: temperature, level, or weight?** If the answer is temperature only, ask what the expected lag is between nitrogen loss and a temperature excursion.
2. **Who receives the alarm at 3am on a public holiday, and how is that tested?** Vendor guidance in this field consistently recommends alerts that reach staff at any hour, periodic alarm testing, and permission-limited alarm settings.
3. **Can an alarm be disabled, and is that action logged against a named person?**
4. **When a tank is flagged for maintenance, what happens to the material inside it in the meantime?**
5. **Has anything ever gone wrong here, and what did the review find?** A facility that has never had an incident in a decade is either very good or not looking.

## Where this leaves ambient storage, including ours

**MySpawn stores a dried DNA record at ambient temperature with GenVault in New Jersey. $99 a year, billed annually, with the first 1,000 members locking the founding rate.** The obvious thing to say here is that none of the above can happen to us, and it is half true in a way that would be dishonest to leave unqualified.

**What is true: a dry, room-temperature record has no liquid nitrogen to run out, no vacuum jacket to degrade, no automatic fill to fail, and no 3am alarm to miss.** Those specific failure modes are genuinely absent, and that is the real argument for the storage model. **We explain what does and does not degrade DNA over time in [how long DNA lasts](/blog/how-long-does-dna-last), and why gametes are a different product with different physics in [how to preserve your DNA](/blog/how-to-preserve-your-dna).**

**What is also true, and we published it first on 17 October rather than waiting to be asked: we do not currently have a published re-testing schedule or published acceptance thresholds for stored member samples.** Ambient storage removes the nitrogen failure modes. It does not remove humidity, contamination, mislabelling, or the plainest failure of all, which is a company that stops existing. **Those are our failure modes and they deserve the same five questions.**

**The honest summary is not "cryogenic bad, ambient good." It is that every storage model fails somehow, and the only thing that separates a recoverable failure from a total one is whether somebody is measuring the right variable and would find out in time.**

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens to samples if a cryogenic tank fails?

They warm up, and biological material that depends on staying below roughly minus 136 degrees Celsius is usually destroyed. How much is lost depends almost entirely on how quickly the failure is detected, not on the tank itself.

### Why do cryogenic storage tanks fail?

Two main ways: the vacuum insulation between the tank's walls degrades, or the liquid nitrogen inside runs out and is not replaced. Handling and age make vacuum loss more likely. Both are survivable if caught early.

### How long does it take for a failing tank to get warm?

Longer than most people expect, which is the problem. In a 2019 bench study an intact tank lost about ten percent of its liquid nitrogen in 4.2 to 4.9 days but took 37.8 to 43.7 days to warm to minus 185 degrees Celsius. Temperature is a late signal.

### Are fertility clinics unsafe?

No, and this article is not an argument that they are. The documented failures are documented because those fields investigate and litigate them. The useful question is not whether a facility is careful but what it measures and who gets told.

### Does ambient-temperature DNA storage avoid these problems?

It avoids these specific ones. A dried record has no liquid nitrogen to run out and no vacuum to lose. It has different exposures, including humidity, contamination, labelling and the custody of the company holding it, and those deserve the same questions.

**Ask whoever holds anything of yours which variable they measure and what happens at 3am when it moves. Ask in writing. The answer, or the absence of one, is the information.**
