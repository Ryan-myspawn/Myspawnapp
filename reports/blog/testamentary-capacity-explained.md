Testamentary capacity is the mental standard a person must meet at the moment they sign a will. **It is a lower bar than most people assume, it is measured at the instant of signing rather than across a lifetime, and a diagnosis of dementia does not by itself make a will invalid.** The standard asks whether you understood what you were signing, roughly what you owned, and who would normally expect to inherit. It does not ask whether you made a sensible decision, a fair one, or the same one you would have made a decade earlier.

That gap, between what people think the standard is and what it actually asks, is where most of the confusion in late-life planning lives.

## What the standard actually asks

The formulation American courts reach for most often traces to an English case from 1870, *Banks v Goodfellow*, which is still cited in US, English, Canadian and Australian courts today. **LABEL: this is a common-law framing carried in practitioner commentary, not a US statute. Every state writes its own version and the wording differs.**

The framing has three positive elements and one negative one. The person signing must be able to understand that they are making a will and what a will does; must understand, in general terms, the extent of what they own; and must be able to comprehend the claims of the people who might expect to benefit, including the ones being left out. The negative element is that they must not be under the influence of a condition that distorts the particular decision being made.

**The crucial qualifier is one courts have repeated for a century and a half: these capacities do not have to be held in the highest degree, or even to the degree the person once held them.** The test is not whether you are as sharp as you were at fifty. It is whether, at the moment of signing, you could do those three things.

## Why the bar is lower than people expect

Practitioner and study material commonly describes testamentary capacity as a *lower* threshold than contractual capacity, the standard for entering a binding agreement. **LABEL: we found this stated in secondary and exam-preparation sources and could not confirm it against a statute or a leading case, so treat it as the common professional description rather than a verified rule.**

The logic behind it is not hard to follow. A contract is negotiated against a counterparty with opposing interests, in real time, with consequences the signer must live with. A will takes effect only after death, can be revoked at any point before then, and is executed with witnesses present. The protective machinery sits in different places.

What follows from the lower bar is the part that matters practically. **A person can be unable to manage their own finances, hold a power of attorney in force, and still have capacity to sign a valid will.** Those are three different assessments, and failing one says nothing automatic about the others. This is also why our article on [what a self-proving affidavit is](/blog/self-proving-affidavit-will) matters more than it looks: the affidavit has the signer attest to sound mind and freedom from constraint, which are precisely the elements a later challenge has to attack.

## A diagnosis is not an answer

Because capacity is measured at the moment of execution, the law recognises the **lucid interval**: a period during which someone with a cognitive condition meets the standard, even if they would not have met it the week before or the week after. Where incapacity has already been established before the will was drawn, the burden generally falls on the person relying on the will to show it was signed during such an interval.

**Here is the honest complication, and it runs against the convenient reading.** A 2015 paper in the *Journal of the American Academy of Psychiatry and the Law* by Shulman and colleagues argues that the familiar idea of good days and bad days in dementia may not transfer to testamentary capacity the way courts have traditionally assumed. **LABEL: peer-reviewed, 2015, and an argument rather than a settled finding.** Fluctuating alertness is not the same thing as fluctuating ability to understand an estate and the claims on it.

So the position a careful reader should hold is two-sided. A dementia diagnosis does not void a will. And the lucid-interval doctrine is not a reliable repair for a will signed when understanding was genuinely gone. Both of those are true at once, which is uncomfortable, and any source that gives you only one half of it is selling something.

## The challenge that usually does the damage is not capacity

In practice, capacity is frequently pleaded and **undue influence** is frequently what the fight is actually about. The two get argued together and they are not the same claim. Capacity asks whether the signer understood. Undue influence asks whether the will reflects someone else's intentions substituted for theirs.

Commentary is consistent on why undue influence is hard: it is exercised in private, so challengers usually work from circumstantial evidence. The law in most places also draws a line between influence and persuasion. Ordinary pressure from a family member is not enough; there has to be something closer to coercion.

## Who has to prove what, and why it depends where you are

**This is the part that cannot be answered nationally, and anyone who answers it nationally is guessing.** The general rule across US jurisdictions is that the person contesting the will carries the burden. The important exception is a presumption that can shift it.

In broad terms, where a **confidential relationship** existed between the signer and a substantial beneficiary, and **suspicious circumstances** surrounded the making or signing of the will, a presumption of undue influence can arise and the burden moves to the person defending the will. From there the states diverge sharply:

- **New Jersey** requires suspicious circumstances that need only be slight, and the proponent must then overcome the presumption on the preponderance of the evidence. A 12 January 2026 Appellate Division decision, *In re Estate of Olga Kolbik*, held that a trial court had misapplied the law on both the suspicious-circumstances analysis and the burden. **LABEL: we read a summary of this decision through a search index and did not open the opinion itself.**
- **Florida** applies a preponderance standard under its probate code and, through the *Carpenter* line of cases, asks whether a substantial beneficiary in a confidential relationship was **active in procuring** the will.
- **Arizona** is reported to shift the burden of persuasion rather than merely the burden of producing evidence, which is a materially heavier thing to carry.
- Some courts treat the presumption as a **bursting bubble** that disappears the moment any rebutting evidence arrives.

The practical reading of that list is not that one state is better. It is that **the same facts can produce different outcomes in different states**, so the only version of this question worth answering is the one asked about a specific state. The same shape appears in [whether a spouse can override a will](/blog/can-a-spouse-override-a-will), where the deciding variable is not the headline rule but what the rule applies to.

## What this means if you are planning late

Four things follow, and none of them require a lawyer to understand:

1. **Do it earlier than feels necessary.** Capacity is assessed at signing, so the single most effective protection is a document signed while nobody could plausibly argue about it.
2. **Expect the fight to be about influence, not understanding.** If a late change benefits one person who was also driving to the appointments and holding the paperwork, that is the shape a presumption is built from, regardless of how clear-headed the signer was.
3. **Contemporaneous evidence beats later reconstruction.** A capacity assessment at the time of signing, properly witnessed execution, and a self-proving affidavit are all cheap at the time and expensive to recreate afterwards.
4. **Ask your state's rule, not the general rule.** As the list above shows, the general rule is the least useful part of the answer.

## FAQ

### What is testamentary capacity in simple terms?
The ability, at the moment you sign, to understand that you are making a will, to know in general terms what you own, and to recognise who would normally expect to inherit, including anyone you are leaving out.

### Can someone with dementia make a valid will?
Yes, potentially. Capacity is judged at the moment of signing, not by diagnosis. The lucid-interval doctrine allows for it. But peer-reviewed commentary argues that fluctuating alertness in dementia does not map neatly onto the ability to understand an estate, so it is not a dependable fix for a will signed too late.

### Is testamentary capacity the same as being able to manage your money?
No. They are separate assessments. Someone can lack the capacity to manage their finances, with a power of attorney in place, and still meet the standard for signing a will.

### Who has to prove a will is invalid?
Usually the person challenging it. That can flip where a confidential relationship and suspicious circumstances raise a presumption of undue influence, and what it takes to raise and rebut that presumption varies substantially between states.

### What is the difference between capacity and undue influence?
Capacity asks whether the signer understood what they were doing. Undue influence asks whether the document records somebody else's wishes in place of theirs. They are argued together and decided separately.
