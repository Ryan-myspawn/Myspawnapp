If you are a man in your thirties, the honest legacy checklist is shorter than the internet suggests: **term life insurance sized to your actual obligations, correct beneficiary designations, a will (plus guardianship if you have kids), and access instructions for your digital accounts.** Those four cover almost everything financial. Then there are two free things almost nobody does — a written family health history and a recorded interview with your parents — and one item with a real expiration date attached to a person rather than a decision. Most men in this age bracket have done none of it, and the data says they know it.

## The numbers, so you know you are not unusual

- **58% of millennials have no estate planning documents at all**, per [Trust & Will's 2026 Estate Planning Report](https://trustandwill.com/learn/estate-planning-report-2026) — while **73% say estate planning is personally important**. That belief-to-action gap is the whole story of this decade of life.
- **52% of millennials own life insurance**, though about **80% acknowledge needing it**; roughly **44% report no coverage**.
- Men are actually somewhat better covered than women here (**36% of men uninsured vs 47% of women**), which is not a victory so much as a low bar.
- **Half of millennials overestimate what coverage costs.** The top stated barriers under 40 are life-stage misalignment (32%), premium cost (28%), and no immediate benefit (25%).
- **32% of millennials have never discussed end-of-life wishes and do not plan to** — the highest of any generation.

Read that last one twice. The paperwork gap is bad; the conversation gap is worse, and it is free to close.

## The four that are actually urgent

### 1. Term life insurance, sized to obligations not to vibes

If anyone depends on your income — partner, kids, a mortgage you signed together, a parent you help — this is the highest-leverage financial decision available to you in your thirties, and it will never be cheaper than it is today. Size it against real numbers: outstanding mortgage, years of income replacement, childcare, education. Term, not whole, unless a professional gives you a specific reason otherwise. The "it costs too much" barrier is usually a pricing misconception; get one real quote before believing the estimate in your head.

### 2. Beneficiary designations (the ones that quietly override your will)

This is the most common expensive mistake in the entire category. **Retirement accounts and life insurance policies pass by beneficiary designation, not by will.** An outdated designation — an ex, a deceased parent, a blank — sends money somewhere your will explicitly says it shouldn't, and no amount of estate drafting fixes it. Log in to every account today and check. It takes twenty minutes and costs nothing.

### 3. A will, and guardianship if you have children

If you have kids, the guardianship clause is the reason to do this now rather than at 45. If you don't, a will still decides who handles your things and who is spared the process. This is the item where 58% of your peers are currently at zero.

### 4. Digital access instructions

Your accounts are governed by platform tools and by law, not by your intentions. Apple's Legacy Contact and Google's Inactive Account Manager each take about ten minutes. Beyond those, write down where things live — password manager, two-factor method, the one email address everything routes through. Our guide on [what happens to your digital life](/blog/digital-legacy-what-happens) covers the legal layer, which is less generous than most people assume.

## The two free things nobody does

### A written family health history

What did your parents and grandparents die of, at what age, and what did they have that nobody talked about? Write it down with names, relationships and ages at diagnosis. Every doctor you and your children will ever see wants this document, and it is genuinely useful in a way that most consumer genetic testing is not. Cost: one conversation.

### A recorded interview with your parents

You are in the last decade where this is easy. Ask what they actually did all day, what they were proud of that nobody noticed, what their parents were like, and what runs in the family. Record it on the phone in your pocket. Our [five-question format](/blog/video-message-18th-birthday) works for this too.

The relevant statistic from above: a third of your peers plan never to have this conversation. It is not a morbid conversation. It is a curiosity conversation that happens to have a deadline.

## The one item with an actual expiry date

## FAQ

**What should a 30-year-old man have in place legally?**
Term life insurance if anyone depends on your income, correct beneficiary designations, a will with guardianship provisions if you have children, and digital account access instructions. That covers the majority of real-world scenarios.

**Is life insurance worth it in your 30s if you have no kids?**
It depends on obligations, not age. If you share a mortgage, support a family member, or have co-signed debt, coverage matters. If nobody depends on your income, it is defensible to skip it and revisit when that changes.

**Do beneficiary designations override a will?**
Yes, for the accounts they govern — retirement accounts and life insurance policies typically pass by designation regardless of what a will says. This is the most common and most expensive oversight in personal estate planning.

**How much estate planning do millennials actually have done?**
58% have no estate planning documents at all, despite 73% saying it matters to them, according to Trust & Will's 2026 report.

**Is preserving DNA part of legacy planning?**
It is optional and it is not a financial protection. Its only distinctive feature is that it cannot be done retroactively, which is why some people do it early. Do the insurance, beneficiaries, will and digital access first.

**What is the single highest-value free thing I can do this month?**
Write your family health history and record one conversation with a parent. Both are free, both take under an hour, and neither can be reconstructed later.
