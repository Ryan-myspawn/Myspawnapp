# How to Read a Scope of Accreditation

**Direct answer:** The certificate is not the document that matters. **The scope is.** A scope of accreditation, sometimes called a schedule, is the published list of the specific activities a body actually assessed. **Accreditation is never general. A laboratory is accredited for named tests or methods, not for being a laboratory**, and it is free to perform other work that nobody has assessed. If you only read the certificate, you have read the cover and not the contents.

## What a scope statement actually contains

Published worked examples are consistent about the fields. A scope typically carries:

- **The organisation's name and the specific location.** Accreditation attaches to a site, not to a brand.
- **A named technical or quality manager**, and contact details.
- **A certificate number.**
- **A validity or expiry date.**
- **The list of accredited activities**, which is the body of the document and usually the longest part by far.

**That list is the point.** One published model scope, produced by an accreditation body for a fictitious calibration laboratory, is headed by the standard it was assessed against, a valid-to date and a certificate number, and then runs into entry-by-entry listings of individual calibrations. **The header is the part people photograph. The entries are the part that answers your question.**

### The sentence worth memorising

**A laboratory accredited for one method may also run tests that are not covered.** Guidance written for people choosing laboratories says exactly this: check that the specific analysis you care about appears on the scope, because the organisation is accredited for particular tests listed on a published schedule rather than in general.

**Nothing improper is happening when a lab does unaccredited work.** Plenty of useful work sits outside any accreditation. The error is on the reading side: seeing a certificate on a wall and assuming it covers whatever you are buying.

## Ranges, points, and the detail that gets skipped

Where an activity covers a range rather than a single value, the document should say which parts of that range are accredited. Published guidance on reading a schedule states that **the certificate should identify which points are accredited**, rather than implying that an entire range is covered because the activity appears at all.

In practice this is where a scope stops being a yes or no and becomes a map. **The question is not "are they accredited" but "is the thing I need inside the accredited part".**

## Flexible scopes, and why a scope can change without a new document reaching you

This is the item almost nobody outside the field knows about, and it is the reason to check a scope rather than file it.

**European Accreditation's document EA-4/17, on describing scopes for medical laboratories, covers scope formats including three-column and four-column layouts, and describes FLEXIBLE scopes.** Under a flexible scope, an organisation may add activities on the basis of defined criteria, without each addition being a fresh assessment event in the way a new scope grant would be.

**The practical consequence: a scope is a living document.** A copy you downloaded last year is a snapshot, and the authoritative version is whatever the accreditation body publishes today on its own register. **This is the same point our article on [what happens when a biobank loses accreditation](/blog/biobank-loses-accreditation) makes about status, applied to the other half of the certificate.** That article tells you to check the scope. This one tells you what you are looking at when you do.

## An honest limit, because it matters for storage specifically

**Every worked example above comes from testing, calibration or medical laboratory accreditation.** ISO/IEC 17025 is the standard for testing and calibration laboratories; the EA-4/17 guidance addresses medical laboratories. **Biobanking has its own standard, ISO 20387, and we could not find an equivalent published model scope written for biobanks.**

**So treat the field list and the flexible-scope concept as the general shape of these documents, and do not assume a biobank's scope will be formatted identically.** We are telling you where the examples come from rather than implying a template exists that we have not seen. **That is the same gap our earlier article flagged for sanctions, and it has not closed.**

## Four things to check, in order

1. **Find the scope, not the certificate.** It lives on the accreditation body's register, under the organisation's name and site. If a provider sends you a certificate image instead, ask for the register entry.
2. **Read the validity date and compare it with today.** A scope is a statement about a period.
3. **Find your specific activity in the list of accredited activities.** Not a similar one. Yours. If it covers a range, find your point in that range.
4. **Check the site.** A group can hold accreditation at one location and do your work at another.

**None of that requires technical knowledge and all four take minutes.** The hard part is knowing the list exists.

## FAQ

**Is the certificate or the scope the important document?**
The scope. The certificate records that accreditation exists; the scope records what it covers. **A certificate without its scope cannot tell you whether your specific need is accredited.**

**Can a laboratory do work that is not on its scope?**
Yes, and it is common and not improper. Accreditation covers named activities, and organisations routinely perform other work. **The risk is assuming a certificate covers everything a provider does.**

**What is a flexible scope?**
A scope format under which an organisation may add activities on the basis of defined criteria. European Accreditation's EA-4/17 guidance on medical laboratory scopes describes flexible scopes alongside three-column and four-column formats. **It means the authoritative scope is the one published today, not the copy you saved.**

**Does accreditation apply to a company or to a site?**
Published scopes name a specific location. **A group can be accredited at one site and perform your work at another, so check the address as well as the name.**

**What should a scope list for a measurement range?**
Guidance on reading schedules says the certificate should identify which points within a range are accredited, rather than implying the whole range is covered. **If your value sits outside the identified points, your work is outside the scope.**

**Is there a published model scope for biobanks?**
We could not find one. The worked examples available are for testing, calibration and medical laboratories. **Biobanking is covered by ISO 20387, and we are not going to present a testing-laboratory template as if it were a biobank one.**
