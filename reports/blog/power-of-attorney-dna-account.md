**Short answer: probably not, unless the document says so in words.** Under the digital-assets law now in force across most of the United States, an agent under a power of attorney is a fiduciary with limited reach. **The contents of your communications require express authorisation in the document itself; a general "all my property" clause is widely held not to be enough.** And because a DNA account's value is almost entirely content, an agent who gets the limited tier often gets something close to nothing.

## The distinction nobody makes: agent versus executor

These are two different people with two different clocks, and the paperwork that creates one does not create the other.

- **An agent under a power of attorney acts while you are ALIVE.** The authority usually begins either immediately or on incapacity, and **it ends at the moment of your death.**
- **An executor acts after you die.** Their authority begins where the agent's ended.

The gap that matters is the one in the middle. **If you have a stroke at 58, your executor has no authority at all, because you are not dead.** Your agent is the only person holding anything, and what your agent can reach depends on a clause most powers of attorney do not contain. Our article on [who inherits your DNA testing account](/blog/who-inherits-your-dna-testing-account) covers the second half of that timeline and deliberately does not cover this half.

## The law underneath, in one paragraph and no more

The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act is the model law governing who may reach your digital property, and it treats agents, executors, trustees and conservators as fiduciaries with differing powers. **Our [digital legacy guide](/blog/digital-legacy-what-happens) explains its three-tier hierarchy and the platform tools that sit at the top of it.** This article stays on one narrow question that guide does not answer: **what an AGENT gets, while you are alive.**

## Catalogue and content are not the same thing

This is the whole mechanism, and it is simpler than it sounds.

**The CATALOGUE is the metadata.** Who communicated with whom, on what date, with what subject line. **The CONTENT is what was actually said.**

**For content, the rule is strict and consistent across the sources we could reach: an agent reaches the contents of electronic communications only where the power of attorney EXPRESSLY authorises it.** A general property clause is described as insufficient ([Braverman Law Group on Colorado](https://www.braverman-law.com/blog/digital-assets-in-probate-applying-colorados-rufadaa-to-protect-your-online-footprint/), [JDJ Law](https://www.jdjlaw.com/post/how-to-prepare-for-your-digital-afterlife)).

**For the catalogue, THE SOURCES DISAGREE, and the disagreement is the single most useful thing on this page.**

- **One Pennsylvania firm's write-up says a power of attorney granting general authority to act DOES reach the catalogue of electronic communications and other non-content digital assets, with content still requiring an express grant** under the state's provision at 20 Pa.C.S. section 3909 ([lawyermarc.com](https://lawyermarc.com/legal-updates/digital-assets-rufadaa-pennsylvania/)).
- **One Connecticut guide says the opposite: that a general grant reaches NEITHER the catalogue nor other digital assets, and that specific digital-asset authority or general authority to act is required**, with content governed separately at section 45a-334j ([settledestate.com](https://settledestate.com/connecticut/digital-assets/)).

**We cannot resolve that from here and we are not going to pretend otherwise.** Both descriptions are secondary sources describing enacted state variants of a model act, **and state variants are exactly where a model act stops being uniform.** **The practical consequence is the same either way: do not rely on a general clause.**

**California is a live example of the ground still moving.** One guide describes 2025 amendments adding agent-specific sections at 879.1 and 879.2 for agents holding express digital authority ([settledestate.com on California](https://settledestate.com/california/digital-assets/)). **Treat that as a pointer to check, not as a statement of California law.**

## Why this bites harder for a DNA account than for email

Consider what the catalogue tier actually yields on a genetic testing service.

**You would learn that a message was exchanged with a match on a date. You would not learn the match, the relationship, the health reports, the raw file, or anything a family would actually want.** **On a DNA account, almost everything of value sits on the content side of the line.**

Then there is the second problem, which is not legal at all. **Consumer DNA companies do not generally recognise fiduciaries. They recognise a product setting.** Some offer a named "legacy contact" or beneficiary feature; some offer nothing and the widely repeated guidance is that you hand over your login. **A statute that entitles your agent to something is of limited use against a support queue with no process for it.** Our article on [genetic privacy laws](/blog/genetic-privacy-laws) covers the duties these companies owe you; none of them is a duty to build a fiduciary workflow.

## What to actually do, in the order that matters

1. **Put an express digital-assets clause in your power of attorney, naming CONTENT of electronic communications specifically.** This is the step that every source agrees on and the only one that survives a state-by-state difference.
2. **Check whether your state's version reaches the catalogue on a general grant.** Given that two guides describe two different answers, **assume yours does not until a lawyer in your state tells you it does.**
3. **Set the company's own tool if one exists**, because platform tools generally sit above estate documents in the hierarchy.
4. **Write down which services hold what.** An agent with perfect authority and no inventory still has nothing to act on. Our guide for [people planning without close family](/blog/single-no-kids-legacy-planning) covers naming agents when the obvious candidates do not exist.
5. **Separate the thing from the account.** This is the part most planning misses, and it is the next section.

## FAQ

**Does a power of attorney let someone log into my DNA testing account?**
Usually not on its own. An agent reaches the contents of electronic communications only where the power of attorney expressly authorises it, and a general property clause is widely described as insufficient. Separately, most consumer DNA companies have no process for recognising an agent at all.

**What is the difference between the catalogue and the content of my account?**
The catalogue is metadata: who communicated, when, and the subject line. The content is what was said. For a DNA account, almost everything a family would want sits on the content side, which is the side that needs express authorisation.

**Does my power of attorney still work after I die?**
No. An agent's authority ends at death, and an executor's begins. They are two different roles created by two different documents, and neither covers the other's period.

**Does a general "all my property" clause cover digital assets?**
For content, the sources we could reach agree it does not. For the catalogue, two state guides describe opposite answers, which is a good reason to assume the stricter one and add express language.

**What should the clause actually say?**
It should name digital assets specifically and authorise access to the content of electronic communications in terms, rather than relying on a general grant. Have a lawyer in your state draft it against your state's enacted version.

**Is my stored physical DNA sample affected by any of this?**
Not directly. A physical sample held by a biorepository is governed by the custody agreement you signed, not by digital-asset law. That is a different document to read carefully, and it is not a substitute for naming an agent.

**This article is general information about how a model law works, not legal advice, and no source in it was opened at its own domain. Every item here is a secondary description of an enacted state variant. Have a licensed attorney in your state confirm anything before you act on it.**
