# Autophagy, Explained: What Your Cells Throw Away and Why Three 2026 Studies Care

**The short answer: autophagy is how a cell digests its own broken parts, and 2026 is the year it stopped being a biology-class word and started being a drug target.** Damaged proteins and worn-out organelles get wrapped in a membrane, delivered to the lysosome, and broken down into pieces the cell reuses. When that disposal system slows, debris accumulates. A small-molecule drug aimed at restoring it is being tested in humans as you read this. None of that means you should buy a supplement, and the gap between the mechanism and the marketing is the most useful thing in this article.

## What autophagy actually is

The word means "self-eating," and it was coined by the Belgian scientist **Christian de Duve**, who won the **1974 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine** for discovering the lysosome, the compartment where the digesting happens.

The mechanism stayed poorly understood for decades. **Yoshinori Ohsumi** won the **2016 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine** for working out the molecular principles governing it, using baker's yeast in the 1990s.

The process itself is easy to picture:

1. A membrane forms around damaged proteins, worn-out organelles or invading pathogens, making a vesicle called an **autophagosome**.
2. That vesicle fuses with a **lysosome**, which is acidic and full of enzymes.
3. The contents are broken down into smaller building blocks, which the cell reuses.

Two things make it more than housekeeping. It runs constantly at a baseline rate, and it can be **ramped up by stress**, including nutrient starvation. And it can be **selective**: cells can tag one specific damaged organelle for disposal. The version that targets mitochondria has its own name, **mitophagy**, and it matters later in this article.

**Dysregulation cuts both ways.** Too little autophagy lets damage accumulate. Too much is also harmful. Published reviews link dysregulation in both directions to disease across the respiratory, circulatory, digestive, urinary and nervous systems, and to cancer. *(Peer-reviewed review literature, not a single study.)*

## Why it is suddenly everywhere in 2026

Three unrelated pieces of work have landed on the cell's disposal system this year. None of them cites the others. That is what makes the convergence worth noticing.

### 1. A drug in humans right now

**Retro Biosciences became a clinical-stage company in 2025 by dosing the first participant in a Phase 1 trial of RTR242**, a small molecule designed to **restore lysosomal function**. The thesis is specific: as people age, and particularly in neurodegenerative disease, lysosomes lose acidity and efficiency, so the disposal system degrades before the damage does.

The trial details, as reported: **randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, in healthy volunteers**, at an early-phase clinical unit in **Adelaide, Australia**. **Eight people took the drug orally in mid-December.** The study includes **exploratory biomarkers tied to autophagy and lysosomal biology**, which is the company's first chance to see whether its mechanism does anything measurable in a human being. In May 2026, at a public summit, Retro's Betts-LaCroix said the trial was going well with **no dose-limiting toxicities** and some data expected around August. *(Trade and company sourcing, not peer-reviewed. Treat the progress remarks as a company's own account.)*

**Say the obvious part out loud: a Phase 1 trial in healthy volunteers is a safety study.** It is designed to answer "does this hurt people and at what dose," not "does this work." A clean Phase 1 is necessary and it is not evidence of benefit.

A second company, **Lysoway Therapeutics (LW-1017)**, has also been reported advancing an autophagy-targeting neurodegeneration candidate toward human testing. *(Reported, not peer-reviewed.)*

### 2. Microplastics, arriving through the lysosome

Separately, a **2026 paper in the *Journal of Hazardous Materials*** reported that polystyrene microplastics taken into Sertoli cells trigger **CEBPB-mediated lysosomal hyperactivation**, degrading the tight-junction proteins that maintain the blood-testis barrier. **Peer-reviewed, laboratory work, not a clinical trial in men.** The relevant point here is not fertility. It is that a toxicology finding arrived at the same organelle a longevity company is trying to repair.

### 3. Senescence, arriving through metabolism

And a **2026 *Nature* paper** (Martini et al., DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10791-2, peer-reviewed) traced how worn-out "zombie" cells drive inflammation, via mitochondrial metabolites feeding histone acetylation at inflammatory gene loci. Blocking a mitochondrial citrate carrier reduced inflammation and improved healthspan **in aged mice**.

Three fields, one neighbourhood: the cell's systems for handling its own damaged parts.

## The part everyone actually searches for: fasting and supplements

This is where the honest answer diverges from the marketing.

**Fasting and autophagy.** The link is real and reasonably well established, but mostly outside human trials. Spermidine levels rise during fasting in yeast, flies, mice **and human volunteers**, and blocking spermidine synthesis reduces fasting-induced autophagy **in human cells**. The downstream cascade runs through hypusination of EIF5A, which favours production of pro-autophagic proteins.

What that does **not** establish is a dose. There is no validated human protocol that says fasting for a particular number of hours produces a particular amount of autophagy in a particular tissue, and anyone quoting you a magic number is quoting a mouse or an assumption.

**Spermidine.** Fasting-induced autophagy appears to depend on it, and supplementation has been studied in older adults. **Long-term human safety data beyond about four months of randomized observation is still accumulating, and there is no human lifespan data at all.** The POLYCAD trial of high-dose spermidine in older adults with coronary artery disease completed recruitment in August 2025 and was expected to finish in 2026.

**Urolithin A.** This has the **stronger published human randomized evidence** of the two as of 2026, including a 2022 trial reporting improvements in muscle endurance and mitochondrial gene expression in adults over 40, and a 2026 systematic review of trials on muscle strength and physical performance. Note the mechanism: urolithin A works mainly through **mitophagy**, the disposal of damaged mitochondria, not general autophagy. They are not interchangeable, and a product that blurs them is telling you something about itself.

**The one-sentence summary:** autophagy induction by fasting is better established in animals and cells than in robust human trials, urolithin A has the better human data of the two supplements, spermidine has the better mechanistic story, and neither has shown that it extends human life.

## FAQ

**What is autophagy in simple terms?**
It is the process by which a cell wraps up its own damaged parts, delivers them to an acidic compartment called the lysosome, and breaks them down into building blocks it can reuse.

**Who discovered autophagy?**
Christian de Duve discovered the lysosome and coined the term, winning the Nobel Prize in 1974. Yoshinori Ohsumi worked out the molecular machinery in yeast and won the Nobel Prize in 2016.

**Is there an autophagy drug in human trials?**
Yes. Retro Biosciences' RTR242, a small molecule intended to restore lysosomal function, entered a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 1 study in healthy volunteers in Australia, with the first participants dosed in mid-December. Phase 1 tests safety, not effectiveness.

**How long do you have to fast to trigger autophagy?**
There is no validated human number. Fasting-induced autophagy is well supported in animals and in human cells, but no trial has established how many hours of fasting produces how much autophagy in a given human tissue.

**Do spermidine or urolithin A supplements work?**
Urolithin A has the stronger published human randomized data as of 2026, mainly on muscle endurance and mitochondrial measures, and it works through mitophagy rather than general autophagy. Spermidine has a strong mechanistic case and thinner long-term human data. Neither has been shown to extend human lifespan.
