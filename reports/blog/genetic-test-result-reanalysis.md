Probably not, and nobody is breaking a rule. **There is no established legal duty requiring a laboratory or a doctor to contact you when your genetic variant is reclassified**, even when the new classification is clinically actionable. Professional bodies disagree with each other about whose job it is. Most guidance says laboratories should re-examine classifications when new evidence appears, but should not be expected to reanalyse your specific data unless somebody asks. In practice that means the person most likely to find out that your result changed is you, and only if you go looking.

## Your DNA did not change. The reading of it did

A genetic test result is two things stacked on top of each other: **a measurement, and an interpretation of that measurement.** The measurement is stable. Your sequence today is the sequence you had at birth. The interpretation is a judgement made by a laboratory on a particular date, using the evidence available on that date.

When a laboratory calls a variant pathogenic, benign, or **of uncertain significance**, it is recording the state of the evidence, not a permanent fact about you. New population data arrives. Expert panels publish curated opinions. Functional studies get done. A family somewhere else gets tested and a pattern becomes visible that was not visible before. [1]

Our article on [what a DNA sample can actually tell you](/blog/what-can-you-learn-from-dna-sample) covers what those categories mean and how often they move. This article is about the gap that opens afterwards.

## The gap: the laboratory updated it and your copy still says the old thing

This is not hypothetical, and there is a number for it.

The ClinGen patient registry **GenomeConnect** ran exactly this comparison. Researchers took variant classifications from participants' own genetic test reports and compared them against what the testing laboratory had most recently submitted to ClinVar. **Forty-nine of 481 classifications, 10.2 percent, were out of date compared with the laboratory's own current position.** [2]

Read that slowly. In roughly one case in ten, the laboratory had already changed its mind, published the change, and the patient was still holding a report that said something else. **The information existed. It had simply not travelled the last few feet.**

The same study found 49 reclassifications among 1,419 variants the registry shared, **3.4 percent**, of which **34, or 69.4 percent, were shared back with participants who had opted in to receive updates.** The direction matters and is reassuring: **34 variants moved from uncertain to benign or likely benign**, two moved from pathogenic to uncertain, and two from pathogenic to benign or likely benign. Most reclassification is good news. [2]

**Epistemic label, stated plainly: these figures are from a registry study published in *Genetics in Medicine* in 2021, using data through October 2020. They are five years old. They describe one registry's participants, not the general population, and we did not open the paper at its own domain.** They are the best concrete numbers available on this question, and they should be read as an illustration of a mechanism rather than as a current national rate.

## Whose job is it? Four bodies, four answers

The honest answer to "who should have told me" is that the field has not settled it.

- **The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics** publishes points-to-consider material aimed at helping laboratories build their own reinterpretation protocols. The thrust is that laboratories should revisit classifications when new evidence or updated methods appear, **but are not expected to reanalyse an individual's data unless it is requested.** [3]
- **The American Society of Human Genetics**, in a 2018 position statement written for the research context, assigns **shared responsibility** across laboratories, patients and ordering providers, and is blunt about the practical limit: **the ordering provider cannot promise that recontact will happen unless the patient initiates it.** The obligation is framed as a good-faith effort inside existing constraints. [3]
- **The Canadian College of Medical Geneticists** states that **there is currently no duty to recontact**, while allowing that there may be a responsibility, and its advisory is about how to do it when it is done rather than whether it must be. [4]
- **The European Society of Human Genetics** declines a positive obligation altogether, leaving it to clinical genetics services to decide in the patient's interest. [3]

Underneath all four sits the thing we wrote about in this month's work and will keep repeating: **there is no standard interval.** No guideline tells a laboratory to re-review prior results every two years, or five, or ever. Each laboratory sets its own practice, and surveys across the United States, the United Kingdom and Europe have found reinterpretation and recontact happening **on an ad hoc basis** with wide variation between providers. [3]

A 2025 perspective on the problem describes significant discrepancies between stated policies, actual laboratory practice, and what the various stakeholders think should happen. Proposed fixes exist, including a shared-responsibility model that puts **monitoring** with the laboratory, because that is where the curation infrastructure lives, and **recontact** with the clinician. **None of these proposals is binding on anybody today.** [3]

## What this means if you are the patient

It means the default is silence, and silence is not evidence that nothing changed.

It also means the practical burden is yours, which is unfair and is nonetheless the situation. Here is what actually works.

**Find out what your laboratory's policy is, in writing.** Not what your doctor thinks it is. Ask the laboratory directly: do you re-review prior results, how often, and under what trigger. Ask who is responsible for contacting you, and at which address. **Ask them to put the answer in writing.** If the answer is "we re-review when a case is requested", you now know you have to do the requesting.

**Ask for reanalysis explicitly, and date the request.** A request is the trigger most guidance actually contemplates. A reasonable interval to ask is every two to three years, or sooner if something changes in your family: a new diagnosis in a close relative, a new cancer, a sudden cardiac event in someone young.

**Keep your contact details current with the laboratory, not just the clinic.** Recontact fails most often at the most boring step. People move.

**Keep your own copy, with its date on it.** A result is only interpretable against the date it was issued. If you are holding a report with no date, you are holding an opinion with no expiry.

## This is a family document, not a personal one

A reclassified variant rarely affects one person. If your uncertain variant becomes pathogenic, the people it most urgently concerns may be siblings and children who were never tested and never saw the original report.

There is professional literature on the duty to inform patients about familial implications, and our [family health history guide](/blog/family-health-history-guide) covers how to have that conversation. The point here is narrower: **if nobody tells you, nobody tells them either.** The chain starts at a recontact that is not anybody's clear responsibility.

## FAQ

### Will my genetic testing lab tell me if my variant is reclassified?
Usually not automatically. **There is no established legal duty to recontact**, and professional guidance is divided on whose job it is. Most laboratories will re-examine classifications when new evidence appears but will not reanalyse your individual case unless somebody requests it. Ask your laboratory for its policy in writing. [3]

### How often should I ask for my genetic test to be reanalysed?
There is no official interval, because **no guideline sets one.** Every two to three years is a reasonable self-imposed rhythm, and sooner if a close relative receives a new diagnosis. The request itself is what triggers most laboratories' review process.

### How common is it for a result to be out of date?
In the GenomeConnect registry study, **10.2 percent of classifications on participants' own reports were out of date compared with what the laboratory had most recently submitted to ClinVar.** That figure is from a 2021 publication using data through October 2020, and describes one registry's participants rather than a national rate. [2]

### Is a reclassification usually bad news?
No. In that same study, **most reclassifications moved toward benign**: 34 variants went from uncertain to benign or likely benign, against two that moved from pathogenic to uncertain and two from pathogenic to benign. The most common outcome of waiting is that an uncertain finding resolves in your favour. [2]

### Who is responsible, the lab or my doctor?
Nobody has settled it. **ACMG** points laboratories toward their own protocols and does not expect case-level reanalysis without a request. **ASHG** calls it shared and says the provider cannot promise recontact unless the patient starts it. **The Canadian College of Medical Geneticists** says there is no duty but may be a responsibility. **ESHG** declines a positive obligation. A 2025 shared-responsibility proposal would put monitoring with laboratories and recontact with clinicians, **and it is a proposal, not a rule.** [3] [4]

### Does storing a DNA sample protect me from this?
Not from the notification problem, no. **Storage keeps material available; it does not keep interpretations current.** The two are separate problems and anybody selling you storage as a fix for the second one is overselling it.
