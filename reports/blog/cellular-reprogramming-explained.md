# Cellular Reprogramming, Explained: What It Means to Partly Un-Age a Cell

**The short answer: reprogramming is the trick of pushing an adult cell backwards along its own developmental path. Pushed all the way, it forgets what it is. Pushed partway and then stopped, it appears to keep its job and shed some markers of age. As of September 2026 that partway version is in a human being, and not one reprogramming candidate has published a human result.**

That last sentence is the whole article. Everything below explains why both halves of it are true at once, and why the gap between them is where the marketing goes wrong.

## Where this comes from

In 2006, **Shinya Yamanaka** and Kazutoshi Takahashi showed that an ordinary adult mouse cell could be turned back into something resembling an embryonic stem cell by forcing it to express four genes. Human cells followed in 2007. Yamanaka shared the **2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine** with John Gurdon for the finding.

The four genes are known as the **Yamanaka factors**: **OCT4, SOX2, KLF4 and c-MYC**, usually shortened to OSKM.

What they do is not genetic editing. The DNA sequence is unchanged. What changes is the **epigenome**: the layer of chemical marks and packaging that decides which genes a cell reads and which it ignores. A skin cell and a nerve cell in the same body carry the same genome and read it completely differently. Reprogramming rewrites the reading instructions.

Run the full program and you get an **induced pluripotent stem cell**, which can become almost any tissue. That is enormously useful for research and enormously unhelpful if the cell in question is currently doing a job inside a living person. A pluripotent cell in an adult organ is not a younger version of that organ. It is a cell with no assignment, and cells with no assignment form tumors.

## Partial reprogramming: stop before it forgets

The idea that made this an aging field rather than a stem-cell field is simple. Do not run the program to completion. Turn the factors on briefly, then off, and see whether the cell drops age-associated marks while keeping its identity.

**The 2016 experiment.** Ocampo and colleagues, publishing in *Cell*, used short cyclic bursts of OSKM in a mouse model of premature aging. The mice showed improvement in cellular and physiological hallmarks of aging and lived longer. The same paper showed the failure mode plainly: sustained high expression of the factors produced **teratomas in liver, kidney and pancreas**. The difference between a therapy and a tumor was the dosing schedule.

**The 2020 experiment.** Lu and colleagues, publishing in *Nature*, took the risk down further by **dropping c-MYC**, the factor most associated with cancer, and using only **OSK**. Delivered to mouse retinal ganglion cells, OSK restored youthful DNA methylation patterns, promoted nerve fiber regeneration after injury, and reversed vision loss both in a glaucoma model and in old mice. The effect depended on the DNA demethylase enzymes TET1 and TET2, which is a mechanistic detail worth knowing because it means the benefit ran through active erasure of epigenetic marks, not through some vaguer notion of rejuvenation.

Those two papers are the reason billions of dollars have moved into this field. **Altos Labs** launched in 2022 with roughly $3 billion. **NewLimit**, cofounded by Coinbase chief executive Brian Armstrong, raised a $435 million Series C on June 2, 2026 at a reported $3.1 billion valuation, and expects its first human study in 2027. **Turn Biotechnologies** and others are pursuing mRNA and small-molecule versions of the same idea.

## What changed in 2026

Everything above is mouse work. Here is the part that is not.

**Life Biosciences**, a company built on research from the Harvard laboratory of David Sinclair, received **FDA clearance of its IND in January 2026** for a candidate called **ER-100**, and on **June 9, 2026** announced that the first participant had been dosed. That is, on the company's account, the first time a partial epigenetic reprogramming therapy has been given to a human.

The design is worth reading closely, because the design is the safety argument:

- **What it targets.** Optic neuropathies: open-angle glaucoma and non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy. Both involve damage to retinal ganglion cells, which is exactly the cell type from the 2020 mouse paper.
- **How it is delivered.** A modified **adeno-associated virus (AAV)** vector carrying **OSK**, injected into the eye. The eye is a contained compartment with its own immune privilege, which limits how far anything can travel.
- **How it is switched on.** Expression is controlled by **systemic doxycycline** given for **eight weeks**. The factors are not permanently on. There is an off switch, and it is an antibiotic taken by mouth.
- **What it measures.** Safety, tolerability, immune response, and a set of visual assessments. Trial registration **NCT07290244**.

Note what is absent from that list: any measurement of the patient's age. This is a first-in-human safety study in an eye disease. It is not an age-reversal trial, and describing it as one is the first place the coverage goes wrong.

## The wording we had to change

On September 14 we wrote an approved line for internal use, meant to keep our own copy honest about this field. It said that several longevity candidates are in human trials right now and not one has a published result, and that no reprogramming candidate has human data at all.

Researching this article, we found the first half had gone stale.

On **September 7, 2026**, *Nature Biotechnology* published a Phase 2a trial of **rentosertib** in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis: 42 patients, with six independently developed proteomic aging clocks built into the analysis as exploratory endpoints. Patients in the 30mg twice-daily arm scored roughly 2.7 to 3.5 years younger than expected on those clocks at week four. So a longevity-relevant candidate has now published, with aging biomarkers as a measured endpoint inside a controlled trial.

Two things keep that from being the headline it sounds like. Rentosertib is a **TNIK inhibitor**, not a reprogramming therapy, so it says nothing about OSK. And the finding is that **biomarkers moved**, not that patients got younger. A protein panel reading younger is a measurement of a proxy. It is evidence about the proxy at least as much as it is evidence about the patient.

The corrected version of our line, which is what we will use from here:

> No reprogramming candidate has published human data. A partial reprogramming therapy is in humans: Life Biosciences dosed the first ER-100 patient on June 9, 2026. Adjacent longevity-relevant candidates have begun to publish, including a Phase 2a in *Nature Biotechnology* on September 7, 2026 that integrated six proteomic aging clocks and reported younger biomarkers, not younger patients.

We are recording the change rather than quietly editing, because a claim about the state of a field carries a date whether or not you print one, and ours had a shelf life of about six weeks.

## Four things this does not mean

**It does not mean aging has been reversed in a person.** One trial, in one organ, measuring safety. No efficacy claim exists yet because no efficacy data exists yet.

**It does not mean reprogramming is safe.** The whole reason the trial is confined to an eye, delivered by AAV, and gated behind a drug-controlled switch is that uncontrolled expression of these factors caused tumors in mice. The containment is the point.

**It does not mean a supplement can do this.** Nothing sold over the counter expresses OCT4, SOX2 or KLF4 in your cells. Products marketed on the back of reprogramming coverage are borrowing the credibility of an eye trial to sell something with no relationship to it.

**It does not mean your stored DNA is a ticket to any of it.** This one is ours to be clear about, so we will be. Preserving a genetic record is storage. It is not a treatment, it confers no health benefit, and it grants no access to any trial. The only honest connection between the two is timing: a stored record does not depend on whether any of this research ever works.

## What to actually watch

If you want to follow this field without being sold to, three signals matter more than any funding announcement:

1. **The ER-100 safety readout.** Immune response to the AAV vector, and whether the doxycycline switch behaves in humans the way it did in mice.
2. **Whether anyone publishes.** Registration is not data. A trial can run for years and produce nothing readable. The first peer-reviewed human result in reprogramming will be a genuine event, and as of this writing it has not happened.
3. **Whether a second tissue gets tried.** The eye was chosen because it is contained. A candidate aimed at a systemic organ would be the real test of whether this is a therapy platform or a very good eye drug.

## FAQ

**What is cellular reprogramming in simple terms?**
It is forcing an adult cell to express a set of genes that push it backwards toward an earlier, more flexible state. The DNA sequence does not change. What changes is the epigenome, the set of marks that decides which genes the cell reads.

**What are the Yamanaka factors?**
OCT4, SOX2, KLF4 and c-MYC. Shinya Yamanaka shared the 2012 Nobel Prize for showing that these four genes could turn an adult cell into a stem-cell-like state.

**What is the difference between full and partial reprogramming?**
Full reprogramming produces an induced pluripotent stem cell that has lost its original identity, which in a living body risks tumor formation. Partial reprogramming turns the factors on briefly and then off, with the aim of shedding age-associated marks while the cell keeps doing its job.

**Has cellular reprogramming been tested in humans?**
Yes, as of 2026. Life Biosciences dosed the first patient in a Phase 1 trial of ER-100 on June 9, 2026, for optic neuropathies, using an AAV vector carrying OSK and a doxycycline-controlled switch. It is a safety study, and no results have been published.

**Does reprogramming make you younger?**
No human result supports that. Mouse studies have shown restored function and, in a premature-aging model, longer life. In humans the only measurements so far are safety measurements. A separate 2026 trial of a different kind of drug reported younger-looking aging biomarkers, which is a proxy moving, not a person getting younger.

**Does preserving my DNA give me access to this?**
No. Storage is not a treatment and confers no health benefit or trial access. For what storage does and does not do, see [how long DNA lasts](/blog/how-long-does-dna-last) and [autophagy explained](/blog/autophagy-explained).
