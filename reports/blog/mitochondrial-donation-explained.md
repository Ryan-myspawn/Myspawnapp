Mitochondrial donation is an IVF-based technique that lets a woman who carries harmful mitochondrial DNA mutations have a genetically related child without passing on the disease. Her and her partner's nuclear DNA is transferred into a donor egg that has healthy mitochondria. As of 2026, it has produced eight healthy babies in the UK under a tightly licensed program, it remains effectively illegal to offer in the United States, and Australia is preparing the world's second regulated program under a law called Maeve's Law. Here is how it works, what the results actually show, and why your country decides whether you can have it.

## The problem it solves: your second genome

Most of your DNA — about 20,000 genes — sits in the cell nucleus, inherited half from each parent. But your cells carry a second, much smaller genome: mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), 37 genes that live inside the mitochondria, the structures that convert food into usable energy. You inherit mtDNA only from your mother.

When mtDNA carries certain mutations, the cells that need the most energy — brain, heart, muscle — fail first. Mitochondrial diseases such as Leigh syndrome and MELAS can cause seizures, organ failure, and early death, and there is no cure. Roughly 1 in 5,000 births is affected by mitochondrial disease caused by mtDNA or nuclear mutations, per the UK's [Newcastle University](https://www.ncl.ac.uk/press/articles/archive/2025/07/mitochondrialdonationtreatment/) research program.

Because mtDNA passes only through the egg, a mother who carries a high mutation load faces a genetic coin flip with every pregnancy. Mitochondrial donation was designed to remove that coin flip.

## How mitochondrial donation actually works

The UK program uses a method called **pronuclear transfer**:

1. The mother's egg is fertilized with the father's sperm by standard IVF. A donor egg with healthy mitochondria is fertilized the same way.
2. Within about 24 hours, the parents' pronuclei — the packets containing their combined nuclear DNA — are lifted out of their embryo.
3. The donor embryo's own pronuclei are removed and discarded, and the parents' pronuclei are placed into the donor embryo.
4. The result: an embryo with nuclear DNA from mom and dad, and mitochondria almost entirely from the donor.

A second method, **maternal spindle transfer**, does the same swap before fertilization — the mother's chromosomes are moved into an emptied donor egg, which is then fertilized. This is the method Australia's program is training on.

The "three-parent baby" headline is technically true but misleading in scale: the donor contributes those 37 mitochondrial genes — well under 0.1% of the child's total DNA — and no traits like appearance or personality, which are governed by nuclear DNA.

## What the eight babies tell us

In July 2025, the Newcastle team published two peer-reviewed papers in the *New England Journal of Medicine* — the first properly regulated, fully reported outcomes for this technique ([STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2025/07/16/ivf-first-births-mitochondrial-replacement-therapy-united-kingdom/), [Science Media Centre expert reactions](https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/expert-reaction-to-two-papers-on-the-use-of-mitochondrial-donation-and-preimplantation-genetic-testing-for-mitochondrial-disease-as-published-in-nejm/)).

The results, stated the way the papers state them:

- **Eight babies — four girls and four boys, including identical twins — were born to seven women** at high risk of transmitting serious mtDNA disease.
- **None has mitochondrial disease.** All were healthy at birth and developing normally at follow-up.
- **The technique is risk reduction, not elimination.** In five of the eight babies, disease-causing mtDNA was undetectable in blood. Three carried low levels — roughly 5–16% — from "carryover" of a small amount of the mother's cytoplasm during the transfer. That is below the threshold generally believed to cause disease, and the papers say so plainly, but it is not zero. Newcastle itself describes the treatment as *reducing* risk, and that honesty is worth trusting more than any headline promising a cure.

Eight babies in eight years sounds slow. It is deliberate: the UK legalized the technique by an act of Parliament in 2015, licensed exactly one clinic (Newcastle, in 2017) through its fertility regulator, the [HFEA](https://www.hfea.gov.uk/treatments/embryo-testing-and-treatments-for-disease/mitochondrial-donation-treatment/), and requires case-by-case approval for every patient.

## Why Americans can't get it

In the United States, mitochondrial donation is not "unapproved" — it is unavailable by law. Since 2015, Congress has attached a rider to FDA appropriations that prohibits the agency from even *accepting* applications for clinical research in which a human embryo is intentionally modified to include a heritable genetic modification. The FDA cannot review the science on its merits; the door is closed before the paperwork can be filed. The rider has been renewed every year since.

That is why the only earlier MRT birth involving a US doctor happened in Mexico in 2016, outside any regulatory framework. The UK's lesson cuts the other way: the technique's credibility today rests on the fact that somewhere, a regulator was allowed to supervise it.

## Australia is next: Maeve's Law and mitoHOPE

Australia is building the world's second regulated program, and 2026 is its pivotal year:

- **March 2022:** the Senate passed the Mitochondrial Donation Law Reform Act — **Maeve's Law**, named for Maeve Hood, a Victorian girl with Leigh syndrome — legalizing mitochondrial donation in a staged, research-first rollout ([Australian Department of Health](https://www.health.gov.au/our-work/mitochondrial-donation), [Mito Foundation](https://www.mito.org.au/mitochondrial-donation/)).
- **November 2025:** the Embryo Research Licensing Committee issued the program's first pre-clinical research and training licence.
- **March 2026:** the mitoHOPE program performed Australia's first maternal spindle transfer using human cells in a training setting ([Monash University](https://lens.monash.edu/from-advocacy-to-action-australia-approves-pre-clinical-training-in-mitochondrial-donation/)).
- **Late 2026 (estimate):** the clinical trial may begin, depending on licensing and ethics approvals — a timeline the program itself calls a best estimate, not a promise.

Watch the pattern: legislation first, licensing second, training third, patients last. It is the Newcastle playbook, adopted almost exactly.

## What this means if mitochondrial disease runs in your family

Practical, honest takeaways:

- **Mitochondrial donation exists, works in the reported cases, and is geographically gated.** Today that means the UK (one licensed clinic, case-by-case) and, likely from late 2026, an Australian clinical trial with its own eligibility rules.
- **It is one tool among several.** For many carriers, preimplantation genetic testing (selecting embryos with low mutation loads) is the first-line option — the second of the two NEJM papers covered exactly that pathway.

## FAQ

**Is a "three-parent baby" really from three parents?**
Genetically, barely. The mitochondrial donor contributes 37 genes — under 0.1% of the child's DNA — and none of the nuclear DNA that shapes traits. Legally, in the UK, the donor is not a parent and donates anonymously (though rules on future contact differ from egg donation).

**Is mitochondrial donation legal in the United States?**
No. A congressional appropriations rider, renewed annually since 2015, bars the FDA from accepting applications for clinical research involving heritable modification of embryos, which includes mitochondrial donation. It is not banned by a standalone statute, but the practical effect is a prohibition.

**Did the technique completely eliminate the disease risk?**
No, and the researchers don't claim it did. Five of eight babies had undetectable disease-causing mtDNA in blood; three had low levels (roughly 5–16%) from maternal carryover — below the level believed to cause disease, but not zero. The honest term is risk reduction.

**How much of a person's DNA is mitochondrial?**
37 genes out of roughly 20,000 — but they are disproportionately critical, because they run the cell's energy production. You inherit them exclusively from your mother.

**When will mitochondrial donation be available in Australia?**
The mitoHOPE program's best estimate is that a clinical trial may commence in the second half of 2026, pending licences and ethics approvals. Access will initially be through the trial, not general fertility clinics.

**Can storing my DNA today give my descendants access to this technology?**
No — and be wary of anyone who implies otherwise. Mitochondrial donation uses fresh eggs and embryos in licensed clinical settings. A stored DNA record is documentation, not treatment: valuable for family health history and future analysis, but it does not enable any reproductive procedure.
