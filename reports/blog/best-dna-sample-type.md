Here's the direct answer: **it depends on whether you're testing today or preserving for decades — and the answer flips.** For immediate lab yield, blood is the gold standard and stabilized saliva works fine. For long-horizon storage at room temperature, the ranking inverts: **keratinized samples — nail clippings and hair — are the durable ones.** Nail clippings stored dry at room temperature have produced full genetic profiles after 10-12 years; untreated saliva can become useless in about 24 hours. The dry, water-free structure that makes hair and nails "dead" tissue is exactly what protects the DNA inside them.

Let's take each sample honestly, including the caveats.

## The comparison, up front

| Sample | Nuclear DNA yield today | Untreated shelf life at room temp | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blood | Highest (the lab standard) | Days (needs cold chain or cards) | Clinical testing now |
| Saliva / buccal swab | Good | ~24 hours untreated; 1-5+ years with stabilizer buffer | Consumer testing kits |
| Hair **with root** | Good (nucleated root cells) | Years to decades, dry | Testing + storage |
| Hair shaft (rootless) | Low, fragmented — but see 2026 update | Years to decades, dry | Long storage; improving readability |
| Nail clippings | Moderate, fragmented but consistently typeable | **Decades** — 10-12 years demonstrated, dry at room temp | Long-horizon storage |

## Blood: the yield king with a logistics problem

Nobody disputes blood's lab performance — it's the reference sample in clinical genetics ([EBSCO overview of forensic DNA sources](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/applied-sciences/dna-extraction-hair-bodily-fluids-and-tissues)). The problem is everything around it: collection needs a draw, and preservation needs refrigeration, freezing, or specialized dried-blood cards. As a "keep this for 40 years in case sequencing gets better" medium, wet blood is high-maintenance. (And [cold storage has its own failure modes](/blog/how-long-does-dna-last) — freezers cycle and fail.)

## Saliva: great for testing, fragile for keeping

Consumer DNA kits use saliva because it's easy and yields solid nuclear DNA. But the honest number is stark: **untreated saliva DNA can degrade within about 24 hours at room temperature**; with a chemical stabilization buffer, validated stability extends to five-plus years ([preservation overview](https://biennialsandeducation.org/how-long-saliva-dna-last); stabilizer chemistry is a whole patent field of its own — [example](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9845489)). That's why your old spit-tube kit isn't an archive: it was engineered to survive shipping, not decades. ([If you're rethinking a testing company as a storage company, ask the custody question first](/blog/23andme-alternative).)

## Hair: the root is the point — and 2026 moved the goalposts

Two different samples hide inside "hair":

- **Hair with the root attached** carries nucleated cells — real nuclear DNA, suitable for standard typing ([Wiley forensic methods reference](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9780470027318.a1104m.pub2)). This is why collection instructions say *pull, don't trim*: a few plucked strands beat a barbershop sweep.
- **Rootless shafts** historically gave only fragmented, mostly mitochondrial DNA — fine for maternal-line work, unsuitable for most standard assays ([hair-shaft extraction literature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1875176811001600)).

The fresh development: a 2026 *Genome Biology* study reported **rootless hair as a reliable source of forensic-grade genetic information** using modern sequencing — the kind of methods leap that keeps making stored keratin samples more readable ([Genome Biology, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13059-026-03981-8) — peer-reviewed). The pattern we keep documenting: [the sample doesn't change; the machines do](/blog/how-long-does-dna-last).

Meanwhile hair's storage profile is excellent: keratin is tough, and dry shafts hold their (admittedly fragmented) DNA for years to decades.

## Nail clippings: the quiet overachiever

Nails get overlooked because they're unglamorous. The data says otherwise:

- Nail clippings stored at **room temperature for 10-12 years** have been fully STR-typed — in some decomposition studies, nails produced profiles when soft tissue and even bone had failed ([Forensic Science International](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S053151310501246X); [nail clippings as a DNA source for genetic studies](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276242546_Human_Nail_Clippings_as_a_Source_of_DNA_for_Genetic_Studies)).
- The mechanism is the same one that preserves ancient DNA: keratinization locks fragmented DNA into a **water-free matrix**, shutting down the hydrolysis and oxidation that destroy wet samples. Modern clinical work even uses nail-derived DNA as a control source in genomic studies ([Haematologica, 2024](https://haematologica.org/article/view/haematol.2024.285054)).
- Collection is the easiest of any sample: clippers you already own.

## FAQ

**What is the best DNA sample type for long-term storage?**
Dry, keratinized samples — hair and nail clippings — hold up best at room temperature over decades. Blood and saliva yield more DNA on day one but require cold storage or chemical stabilization to survive.

**Can you get DNA from fingernail clippings?**
Yes. Nail clippings contain fragmented nuclear DNA locked in keratin, and samples stored dry at room temperature for 10-12 years have produced full STR profiles.

**Does hair need the root for DNA?**
For standard nuclear DNA typing, yes — the root's nucleated cells are the classic source. Rootless shafts historically gave mainly mitochondrial DNA, though 2026 peer-reviewed methods now recover substantially more from shafts alone.

**How long does saliva DNA last?**
Roughly 24 hours untreated at room temperature; five or more years in a validated stabilization buffer. Saliva kits are engineered for shipping and testing, not decades of archiving.

**Is blood better than saliva for DNA testing?**
For maximum yield and clinical-grade work, yes. For consumer testing, stabilized saliva is usually sufficient — the differences matter more for storage than for testing.

**Why does MySpawn use hair and nails instead of saliva?**
Because the goal is a decades-long ambient-temperature archive, and keratin is the medium with demonstrated multi-year room-temperature stability, while saliva depends on buffers and blood on cold chains.
