# Can a Spouse Override a Will?

*Published: November 6, 2026 · Lane (c) legacy planning · myspawn.me/blog*

In most of the United States, yes, within limits. A surviving spouse who is left out of a will, or left very little, can usually claim a statutory minimum share of the estate instead of taking what the will says. The mechanism is called an **elective share**, sometimes a forced share. Roughly forty states have one in some form. **But the percentage is the least useful thing to know about it, because what the percentage is applied to varies more than the percentage itself.**

This article is about the three questions that actually decide the outcome. It is not legal advice, and the one instruction in it that matters is to read your own state's statute or ask someone who has.

## The right is to elect, not to rewrite

An elective share does not invalidate a will. **The will stands. The spouse is given a choice: take what the document leaves them, or take the statutory share instead.** Hence "elective".

That distinction has a practical consequence people miss. **A spouse who is left a generous bequest may have nothing to gain by electing, and a spouse who is left nothing may still have to act, in writing, within a deadline, to get anything at all.** The right is not automatic in the sense of arriving by itself. It is a claim that must be made.

It can also usually be given up. **Spouses can waive the elective share by prenuptial or postnuptial agreement** ([FindLaw overview](https://www.findlaw.com/forms/resources/estate-planning/last-will-and-testament/what-is-an-elective-share.html)). A waiver signed years ago is often the reason an expected claim goes nowhere.

## Question one: does my state have one at all

The protection is not universal and the exceptions are not small.

**Georgia has no elective share statute whatsoever**, according to a state-by-state survey of the requirements ([survey summary](https://ezel.ai/surveys/spousal-elective-share-requirements)). *Labelled: this is an aggregator's summary, not the Georgia code. Verify it in the code before relying on it.*

**Community property states generally do not recognise a surviving spouse's elective rights**, because they handle the same problem by a different route: a spouse already owns a half interest in community property during the marriage, so there is nothing to elect into. **A protection that arrives earlier is not the absence of a protection.**

So the honest first answer to "can my spouse override my will" is: **it depends on which of three regimes you are in**, and two of them are not the one most articles describe.

## Question two: a percentage of what

This is the question that decides most real cases, and it is the one that almost never appears in the headline.

**Some states apply the share to the probate estate only.** That is the property that passes under the will. **Others apply it to an augmented estate**, a larger pool constructed by statute that reaches specified non-probate transfers: certain joint accounts, payable-on-death designations, some trusts and some lifetime gifts ([Cornell Legal Information Institute on the spousal share](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/spousal_share)).

**The difference is not academic. It is usually the whole case.** A person whose assets mostly pass outside the will can leave a very small probate estate behind. In a probate-only state, a percentage of a small number is a small number. In an augmented-estate state, the same arrangement is pulled back in and counted.

One source names **Oregon as a state that limits the claim to probate assets** ([survey summary](https://ezel.ai/surveys/spousal-elective-share-requirements)). *Labelled: aggregator summary again. The point to carry is the category, not the state.*

This is the same mechanism our article on [what a transfer-on-death deed actually moves](/blog/transfer-on-death-deed-what-it-moves) describes from the other direction: non-probate transfers are not immune to everything, and statutes routinely reach them for spousal and child allowances. **Anyone planning around "it avoids probate" should know that avoiding probate and avoiding a spousal claim are different achievements.**

## Question three: how much, and we are not going to tell you a number for your state

**Here is where this article departs from most of what you will find.**

The commonly repeated range is **one-third to one-half of the estate**. The **Uniform Law Commission has suggested fifty per cent** ([Ward and Smith summary](https://www.wardandsmith.com/articles/spouses-entitlement-to-your-estate)). **Florida's statute is quoted as an amount equal to thirty per cent of the elective estate.** **West Virginia uses a sliding scale keyed to the length of the marriage**, with one reform scheme's included share starting at three per cent for a marriage of under a year ([West Virginia Code 42-3-1](https://code.wvlegislature.gov/42-3-1/)).

**Those four facts do not sit on one line, and that is the finding rather than a failure of the research.** A single number, a suggested model number, a different fixed number, and a schedule that starts at three per cent are four different answers to "how much".

**We are not naming a settled percentage for any state in this article.** Not because the numbers are secret, but because a percentage quoted without its base, its state, its version year and its sliding scale is a number that will be wrong in a predictable direction: **too high for someone in a probate-only state with a short marriage, too low for someone in an augmented-estate state.** The percentage is the most quotable part of this subject and the least portable.

**The sources also disagree with each other at a higher level.** One states the general rule as a spouse being unable to disinherit a spouse absent a waiver. The Georgia and community-property exceptions make that rule false as stated. **When a general rule and its exceptions appear on different pages, the exceptions are usually the newer information.**

## Question four, the one with a clock on it

**Elective share rights have deadlines**, generally tied to the opening of probate or the date of a notice. **We are not naming a period, because they differ by state and because a missed window is the single most common way this right disappears.**

What to take from that: **if you think you may have a claim, the question of how much comes after the question of by when.** A share you were entitled to and did not claim in time is worth the same as no share at all.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I disinherit my spouse completely?
In most states, not reliably, unless they waived the right by agreement. In community property states the question works differently because a spouse already owns a share of community property. At least one state is reported to have no elective share statute at all. **The answer depends on your state and on whether a valid waiver exists.**

### Does an elective share override a trust?
Sometimes. It depends on whether your state uses a probate-only base or an augmented estate that reaches specified non-probate transfers, including some trusts. **A trust is not automatically outside the claim, and treating it as though it were is a common and expensive assumption.**

### What is the elective share percentage in my state?
We are deliberately not publishing a per-state figure. The commonly cited range is one-third to one-half, the Uniform Law Commission has suggested fifty per cent, one state's statute is quoted at thirty per cent, and at least one uses a sliding scale by length of marriage. **A percentage without its base and its state will mislead you. Read your state's statute.**

### Does a prenuptial agreement stop it?
Usually yes, if valid. Waiver by prenuptial or postnuptial agreement is the standard route. Validity is its own question and is where these disputes are actually fought.

### My spouse left me out of the will. What do I do first?
Find out whether your state has an elective share, what it is applied to, and what the deadline is. **Do the deadline first.** Then speak to a probate attorney in that state.

### Does this apply to unmarried partners?
No. The elective share is a protection attached to marriage. **Unmarried partners generally have no equivalent statutory claim**, which is why planning documents matter far more in that situation, not less.

## What this comes down to

"Can a spouse override a will" is a question with a yes attached to three conditions. **Does your state have the right, what pool does it apply to, and was it claimed in time.** The percentage everybody quotes is the fourth thing that matters, not the first.
