# How to Record a Family Health History (and What Doctors Actually Do With It)

**A family health history is a written record of the diseases in your blood relatives, who had them, and at what age. It is free, it takes one conversation, and it changes real clinical decisions: a first-degree relative diagnosed with colorectal cancer before 60 moves your own first colonoscopy to age 40, or ten years before their diagnosis, whichever comes first. Fewer than half of Americans have ever actually collected one. Here is how to do it properly in an afternoon, what to ask, where to store it, and an honest comparison with what a DNA test can and cannot tell you.**

## Why this is worth an afternoon

The value is not sentimental. A family health history is one of the few pieces of information that changes what a doctor *does*, rather than what they know.

The CDC is direct about it: having a family health history of a chronic disease such as cancer makes you more likely to get that disease, and knowing it helps your provider decide **what screening tests you need and when** ([CDC, Family Health History and Adults](https://www.cdc.gov/family-health-history/family-health-history-and-you/family-health-history-and-adults.html)). Screening earlier is the entire mechanism. Nothing about the history changes your biology; it changes the calendar.

And the window to collect it closes. The people who remember what your grandfather died of, and how old he was, are a finite resource. This is the one item on any legacy checklist with a genuine deadline attached, which is why it outranks most of what we write about.

## What doctors actually change because of it

Concrete examples, all from current professional guidance:

**Colorectal cancer.** If you have a first-degree relative diagnosed with colorectal cancer or advanced polyps before age 60, or two or more first-degree relatives diagnosed at any age, American College of Gastroenterology guidance is to begin screening at **age 40, or ten years before the youngest affected relative's diagnosis, whichever comes first** ([ACG colorectal screening guidelines, summarised by AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2022/0300/p327.html)). That is potentially a fifteen-year head start, decided entirely by a fact you either know or do not.

**Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer.** NCCN criteria route people toward *BRCA1/BRCA2* genetic testing substantially on the basis of family pattern — who was affected, how young, and on which side. Family history is the gate that gets you the genetic test, not the other way round.

**Lynch syndrome.** For people with confirmed high-risk variants, colonoscopy every one to three years starting at age 30-35, or ten years before the earliest family diagnosis. Again the family's ages at diagnosis set the schedule.

**Prostate cancer.** Referral criteria consider a first-degree relative with prostate cancer alongside close relatives with triple-negative or male breast cancer — a pattern only a recorded history reveals, because those look like unrelated cancers to a family telling stories informally.

The through-line: **ages matter as much as diagnoses.** "My dad had colon cancer" is a story. "My dad was diagnosed with colon cancer at 52" is a clinical instruction.

## Family health history versus a DNA test: the honest comparison

This is where most articles overclaim in one direction or the other, so here is the actual state of the evidence.

**Where family history wins.** A Cleveland Clinic team led by Charis Eng compared thorough family-history assessment against a direct-to-consumer genomic screening tool in 44 people for breast, prostate and colorectal cancer risk. The family history performed better, and the consumer genomic screen **missed nine people who were at high risk for colon cancer on family-history grounds** ([reported by ABC News](https://abcnews.go.com/Health/Wellness/family-history-predictor-disease-risk-genomic-screening/story?id=12055615)). Small study, and worth reading as a caution about consumer screening rather than a verdict on genomics.

**Where it is more even, and where it loses.** A peer-reviewed comparison in *PLOS Genetics* found family history is most useful for **common, heritable conditions** — for something like coronary artery disease it explains roughly **20-30% of disease heritability, comparable to the best SNP models**. But for **less common conditions** such as Crohn's disease, family history accounts for **under 4% of heritability**, and genetic markers outperform it in almost every such case ([Do et al., *PLOS Genetics*, peer-reviewed](https://journals.plos.org/plosgenetics/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pgen.1002973)).

So the honest summary is not "family history beats DNA tests." It is:

- For the common conditions that kill most people, a good family history is **competitive with genomic prediction and free**.
- For rarer conditions, genetic data does things family history cannot.
- Family history is what gets you *referred* for the genetic testing that actually matters clinically.
- Neither is a diagnosis. Both shift probabilities.

## How to record one, step by step

### Go three generations deep

Minimum useful scope: **you, your siblings and children; your parents, aunts and uncles; your grandparents.** Add half-siblings and note which side. If you are adopted or donor-conceived, record what you can and mark the gaps explicitly — a documented gap is still information a doctor can use.

### Ask these seven things about each person

1. Are they living? If not, **what did they die of, and at what age**?
2. What conditions have they been diagnosed with — and **at what age for each**?
3. Any cancer? Which organ specifically, not just "cancer"?
4. Heart disease, stroke, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes?
5. Any mental-health or neurological diagnoses?
6. Pregnancy losses, birth defects, or children who died young?
7. Ancestry and country of origin — some conditions cluster in specific populations.

**The ages are the part people skip and the part doctors need.** If someone cannot remember exactly, record the decade and mark it as approximate rather than leaving it blank.

### Do it on a day people are already together

The CDC and NHGRI have run Thanksgiving as National Family History Day precisely because the logistics solve themselves. Any gathering works. Ask one person at a time rather than the whole table — people are more precise one-on-one, and the family-mythology version gets corrected faster.

Record it. A voice memo on your phone is fine and is far better than trusting your memory of the conversation. If a relative is willing, a short video of them describing their own history is worth more later than the transcript, for reasons that have nothing to do with medicine.

### Put it somewhere both a doctor and your family can reach

The free **My Family Health Portrait** tool, developed by the CDC with the National Human Genome Research Institute and partners, organises the information and lets you share it with your provider and relatives ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/family-health-history/about/index.html); [NHGRI](https://www.genome.gov/news/news-release/Announcing-a-New-CDC-Home-for-the-US-Surgeon-Generals-Family-Health-History-Tool)). A plain document works too. What matters is that it is written, shareable, and that **someone other than you knows it exists** — the most common way an archive fails is not decay, it is that the one person who knew about it is gone.

Update it when someone gets a new diagnosis. A history recorded once at 35 is a snapshot, not a record.

## FAQ

### How far back should a family health history go?
Three generations is the practical standard: your generation, your parents' generation including aunts and uncles, and your grandparents. Going further rarely changes a clinical decision, because the information gets less reliable faster than it gets more useful.

### What if I'm adopted and don't know my biological family?
Record what you have and mark the gaps explicitly. "Adopted, biological family history unknown" is itself clinically meaningful — it tells a provider they cannot rely on family-history-based risk stratification for you, which sometimes shifts them toward earlier baseline screening or genetic testing instead.

### Is a DNA test a substitute for family health history?
No, and for common conditions the family history is often the stronger tool. It is also usually what gets you referred for clinically meaningful genetic testing. The two answer different questions and genetic data outperforms family history mainly for less common conditions.

### What's the single most important thing to record?
Ages at diagnosis. Most screening guidelines that change because of family history are triggered by how young a relative was, not merely by the fact that they were affected.

### Where should I store it so it's actually useful?
Somewhere shareable with a clinician and known to at least one other family member. The CDC's free My Family Health Portrait tool is built for exactly this. A plain document is fine. A file only you know about is not.

### How often should I update it?
Whenever anyone in the recorded group receives a significant new diagnosis, and otherwise every few years. New diagnoses in relatives can change your own screening schedule.
