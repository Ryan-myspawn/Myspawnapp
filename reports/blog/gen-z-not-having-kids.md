# Why Gen Z Isn't Having Kids (and What They're Preserving Instead)

*Published: August 23, 2026 · Lane (e) audience angles, founder standing keyword · myspawn.me/blog*

The short answer, straight from the surveys: Gen Z isn't refusing children so much as postponing them under pressure. Around 30 percent of Gen Z and millennial adults say they don't have and don't want kids, but a larger group, 44 percent, say they want children within five years. The blockers they name are concrete: 84 percent cite money as a key factor, and raising a child in the US now runs $20,000 to $26,000 a year. Meanwhile Americans still call 2.7 children "ideal" while the actual birth rate sits near 1.6, the widest gap between wanting and having since the early 1970s. This article walks through the real numbers, what they do and don't mean, and the option-preserving moves people are making while they decide.

## What the surveys actually say

Cut through the culture war and the data is surprisingly consistent:

- **"Never" is a minority position.** Independent Center polling of Gen Z and millennials found 30 percent neither have nor want children, while **44 percent want children within the next five years** ([Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/gen-z-millennials-birth-rate-fears-2093678)). The largest camp is "yes, but not under these conditions."
- **Money is the stated reason, overwhelmingly.** A Securian Financial survey found **84 percent** cite finances as a key influence, and 68 percent say the cost of raising children specifically makes parenthood less likely. MassMutual polling found nearly **1 in 4** millennials and Gen Zers saying they won't have kids for financial reasons ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/millennials-gen-z-childless-money-finances-massmutual/)).
- **The mood is heavy.** In Care.com's perception-of-parenting survey, **52 percent** feel pessimistic about raising kids in the US today; only 17 percent feel optimistic ([Care.com](https://www.care.com/c/perception-of-parenting-survey/)).
- **The costs are real, not vibes.** Current estimates put raising a child at **$20,000 to $26,000 per year**, comfortably past $300,000 to age 18 ([BadCredit.org study roundup](https://www.badcredit.org/studies/plans-to-have-children-in-america/)).

## The gap nobody talks about: wanting more than they'll have

Here is the most important and least quoted finding in the whole debate. Americans, Gen Z included, still describe the **ideal family as 2.7 children** on average ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/694640/americans-ideal-family-size-remains-above-two-children.aspx)). The actual US fertility rate is about **1.6 births per woman**. The Institute for Family Studies' 2026 State of Fertility report calls the distance between those numbers the widest since the early 1970s, approaching Depression-era territory ([IFS](https://ifstudies.org/report-brief/the-demographic-dead-end-2026-state-of-fertility-report)).

And it's global. The UN Population Fund's 2026 Demographic Futures survey of **108,926 adults aged 18 to 39 across 73 countries** found the same pattern everywhere: **81 percent** name financial security as a precondition they don't yet have, and **57 percent** point to economic pressure and housing costs as the main obstacle ([UNFPA](https://www.unfpa.org/publications/lives-choices-and-futures-demographic-futures-survey)).

Read plainly: this is not a generation that stopped wanting families. It is a generation doing cost-benefit analysis out loud on a decision every previous generation made mostly on autopilot, and answering "not yet" far more often than "never." Fertility researchers now write about the desired-versus-actual family size gap as a clinical topic in its own right ([Fertility and Sterility, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0015028225019090)).

## Why both culture-war stories get it wrong

The pronatalist reading says Gen Z is selfish; the anti-natalist reading says Gen Z has been liberated from wanting kids. The surveys support neither. Deferral under uncertainty is the story: housing, wages, childcare math, and a 52-percent-pessimism climate. Judging either choice misses the practical problem that deferral creates, which is the next section, and it's the part almost nobody puts in writing.

## Deferral has a biology problem (and a memory problem)

You can postpone a decision indefinitely. Two things do not postpone with you:

1. **Biology.** Fertility declines with age for everyone. That is why egg and sperm freezing exist, why fertility preservation went from taboo to employer benefit in a decade, and why the medical field treats preservation before risk as standard planning. Those are clinical decisions with real costs, made with a doctor, and this is the honest boundary: **DNA banking is not fertility preservation.** A stored DNA sample cannot be turned into a child, and any company that hints otherwise is selling science fiction. ## FAQ

**Is Gen Z really not having kids?**
Mostly they're delaying. Polling shows about 30 percent of Gen Z and millennials don't want children, while 44 percent want them within five years. The US fertility rate (~1.6) sits far below the family size Americans still call ideal (2.7), the widest such gap since the early 1970s.

**Why is Gen Z not having children?**
The dominant stated reasons are financial: 84 percent cite money as a key influence, with housing, childcare costs (over $20,000 a year per child), and general pessimism (52 percent negative on raising kids in the US) leading the list.

**Do people end up with fewer children than they wanted?**
Yes, on average. Demographers document a persistent gap between intended and completed family size in the US and Europe, and the 2026 data shows it widening. Deferral plus biology is the usual mechanism.

**Does freezing eggs or sperm keep the option open?**
It can help and is the established medical route, but it's a clinical decision with real costs and no guarantees; talk to a fertility clinic. DNA banking is a different, non-clinical layer: it preserves your biological record, not your fertility.

**What can I do now if I'm undecided about kids?**
Three decision-neutral moves: record the people you love (video beats everything), preserve your DNA as the family's reference record, and get basic estate documents in place. None of them commits you to a choice; all of them are hard to do later.
