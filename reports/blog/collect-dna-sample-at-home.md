Collecting DNA at home takes about seven minutes and almost always works, because the failure modes are few and boring. **Use a clean, dry mouth for a buccal swab or pull hair with the root attached; air-dry everything completely before it goes anywhere; store and ship in paper, never plastic; keep it cool and dry; and don't touch the business end.** Those five rules cover nearly every ruined sample. Everything below is the detail behind them.

## First, what you are actually collecting

Different sources carry different amounts of usable DNA, and they behave differently in storage.

**Buccal (cheek) swab.** Cells from the inside of your cheek. Standard for consumer and paternity testing because it is painless and yields plenty of nuclear DNA. Swab DNA stays viable for months when dried and stored in a paper envelope in a cool, dry place ([DDC collection instructions](https://dnacenter.com/buccal-swab-test-instructions/)).

**Hair with the root attached.** The follicle, not the strand, is where the DNA is. Cut hair is largely useless for nuclear DNA; **plucked** hair with a visible white bulb is not. Comparative work has found hair follicles hold up better than buccal swabs over time, with greater deterioration observed in swabs — a useful reason to collect both if you can.

**Nail clippings.** Lower yield than the other two but genuinely durable, and they need no special technique. A reasonable third sample rather than a first choice.

Our own kit uses hair and nails specifically because both tolerate ambient storage well and neither requires a lab appointment. If you want the full comparison, see [which DNA sample type is best](/blog/best-dna-sample-type).

## The collection itself, step by step

### For a buccal swab
1. **Do not eat, drink, smoke or chew gum for at least 30 minutes beforehand.** Food debris and bacteria in the mouth are the most common contaminant.
2. Rinse with water, then wait a few minutes so the mouth is clean but not wet.
3. **Hold the swab by the handle only.** Never touch the tip, and never set it down on a counter.
4. Rub firmly against the inside of one cheek for 30 to 60 seconds, rotating the swab so every side makes contact. Pressure matters more than time — you are scraping cells loose, not moistening the swab.
5. Repeat with a fresh swab on the other cheek.

### For hair
1. Pull, do not cut. Grip close to the scalp and pull firmly in one motion.
2. Look for the **bulb** at the end. A visible white or translucent root means you have DNA. No bulb, no sample.
3. Collect several strands, since not every pull brings a good root.
4. Handle by the shaft, not the root end.

### For nails
1. Wash and dry hands first, then clip.
2. Avoid nail polish, glue-on nails and heavy hand cream residue.
3. Larger clippings beat slivers.

## The five things that actually ruin a sample

### 1. Moisture
The single biggest killer. Damp samples grow mold and bacteria, and bacterial activity degrades DNA fast. **Air-dry everything completely before packaging** — swabs upright, not lying on a surface, for at least the time your kit specifies.

### 2. Plastic
Counterintuitive, and worth internalizing: paper "breathes" and lets residual moisture escape, while a sealed plastic bag traps it and becomes a mold incubator. Collection guidance is consistent on this — **store and ship in paper envelopes, never plastic bags.** If your kit ships with a plastic sleeve, it is for the outer packaging, not for the sample itself.

### 3. Contamination from you (or lunch)
Touching a swab tip transfers your skin cells and oils. Eating beforehand adds food and bacteria. Sharing a work surface with another person's sample creates mixtures that can be unusable. One sample, one clean surface, one pair of hands.

### 4. Heat and light
Cool and dry is the whole instruction. A glovebox in summer, a windowsill, or a mail satchel left in the sun are all worse than a drawer indoors. This is also why serious storage is not a shoebox in the attic: see [how long DNA actually lasts](/blog/how-long-does-dna-last).

### 5. Delay
Every extra day in an unstabilized envelope is a day of slow degradation. Collect, dry, seal, and send the same week. The sample's clock starts the moment it leaves your body, not the moment somebody remembers to mail it.

## FAQ

**Can I use cut hair for DNA?**
Generally no. Nuclear DNA lives in the follicle, so hair must be **plucked with the root bulb attached**. A cut strand carries little usable nuclear DNA, though specialized labs can sometimes recover mitochondrial DNA from the shaft.

**Why paper envelopes instead of plastic bags?**
Paper allows residual moisture to escape; plastic traps it and encourages mold and bacterial growth, which degrades DNA. Collection guidance from testing providers is consistent on this point.

**How long does a home-collected sample last before it's processed?**
A properly dried buccal swab in a paper envelope in a cool, dry place stays viable for months; hair follicles typically hold up longer. That is a window, not a plan — send it the same week.

**Does eating before a cheek swab really matter?**
Yes. Food particles and bacteria are the most common contaminants in home collection. Wait at least 30 minutes after eating, drinking, smoking or chewing gum.

**Can I collect a sample from someone else, like an elderly parent?**
Yes, with their informed consent, and it is one of the most worthwhile things you can do this year. Use fresh swabs, a clean surface, and keep samples separately labeled so they never mix.

**Do I need to freeze the sample at home?**
No. For dried swabs, hair and nails, cool and dry beats cold and damp. Freezing a moist sample is worse than drying it properly and keeping it at room temperature.
