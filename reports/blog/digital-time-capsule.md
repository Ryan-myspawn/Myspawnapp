Here's the direct answer: a digital time capsule that will actually open in 50 years needs four things, and none of them is a special gadget. Boring, ubiquitous file formats (JPEG, PDF, MP4). Three copies on two different kinds of storage, because every physical medium dies. A named human custodian with delivery instructions, because most capsules are lost, not corrupted. And, the step everyone skips, a refresh ritual: once every decade or so, the files get copied forward onto whatever storage is current. No medium survives 50 years reliably. The cycle does. Everything below is the evidence and the build plan.

## Why "just burn it to a disc" fails the test

The numbers on optical discs are a masterclass in reading storage claims. Manufacturers advertise 100 to 200 years for recordable CDs and DVDs under ideal conditions. The Library of Congress and NIST, who actually accelerated-aged these discs, found five of seven CD-R products tested had estimated life expectancies over 45 years, with the other two landing at 30 to 45 years ([LoC preservation research](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/scientists/projects/cd-r_dvd-r_rw_longevity.html), [NIST/LC longevity study](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/resources/rt/NIST_LC_OpticalDiscLongevity.pdf)). The U.S. National Archives, planning for worst cases, uses 10 to 25 years, with conservative estimates as low as 2 to 5 for ordinary discs in ordinary closets.

Three institutions, three answers, and the spread IS the lesson: real-world longevity depends on the specific product, the storage environment, and luck. "Disc rot" (oxidation and hydrolysis of the recording layer) is real and invisible until playback fails. And even a perfect disc needs a working drive, which is already vanishing from laptops. A single copy on any single medium is a bet, not a plan.

## The format rule: boring beats best

File formats die faster than media. Flash is gone; countless proprietary formats from the 1990s are effectively unreadable today. The formats that survive are the ones that are everywhere, openly documented, and boring:

- **Photos: JPEG** (plus the original camera file if you like, but always a JPEG copy).
- **Documents: PDF, ideally PDF/A** (the archival profile), plus plain text for anything that matters most. Plain text is the cockroach of file formats, in the best way.
- **Video: MP4 with H.264.** The most widely supported video encoding in history; the format a 2076 tool is most likely to still ingest or migrate.
- **Audio: MP3 or WAV.**

The heuristic: if a format needs one specific company's software to open, it fails the 50-year test today, no aging required.

## The storage math: 3-2-1, honestly applied

The archivist's 3-2-1 rule: three copies, on two different types of storage, one of them offsite. For a family capsule in 2026 that translates to:

1. **A local drive** (external SSD or hard drive). Honest lifespan: hard drives commonly fail in 3 to 8 years; treat any drive as temporary housing, not a vault.
2. **A mainstream cloud account** (the offsite copy), with legacy access configured, Apple Legacy Contact or Google Inactive Account Manager, so the account outlives its owner administratively (setup covered in our [digital legacy guide](/blog/digital-legacy-what-happens)).
3. **One archival-grade physical copy if you want belt-and-suspenders**: a quality optical disc or an "archival" disc stored cool, dark, and dry. Treat vendor century claims as marketing until a third party ages them; the LoC numbers above are the honest ceiling to plan around.

And the rule that makes all of this work: **no copy is permanent, so the capsule lives by migration.** Once a decade, copy everything forward to current storage, verify a few files open, and update the index. One hour per decade is the entire maintenance budget.

## The custody layer: where digital capsules actually die

Media failure gets the headlines; custody failure gets the capsules. A drive nobody knows about is indistinguishable from no drive. The fix is the same one we prescribed for [physical time capsules](/blog/family-time-capsule-ideas): a named keeper, written into estate documents, holding a one-page index (what exists, where each copy lives, what format, when it was last migrated) and a trigger tied to an event rather than to memory.

## What a complete digital capsule contains

- The five-question family interview video (MP4): the single highest-value file.
- A curated photo folder, 100 to 500 images with real filenames ("1998_grandpa_lakehouse.jpg" beats "IMG_4471.jpg" forever).
- Scanned documents: the recipe card, the naturalization papers, the handwritten anything (600 DPI TIFF masters, JPEG copies).
- A plain-text index and a plain-text family tree: names, dates, relationships. The lowest-tech file is the skeleton key for everything else.
- One "state of the world" note per person, dated.

And a boundary worth naming: a digital capsule preserves what people made and said. ## The 50-year test, as a checklist

1. Could a stranger in 2076 identify your formats? (JPEG/PDF/MP4/plain text = yes.)
2. Do three copies exist on two media types, one offsite?
3. Is legacy access configured on the cloud copy?
4. Does one named person hold the index and the trigger?
5. Is the next migration year written down?

Five yeses and your capsule is more durable than most institutions' archives.

## FAQ

**How long do CDs and DVDs really last?**
Depends who you ask: manufacturers claim 100-200 years; Library of Congress testing put most quality CD-Rs over 45 years; the National Archives plans around 10-25 years, with worst cases at 2-5. Plan to the conservative number and migrate.

**What are the best file formats for a digital time capsule?**
JPEG for photos, PDF (ideally PDF/A) plus plain text for documents, MP4/H.264 for video, MP3 or WAV for audio. Ubiquity and open documentation beat technical superiority.

**Is cloud storage safe for 50 years?**
No single service should be trusted for 50 years; services shut down and accounts lapse. Cloud works as ONE of three copies, with legacy access configured, inside a migration cycle.

**How often should I refresh a digital archive?**
About once a decade: copy everything to current storage, spot-check that files open, update the index. That single ritual defeats both media rot and format extinction.

**Are digital time capsule apps reliable?**
Judge any service, including milestone-unlock vaults like ours, by its custody answers in writing: who holds the data, what happens in a shutdown, how delivery is triggered. Keep an independent copy regardless.
