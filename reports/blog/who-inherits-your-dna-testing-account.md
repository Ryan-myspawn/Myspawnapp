# Who Inherits Your DNA Testing Account?

**It depends on which company you tested with, and the four largest do four different things. Ancestry is reported to let you name a free legacy contact who, on proof of death, receives the account and can sign in to it. FamilyTreeDNA has long allowed a named beneficiary who can take control and order further tests on your stored sample. MyHeritage and 23andMe are reported to have no advance designation at all, so your family must apply afterwards with a death certificate and proof of being your heir or executor. There is no industry standard and no rule that any of them must tell you which model you signed up for.**

If you tested with more than one company, you have more than one answer, and you almost certainly have not checked.

**Epistemic note, stated up front.** Everything below about specific company policies was **read through a search index rather than opened at the companies' own domains**, including one company's own support article that appeared in results. **Treat every company-specific statement here as reported rather than verified, and check the current setting inside your own account.** Policies here moved during 2026 and are still moving. This is not legal advice.

## Four companies, four different mechanisms

**Ancestry** is reported to have added a Legacy Contact feature in Account Settings, free to all members, as part of terms-of-service updates effective 12 May 2026. You name one person, who must be at least 18 and does not need an Ancestry account of their own. After you die, that person notifies Ancestry and supplies a death certificate or a published obituary, and **Ancestry transfers the account to them**. The reporting is specific about what "transfer" means here: the legacy contact can **sign in**, change the username, password and email address, and edit the public profile. ([Ancestry support article](https://support.ancestry.com/s/article/Ancestry-Legacy-Contact?language=en_US), [summary of the 2026 terms changes](https://genealogybargains.com/ancestry-terms-of-service-changes-2026/).)

**FamilyTreeDNA** has allowed a named beneficiary for years. That person can contact the company after your death to take control of the account, **including the right to order additional tests on the DNA sample the company still holds**. With nobody designated, heirs can approach with confirming details and access may be granted at the company's discretion. ([The DNA Geek](https://thednageek.com/do-you-have-a-legacy-plan-for-your-dna/), [Your DNA Guide](https://www.yourdnaguide.com/ydgblog/add-beneficiary-dna-test).)

**MyHeritage** is reported to have no advance mechanism. Transfer happens after the fact, and where the account includes a DNA kit the company asks for proof that you are the legal heir, such as a marriage or heirship certificate, plus the death certificate and your photo ID. ([MyHeritage help article](https://www.myheritage.com/help/en/articles/15116690-how-to-transfer-account-ownership-or-research-on-myheritage).)

**23andMe** is reported to have no beneficiary program either. The widely repeated guidance from genealogy writers is that if you want someone to have your results, **you give them your login credentials while you are alive**. After death, the company will reportedly release genetic data to an executor, personal representative or beneficiary who submits proper legal documentation. ([The Legal Genealogist](https://www.legalgenealogist.com/?p=14546), [Your DNA Guide](https://www.yourdnaguide.com/ydgblog/add-beneficiary-dna-test).)

**One conflict worth seeing rather than smoothing over.** Several well-regarded genealogy sources state plainly that **FamilyTreeDNA is the only company that lets you name a beneficiary**. Ancestry's 2026 feature appears to contradict that, and the likeliest explanation is that the advice predates the feature. **The lesson is not which source is right. It is that guidance here goes stale inside a year**, so a checklist you read in 2024 is not one you can rely on now.

## "Legacy contact" is a product name, not a job title

This is the part that catches people out, and it is the same trap we described in our guide to [platform legacy settings](/blog/platform-legacy-settings-explained).

"Legacy contact" is not defined anywhere in law. It is a feature name, and each company decides what it buys. The gap is not subtle. **On Facebook, a legacy contact explicitly cannot log in as you and cannot read your private messages**; they manage a memorialized profile from the outside. ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/social-media/facebook-legacy-contact-runs-your-profile-after-you-die-n305086), [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/control-what-happens-to-your-facebook-after-you-die).) **On Ancestry, the reported model is the opposite: the contact gets the account.**

Same two words. One is custodianship from the outside. The other is a handover of the keys.

"Digital executor" has the same problem from the other direction. Estate planners use the phrase, but in most places it is **not a separate legal office**. What exists is a fiduciary, named in your will or trust, whose authority over accounts runs through your state's version of RUFADAA. We explained that machinery, including why an online tool you set up beats your will, in [what happens to your digital life when you die](/blog/digital-legacy-what-happens). Nothing here changes that hierarchy. It shows how differently companies fill the top tier of it.

## What you are actually handing over is not a photo album

Here is the part that makes a genetics account different in kind from a social account, and it is the reason this is worth twenty minutes of your Saturday.

Inherit a Facebook account and you inherit someone's past. Inherit a DNA testing account and you inherit **a live, re-queryable position inside a database of living people**. A match list is not a record of what someone did. It names cousins, half-siblings and parents who are alive right now, who tested for their own reasons, and **who never agreed to be visible to whoever you happened to name in a settings menu.**

This is not a worry invented by privacy writers. It is a working professional standard: guidance for certified genealogists holds that **using details from a living person's match list requires their informed consent even when you manage the kit**. ([Board for Certification of Genealogists](https://bcgcertification.org/?p=33762).) Relative-matching is opt-in at the major companies for the same reason. ([GEDmatch privacy checklist](https://www.gedmatch.com/blog/dna-privacy-settings-essential-checklist-for-genealogists/), [Ancestry Privacy Center](https://www.ancestry.com/c/privacy-center).)

So the question about your designee is not only "do I trust this person with my results". It is **"do I trust this person with other people's results"**, because that is a material part of what is in the box.

## The instruction that should bother you

Of everything above, the detail worth sitting with is that the standard workaround at a company with no beneficiary feature is **share your password**.

It is not malicious advice. It is what is left when a product has no handover mechanism. But it asks you to do the one thing the rest of your financial and digital life tells you never to do, it leaves no record of who received what, no way to revoke access if a relationship changes, and it usually breaks the moment two-factor authentication meets a new device. **A password is not a succession plan. It is a plan to be lucky.**

Where that is genuinely the only option, put the instruction in your estate documents rather than a text message: name the account, name who should receive it, and state explicitly that you consent to the fiduciary accessing it. That is the step RUFADAA actually reads.

## Twenty minutes, this weekend

1. **List every company holding a sample or result for you.** Most people under-count by one, usually a kit bought as a gift years ago.
2. **Open each account's settings and look for a legacy contact or beneficiary field.** If it exists, fill it in. If it does not, write down that it does not.
3. **Decide the matching question deliberately**, not by default. Leaving relative-matching on is fine. Leaving it on without ever having thought about it is the thing to fix.
4. **Put the list where your executor will find it**, account names but not passwords. Being findable beats being clever, the same point we made about [what happens when a DNA company shuts down](/blog/what-happens-dna-company-shuts-down).
5. **Tell the person you named.** A designation nobody knows about is a designation nobody will use.

## FAQ

### Does my will override my DNA company's legacy contact setting?
Usually not. Under most states' version of RUFADAA, an **online tool** you completed inside the service takes priority over instructions in a will. If the two disagree, the setting generally wins, which is why filling in the setting matters more than most people expect. See [what happens to your digital life when you die](/blog/digital-legacy-what-happens).

### Can my family just use my login after I die?
Technically it often works and it is widely suggested, but it is fragile and it is not a plan. Two-factor authentication on a new device commonly breaks it, nothing documents who was meant to receive access, and there is no way to revoke it. Where a company offers a real beneficiary field, use that instead.

### Is a digital executor a legal role?
In most places, no. It is a convenient phrase for the person you name in your estate documents to handle accounts. The authority that actually operates is fiduciary authority under your state's digital-assets law, which is why the wording in the documents matters more than the job title.

### What happens to the physical DNA sample, not just the account?
A separate question, governed by the company's retention policy rather than by who holds the login. At least one company's beneficiary can order **new tests** on the stored sample, which is a larger power than reading old results. See [how long labs keep your DNA sample](/blog/how-long-labs-keep-dna-sample) and, on the ownership question underneath it, [who owns your DNA after you die](/blog/who-owns-your-dna-after-you-die).

### Does whoever inherits my account see my DNA matches?
If relative matching is switched on and they have account access, generally yes, including matches who are living people. Professional genealogy standards treat those details as requiring the consent of the living person concerned before they are disclosed further. Decide the matching setting deliberately.
