Private cord blood banking costs roughly **$1,500 to $3,000 upfront plus $175 to $250 per year**, and the two major US medical bodies do not recommend it for most families. The main alternatives are **public donation** (free, but the unit becomes available to anyone who needs it), **sibling-directed banking** when a family member has a diagnosed condition, **cord tissue banking** (still largely experimental), and **preserving information rather than cells** through a documented family health history or a stored DNA record. Those options do different jobs, and only one of them is a transplant-ready cell product.

## What private cord blood banking actually costs in 2026

Published 2026 pricing across the major providers:

- **Collection, kit, shipping, and processing:** about **$1,500 to $3,000** ([CBR](https://www.cordblood.com/blog/cord-blood-banking-cost), [Americord](https://www.americordblood.com/articles/cost-guide-cord-blood-and-tissue-banking-explained)).
- **Annual storage:** roughly **$175 to $250** per year, with some banks ranging **$165 to $330** ([MiracleCord](https://miraclecord.com/cord-blood-banking-cost/)).
- **Prepaid options:** StemCyte publishes $795 processing plus $200/year, an 18-year prepaid plan at $3,630, or lifetime prepaid at $5,695 ([StemCyte](https://stemcyte.com/cord-blood-banking-cost/)).
- **Total over 18 years:** commonly **$5,000 to $10,000** depending on plan and provider.

Entry promotions exist (one bank advertises $710 to start with a $199 annual fee), so the sticker you see first is rarely the number you pay across two decades. Ask for the 18-year total in writing.

## What the medical societies say, in their own words

This is the part sales material tends to compress, so here it is straight.

The **American Academy of Pediatrics** does not recommend that parents routinely store their infant's cord blood for future use, unless there is an immediate medically indicated use for a sibling ([AAP policy, *Pediatrics*](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/140/5/e20172695/37866/Cord-Blood-Banking-for-Potential-Future)).

The **American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists** advises that patients asking about cord blood banking be told there is approximately a **1 in 2,700 chance** that an autologous unit (the baby's own) will be used for that child or a family member, and that clinicians provide balanced information on public versus private banking ([ACOG Committee Opinion](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2019/03/umbilical-cord-blood-banking)).

There is also a biological limit worth understanding before you spend anything: **a child's own cord blood generally cannot be used to treat a genetic disease or a leukemia in that same child**, because the stored cells carry the same genetic variant or the same premalignant cells that caused the condition. That is why sibling-directed banking, where the cells go to a *different* family member, is the case both societies treat most favorably.

None of this means cord blood is useless. Cord blood transplants are real, established medicine for a defined set of blood and immune disorders. It means the private, just-in-case version is a low-probability purchase, and you deserve to make it knowing the probability.

## The alternatives, ranked by what they actually give you

### 1. Public donation (free)

You donate the cord blood to a public bank; it is typed, listed, and made available to any matched patient who needs it. Cost to you: nothing. What you give up: exclusive claim on that unit.

Public banking has the strongest public-health case of any option here, and it is the one most families never hear about because nobody profits from telling them. Availability depends on your delivery hospital participating.

### 2. Sibling-directed banking (medically indicated)

If a sibling has a diagnosed condition treatable by a cord blood transplant, directed banking is the scenario the AAP explicitly carves out. Many banks offer free or reduced-cost programs in this case. This is a clinical decision made with your hematologist, not a marketing decision.

### 3. Cord tissue banking (label it experimental)

Increasingly upsold alongside cord blood, cord tissue preserves mesenchymal stromal cells. Be precise about status: these are the subject of active clinical research, not established standard-of-care therapy for approved indications. If a bank's pitch for cord tissue sounds more confident than the published evidence, that gap is the product.

### 4. Preserving information instead of cells

Everything above stores *living cells* for potential treatment. A different category stores *information*, which is cheaper, has no expiry driven by cell viability, and answers a different question.

### 5. Doing nothing (a legitimate answer)

Given a 1-in-2,700 autologous-use figure and an AAP recommendation against routine private storage, "we donated publicly and put the money into a 529" is a defensible, informed decision. It is not the answer a bank's brochure will give you, which is exactly why it belongs on the list.

## How to decide in one sitting

1. **Ask your OB whether your hospital supports public donation.** If yes, that is the default worth beating.
2. **Ask whether anyone in the family has a condition treatable by transplant.** If yes, talk to a hematologist about directed banking today, not to a call center.
3. **If you are still considering private banking, get the 18-year total cost in writing**, including annual increases, and ask what happens to your unit if the company is acquired or closes.
4. **Do the free things regardless:** write the family health history, and record the grandparents while they can still answer questions. See [what to leave your grandchildren](/blog/what-to-leave-grandchildren).
5. **Then, if a preserved biological record matters to you for its own sake**, price it as its own decision rather than as a cord blood substitute.

## FAQ

**Is private cord blood banking worth the money?**
For most families, the major medical societies say no. The AAP does not recommend routine private storage absent a medically indicated sibling use, and ACOG cites roughly a 1 in 2,700 chance of autologous use. It can be worth it in specific clinical situations, which a hematologist should define.

**Can my child's own cord blood treat their own genetic disease?**
Generally no. The stored cells carry the same genetic variant, and in leukemia they may carry the same premalignant cells. This is the single most misunderstood fact in the category.

**What is the cheapest alternative to private cord blood banking?**
Public donation, which is free if your hospital participates. Writing a family health history is also free and useful to every clinician your child will ever see.

**Is cord tissue banking established medicine?**
Not yet for most advertised uses. Mesenchymal stromal cell applications are an active research area; treat confident marketing claims as ahead of the published evidence.

**Is storing DNA the same as banking cord blood?**
No. Cord blood preserves living, transplantable stem cells. A DNA record preserves genetic information and cannot be transplanted. They solve different problems, and any provider implying otherwise, including in our category, is overselling.

**What happens to my cord blood unit if the bank goes out of business?**
Ask before you sign. Contracts vary on transfer, continuity, and what fees survive a sale. The same question applies to any long-term biological storage, ours included — see our guide on [what happens when a DNA company shuts down](/blog/what-happens-dna-company-shuts-down).
