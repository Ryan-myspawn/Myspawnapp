Here's the direct answer: **when you die, your accounts don't transfer to your family — access does, and only if you set it up in advance.** Without setup, your heirs face a patchwork: Apple requires a court order or a pre-named Legacy Contact; Google deletes inactive accounts unless the Inactive Account Manager was configured; Facebook either memorializes or deletes; and under US law (RUFADAA, adopted in some form across nearly every state), nobody — not even your executor — can read your private messages unless you explicitly consented in writing. The fix takes about 30 minutes and costs nothing. This article walks through exactly what each platform does, what the law says, and the checklist.

## What each platform actually does

| Platform | The tool | What your family gets | What they never get |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apple | Legacy Contact (iOS 15.2+) | iCloud photos, notes, mail, files — with the access key + death certificate | Keychain passwords, purchased media licenses, payment info |
| Google | Inactive Account Manager | Whatever data you selected, after your chosen inactivity window (up to 18 months); or the account is deleted | Anything, if you never set it up and the account times out |
| Facebook | Legacy Contact + memorialization | A managed memorial profile (pinned posts, new friend requests, profile photo) | Login access, your past posts' editing, your messages |
| Instagram | Memorialization on request | A frozen memorial profile | Login or content changes |
| X and most others | No legacy tool | Account deactivation with a death certificate | Content access |

Sources: [Nolo's legacy-contact guide](https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/how-to-add-legacy-contacts-to-your-accounts.html), [Everplans on Apple's Digital Legacy program](https://www.everplans.com/articles/apple-ios-15-digital-legacy-program-offers-some-control-over-an-account-after-death-follows-efforts-from-facebook-and-google), platform policy summaries current as of 2026 ([CareTabs 2026 guide](https://www.caretabs.com/what-happens-digital-accounts-when-you-die/)).

Notice the pattern: every tool is **opt-in, in advance**. None of them can be invoked retroactively by a grieving family with a shoebox of paperwork.

## The law underneath: RUFADAA in plain English

The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA) is the model law that decides who can reach your digital property. As of 2026 it has been adopted by 47 states plus DC, with California, Louisiana, and Massachusetts running equivalent statutes of their own ([Trust & Will explainer](https://trustandwill.com/learn/what-is-rufadaa), [LegalClarity summary](https://legalclarity.org/revised-uniform-fiduciary-access-to-digital-assets-act-rufadaa/)).

It works as a three-tier hierarchy, and the order matters:

1. **The platform's own tool wins.** If you named someone in Google's Inactive Account Manager or Apple's Legacy Contact, that choice overrides everything — including your will.
2. **Your estate documents come second.** A will, trust, or power of attorney that explicitly grants digital-asset access controls where no online tool was used.
3. **The platform's terms of service decide by default.** If you did neither, the fine print you clicked "agree" on years ago governs — and it usually says access dies with you.

One more detail that surprises families: even a fully empowered executor gets your **files** more easily than your **words**. The content of your emails, chats, and DMs is legally walled off unless you explicitly consented to its release in writing. Your executor might inherit your photos and never legally read the messages that explain them.

## The 30-minute digital legacy checklist

1. **Apple:** Settings → your name → Sign-In & Security → Legacy Contact. Name one or two people; share the access key. (5 min)
2. **Google:** myaccount.google.com → Data & privacy → Inactive Account Manager. Pick your window, your person, and which data they get. (5 min)
3. **Facebook/Instagram:** Settings → Memorialization → name a Legacy Contact, or choose deletion. (5 min)
4. **Password manager:** most (1Password, Bitwarden, Dashlane) offer emergency access — name a trusted contact with a waiting period. (5 min)
5. **One paragraph in your will:** explicit consent for your fiduciary to access digital assets *including the content of electronic communications*. Without that sentence, tier 2 doesn't reach your messages. (Ask your attorney; not legal advice.) (5 min)
6. **A one-page inventory:** where things live — not passwords, just the map. Store it with your estate documents. (5 min)

## FAQ

**What happens to my Google account when I die?**
Unless you configured Inactive Account Manager, nothing transfers; after your chosen inactivity period (or by family request with documentation), Google may delete the account. With IAM configured, your named contact receives the data you selected.

**Can my family access my iPhone after I die?**
Only if you named them as an Apple Legacy Contact (they'll need the access key and a death certificate), or with a court order. Passcodes and Keychain passwords are never released.

**Can my executor read my emails and messages?**
Usually not. Under RUFADAA, the content of electronic communications requires your explicit prior consent — in a platform tool or in your estate documents. Without it, executors can typically access files and records, not correspondence.

**Does my will override Facebook's legacy contact setting?**
No. RUFADAA's hierarchy puts the platform's own tool first — an online designation overrides your will for that account.

**What is a digital legacy?**
Everything you leave behind in accounts and files — photos, messages, documents, subscriptions, domains, crypto — plus the access rights that determine whether anyone can reach it.

**What's the difference between digital legacy and a digital will?**
A digital will (or a digital-assets clause in your regular will) is the legal instrument; your digital legacy is the stuff itself. You need both, plus the platform tools, because the tools outrank the paperwork.
