In vitro gametogenesis, or IVG, is the attempt to grow functional human eggs and sperm in a laboratory from ordinary cells, such as a blood or skin cell, rather than harvesting them from ovaries or testes. It works in mice: healthy pups have been born from lab-made eggs. In humans, it does not work yet. As of August 2026, no laboratory has produced a mature, fertilization-ready human egg through IVG in a peer-reviewed study, and no human being has ever been born from a lab-made gamete. This article explains how IVG is supposed to work, what has genuinely been achieved, which recent headlines are verified science versus company press releases, and what it could mean for how families think about the future.

## What does in vitro gametogenesis actually mean?

Every person begins as one egg and one sperm. For people who cannot produce viable gametes, because of age, cancer treatment, injury, or biology, that requirement has always been final. IVG proposes a workaround in three steps:

1. **Take an ordinary cell.** A skin biopsy or blood draw supplies adult cells.
2. **Reprogram it.** Using induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology, which earned the 2012 Nobel Prize, the cell is wound back into a stem-like state that can, in principle, become any cell type.
3. **Guide it into becoming a gamete.** The stem cells are coaxed through the developmental program that normally happens in ovaries or testes, ending, in theory, with a mature egg or sperm usable in ordinary IVF.

Steps one and two are routine in labs worldwide. Step three is where human IVG has been stuck for more than a decade.

The reason scientists persist is that the mouse version is solved. Japanese teams led by Katsuhiko Hayashi and Mitinori Saitou produced mouse pups from stem-cell-derived eggs years ago, and those pups grew into fertile adults. Mice are not humans, but they are proof that the concept is not physically impossible.

## How close are scientists to lab-grown human eggs and sperm?

The past 14 months produced more IVG news than the previous decade. Here is each claim, labeled by evidence level.

### Lab-grown early human eggs (Conception Biosciences, not yet peer-reviewed)

In a company announcement dated July 2026, [Conception Biosciences](https://www.conception.bio/science-and-updates/the-first-early-human-eggs-from-stem-cells) reported growing the first early human egg cells, called primary oocytes, from stem cells derived from a blood draw, inside lab-made "mini ovaries" (ovaroids). Independent scientists quoted in coverage considered the claim plausible but noted the data has not been published or replicated. Crucially, these are immature, early-stage eggs. They cannot be fertilized, and the distance from a primary oocyte to a mature egg is exactly the step that has defeated every lab so far.

### Lab-grown human sperm (Paterna Biosciences, not yet peer-reviewed)

In May 2026, Utah-based Paterna Biosciences [announced](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/paterna-biosciences-announces-world-first-achievement-lab-grown-human-sperm) it had grown functional human sperm from testicular stem cells and used them to fertilize eggs, forming early embryos. If verified, that would be a genuine world first. It has not been verified: no peer-reviewed paper, no independent replication, and no regulatory clearance exist, a caveat [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/utah-startup-claims-its-lab-grown-sperm-can-produce-embryos-in-potential-fertility-breakthrough-2000750528) and [The Salt Lake Tribune](https://www.sltrib.com/news/2026/04/25/utah-based-paterna-says-it-grew/) both stressed. Scientists have pursued in vitro spermatogenesis for nearly a century and succeeded only in animals.

### Eggs from skin cells (OHSU, peer-reviewed, with honest limits)

The strongest published human result came from Shoukhrat Mitalipov's team at Oregon Health & Science University. In a September 2025 *Nature Communications* paper, the team transplanted skin-cell nuclei into donor eggs stripped of their own nuclei and triggered a novel chromosome-halving process they call mitomeiosis, producing 82 human oocytes that could be fertilized, as covered by [OHSU](https://news.ohsu.edu/2025/09/30/ohsu-researchers-develop-functional-eggs-from-human-skin-cells) and [STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2025/09/30/fertility-pioneer-shoukhrat-mitalipov-research-update/). The honest fine print, which the authors themselves emphasized: only about 9 percent of embryos reached the blastocyst stage, chromosome errors were pervasive, and none had a fully normal chromosome set. Mitalipov's own estimate for clinical readiness is roughly a decade, assuming the chromosome problem gets solved.

### The core blocker: meiosis

The step no lab has cleanly recreated in a dish is meiosis, the specialized cell division that halves chromosomes and shuffles them correctly. In February 2026, researchers in the Hayashi and Saitou orbit reported a concrete reason: lab-grown human germ cells fail to undergo a centromere-tethered genome reorganization that natural germ cells perform. Identifying a specific blocker is progress, but it also confirms why "we made early eggs" claims and "we made usable eggs" remain very different sentences.

## Is anything IVG-adjacent already in clinics?

Yes, and it is the most commonly confused point in this field. [Gameto](https://www.gametogen.com/science/fertilo/), a New York biotech, uses iPSC-derived ovarian support cells to mature a patient's own eggs outside the body, cutting hormone injections by about 80 percent. Its Fertilo program produced its first baby in December 2024 and is now in a US Phase 3 trial ([NCT06858111](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06858111)), recruiting at up to 20 clinics with an interim readout expected in late 2026 and primary completion estimated for May 2027, backed by a [$44 million Series C](https://www.biospace.com/press-releases/gametos-44m-series-c-to-help-redefine-reproductive-care-as-first-patients-recruited-for-pivotal-stem-cell-phase-3-trial).

Fertilo is not IVG. The eggs come from the patient; stem cells only play a supporting role. But it matters for two reasons: it is the first iPSC-derived reproductive therapy to reach Phase 3 in the US, proving regulators will engage with stem-cell fertility medicine, and it shows the field advancing along a spectrum rather than in one leap.

## Who is writing the rules?

The US fertility establishment moved in early 2026. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine published a formal [Ethics Committee opinion on IVG](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/ethical-considerations-of-in-vitro-gametogenesis-an-ethics-committee-opinion-asrm-2026/) (*Fertility and Sterility* 2026;125:617-21) confirming that IVG is not currently feasible in humans, that no human safety data exists, and that major scientific and ethical questions must be settled before any clinical use. Separately, US law currently prevents the FDA from even reviewing clinical applications that involve heritable modification of embryos, which means any first IVG baby faces a regulatory wall on top of the scientific one.

For anyone reading breathless headlines, that is the calibration to keep: the professional body governing US fertility medicine says the technology is not ready, and the experts most often quoted put clinical IVG 10 to 20 years out.

## Frequently asked questions

### Has a baby ever been born using IVG?

In humans, no. Mice have been born from stem-cell-derived eggs and have grown into healthy, fertile adults. Every human IVG claim so far involves immature eggs, chromosomally abnormal embryos, or unverified company announcements. The one stem-cell-related human birth, Gameto's December 2024 baby, used the patient's own egg with stem-cell-derived support cells, which is not IVG.

### How long until IVG is available to patients?

Most quoted experts estimate 10 to 20 years, and OHSU's Mitalipov estimates about a decade for his skin-cell approach if the chromosome-error problem is solved. Those are estimates about an unsolved research problem, not product timelines, and the 2026 ASRM ethics opinion confirms no human safety data exists yet.

### What is the difference between IVG and IVF?

IVF fertilizes an existing egg with existing sperm outside the body. IVG aims to create the egg or sperm itself from a non-reproductive cell. If IVG ever works, it would plug into standard IVF as a front end for people who cannot produce gametes at all.

### Can DNA stored today be used for IVG later?

Unknown, and anyone who promises otherwise is overselling. Current IVG research starts from living cells that are reprogrammed into stem cells. A preserved DNA record is an information and identity archive, and preserved cells are a biological archive, but no science today converts a stored sample into a gamete. Preservation keeps material available for whatever future science can legally and technically do with it, and that is the only honest claim.

### Who is funding IVG research?

A mix of academic labs (OHSU, Kyoto, Osaka) and venture-backed startups: Conception Biosciences (backed among others by Sam Altman), Gameto (about $127 million raised), Paterna Biosciences, and earlier-stage players like Dioseve and Ovelle Bio. Investor interest is strong precisely because the eventual market, fertility without biological gamete limits, would be enormous.

### Is IVG legal?

Research on human IVG is legal in the US under standard oversight, but clinical use is effectively blocked: the FDA cannot currently review applications involving heritable embryo modification, and the ASRM's 2026 opinion states the field is not ready. Other countries, including Japan and the UK, are drafting their own frameworks.
