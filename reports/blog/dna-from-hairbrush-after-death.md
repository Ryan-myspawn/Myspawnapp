# Can You Get DNA From a Hairbrush After Someone Dies?

**Short answer: usually not the DNA you actually want. A hairbrush full of shed hair contains mitochondrial DNA, which traces only the direct maternal line and is useless for the cousin-matching that most family-history questions need. For that you need nuclear DNA, and nuclear DNA lives in the root tissue at the follicle, not in the strand. Licked envelopes and stamps are a better bet and still fail roughly nine times out of ten. Specialist labs will attempt it from about $240, up to around $2,000 for difficult samples, and they charge whether or not they find anything.**

This is one of the most common questions in genealogy forums and one of the least honestly answered. Here is what is actually possible, what it costs, and the constraint at the end that catches almost everyone.

## Why the hairbrush usually fails

Hair carries two different kinds of DNA in two different places, and the difference decides everything.

**Nuclear DNA**, the kind used for autosomal testing and cousin matching, sits in the **living tissue at the root** that anchors the strand to the scalp. **Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)** sits in the **shaft**, the part you can see.

A hairbrush collects **shed hair**: telogen-phase strands that fell out naturally at the end of their growth cycle. And shed hair is the problem. **The cells in a shed hair shaft are fully keratinized**, meaning dead, compacted protein. **Keratinisation degrades nuclear DNA to the point where standard STR profiling becomes impossible.**

So for standard autosomal work you need a hair that was **pulled**, with **visible tissue attached to the root end.** Cut hair will not do it. Brushed-out hair usually will not either.

**Honest caveat, because "never" is too strong:** this is an active research area. Work funded by the National Institute of Justice and published methods for concurrent nuclear and mitochondrial genotyping are specifically aimed at recovering nuclear DNA from **rootless** shafts. **Label: emerging research, not routine service.** The right statement today is *not achievable with standard methods*, not *impossible forever*.

## The consolation prize is smaller than it sounds

If a lab recovers mtDNA from a rootless shaft, that is a real result. It is also a narrow one.

**Mitochondrial DNA traces the direct maternal line only**: your mother, her mother, her mother, back. It says nothing about your father's side, and it does not produce the kind of profile that matches you to second cousins in a database.

So a brush full of your grandmother's hair may well yield mtDNA. **That tells you about her mother's line and cannot answer most of the questions people are actually asking**, which are usually about relationships, parentage, or matching against living relatives.

## What works better, and how much better

Anything the person **licked** is the stronger candidate, because saliva carries cells and paper is straightforward to process.

**Envelopes with sealed flaps. Postage stamps. Postcards. Aerogrammes.**

A company called To the Letter DNA offers commercial testing of exactly these artifacts for genealogical purposes, with prices **starting around $310 AUD.** In the US, the DNA Testing Centre in Lansing, Michigan advertises processing from bloodstains, fingernail clippings, hair and cigarette butts, **starting around $240 USD.** Difficult samples requiring specialist lab work run **in the region of $2,000 per sample or more.**

Now the number that matters. **One lab reports the likelihood of recovering usable DNA from licked envelopes and stamps at roughly 10 percent.**

**And you pay either way.** Labs charge for the extraction attempt whether or not it yields a usable profile. That is not sharp practice; the work is the same. **It does mean a $240 attempt is a $240 lottery ticket with roughly one-in-ten odds**, and a $2,000 attempt on a precious irreplaceable letter is a decision worth sleeping on, because **the test consumes the sample.**

## The constraint almost nobody finds out until afterwards

Suppose it works. You have a profile.

**You cannot upload it everywhere.** Of the major consumer genealogy databases, **only FTDNA and MyHeritage accept uploaded profiles from third-party artifact testing.** The others do not.

So the realistic outcome of a successful, expensive, one-in-ten artifact test is a profile you can search against **two** databases rather than the whole field. **That is still worth having. It is not what most people picture when they send the envelope off**, and it is the single most common unpleasant surprise in this process.

**Verification note, and it matters more here than anywhere else in this article: upload policies change.** This is exactly the kind of fact that goes stale quietly. **Confirm current acceptance with the database itself before you spend anything.**

Here is the comparison nobody lays out, and it is the reason this article exists.

## If you are holding artifacts right now

Practical, in order.

**Stop handling them.** Every touch adds contamination and modern DNA competes with degraded old DNA in an extraction.

**Store them dry, dark and cool, in paper, not plastic.** Plastic traps moisture and moisture is what destroys DNA. An acid-free paper envelope in a drawer beats a sealed bag every time.

**Do not lick, moisten, steam or attempt to open a sealed envelope.** The seal is the sample.

**Separate hairs with visible root tissue from the rest and store them apart.** Those are the only strands with a realistic chance at nuclear DNA, and they are worth far more than the brush they came from.

**Inventory before you spend.** Write down what you have and who it came from. **Labeling matters more than most preservation**, a point we made in [Who Ends Up With the Family Archive](/blog/who-keeps-the-family-archive): an unlabeled bag of hair from an unknown relative is not a sample, it is a storage problem.

**Ask three questions before paying anyone:** what is your success rate on this artifact type, is the sample consumed, and which databases accept your profile.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you get DNA from hair without the root?

You can usually get **mitochondrial DNA**, which is present in high copy number in the shaft. **You generally cannot get usable nuclear DNA** by standard methods, because the cells in a shed hair shaft are fully keratinized and nuclear DNA is degraded past the point where STR profiling works. Research into recovering nuclear DNA from rootless shafts is active, but it is **not a routine commercial service.**

### Is mitochondrial DNA useful for genealogy?

For one specific thing: tracing the **direct maternal line**. It will not match you to cousins the way an autosomal test does, and it says nothing about your father's side. **If your question is about relationships or parentage, mtDNA will usually not answer it.**

### How much does artifact DNA testing cost?

Specialist services advertise from around **$240 USD**, and around **$310 AUD** for envelope and stamp testing. **Difficult samples can run to roughly $2,000 or more.** You are charged for the attempt regardless of the outcome.

### What are the odds it works?

For licked envelopes and stamps, **one lab reports roughly 10 percent.** Odds vary by artifact, age, storage conditions and handling history, so treat any single figure as indicative rather than precise.

### Where should I store old letters and hair before testing?

**Dry, dark, cool, and in paper rather than plastic.** Moisture is the main enemy. Handle as little as possible, keep root-bearing hairs separate from shed hairs, and **do not open sealed envelopes**, because the sealed flap is the part with the DNA in it.
