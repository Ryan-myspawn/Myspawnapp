The legal right to take possession of a body and decide what happens to it is called the **right of sepulcher**, and in most US states it does not belong to whoever was closest to you. **It belongs, in order: to a person you named in writing while alive, and failing that, to whoever sits highest on your state's statutory priority list.** Your own preferences carry weight only to the extent you recorded them in the form your state recognises.

Which leads to the single most common and most expensive mistake in this area. **Putting your funeral wishes in your will usually does not work, and it fails for a reason that has nothing to do with the law.**

## Why the will is the wrong document

A will is read after the fact. Secondary guidance on this is consistent and blunt: a will usually is not located and read until after services are well under way or already completed. **By the time anybody opens it, the decision it was meant to govern has been made.** The document can be perfectly valid and perfectly useless at the same time.

**This is not a drafting problem, it is a sequencing problem**, and no amount of care in the wording fixes it. The instrument has to be one that reaches the funeral director in the first forty-eight hours.

## What actually works: the written designation

Most states have revised their statutes to allow a **separate designation of an agent** for final disposition. The form travels under names like **Designation of Agent for Final Disposition** or **Authorization of Final Disposition**, and in some states the authority is folded into the health care proxy or the power of attorney instead.

Formalities vary and they are not decorative:

- **New York** (Public Health Law § 4201) gives first priority to an agent named in a **written instrument**, and the state Department of Health publishes a basic form. **A handwritten note is not enough.**
- **Washington** requires the designation to be in a document **signed and dated by the decedent in the presence of a witness**.
- **Rhode Island** calls for a **signed, notarized original**.
- **Louisiana** works from a **notarized declaration**, and only then falls to a priority list.
- **Missouri** puts at the top an **attorney in fact under a durable power of attorney that specifically grants the right of sepulcher**. A general power of attorney is not assumed to carry it.

## The pattern underneath all of it

Read six of these statutes side by side and one structural fact stands out. **In state after state, the top rank on the list is not a person at all. It is an instrument.**

Missouri's first rank is an attorney in fact holding a specific grant. New York's is an agent named in a written instrument. Louisiana works from a notarized declaration. Guam's statute provides for a person designated by the deceased to act as next of kin under a valid designation of right of sepulcher, placing the nearest surviving relative by consanguinity or affinity below that.

**The relatives only appear once the instrument is missing.** That is the opposite of how most people assume this works, and it is the reason the practical advice below is so short: the entire question is whether a document exists and whether it was executed the way your state requires. **Everything after that is a fallback.**

## When nobody designated anyone

Absent a valid designation, authority falls to a ranked list of relatives, and **the ranking is a state-by-state matter with no national rule.** What the statutes have in common is more interesting than where they differ.

**They impose conditions on the person at the top.** Missouri requires that the person be **eighteen or older, mentally competent, and willing to assume responsibility for the costs** of disposition. Rank alone is not enough; an unwilling or unable relative does not hold the right by virtue of being related.

**They handle ties explicitly.** Maine starts with the spouse, then a domestic partner, and where several relatives share a rank, **the majority of that group decides**. If that group cannot reach a majority, **a court decides.** That is the mechanism by which a disagreement between four adult siblings becomes a hearing.

**They can strip authority for cause.** A Washington bill analysis describes control passing down the list where a person has certain **protection-order or abuse-related convictions**.

**And they have a floor.** Missouri's list ends, where no next of kin assumes responsibility, with **any person or friend who assumes financial responsibility** for the disposition. **The last rank in the statute is not a relative at all. It is whoever pays.**

There is also a notification duty worth knowing: a person exercising the right in Missouri who knows of someone with a **superior** right must notify them before making final arrangements.

## What this means if you are estranged

This is the case the designation statutes were built for. **The designated-agent route lets you name someone other than a spouse or next of kin**, which is the only reliable way to keep the decision away from a family member who would otherwise sit at the top of the list by operation of law.

The reverse case matters too, and we cover the general version in [who is legally next of kin](/blog/who-is-next-of-kin). **Being the person who was actually there is not a legal rank.** Our article on [legacy planning when you are estranged](/blog/estranged-family-legacy-planning) covers the broader problem; this is its sharpest instance, because the decision happens within days and cannot be undone.

## The limit on your agent

One honest qualifier that the sources are consistent about. **Your designated agent is not obliged, in any state, to carry out wishes that are highly impractical, illegal, or financially burdensome.** Naming an agent gives somebody the authority to decide. It does not convert your preferences into binding orders, and a plan that depends on a person doing something unreasonable is not a plan.

## What to actually do

1. **Find out whether your state has a designation form, and use that form.** Not a letter, not a note, not a clause in the will.
2. **Check the formalities.** Witnessed, notarized, or incorporated into a power of attorney; the requirement is specific and a document that misses it may rank below someone you did not choose.
3. **If you are relying on a power of attorney, check that it grants this specifically.** At least one state requires the grant to be express.
4. **Tell the person.** An agent who learns of the appointment from a funeral director is starting from behind.
5. **Keep it somewhere reachable in forty-eight hours.** The same sequencing problem that defeats the will defeats a safe deposit box.

## FAQ

### What is the right of sepulcher?
The legal right to take custody of a deceased person's body and control its disposition. In most US states it goes first to an agent the person named in writing, and otherwise to whoever sits highest on the state's statutory priority list.

### Can I put my funeral wishes in my will?
You can, and in many states it is permitted, but it frequently fails in practice because a will is usually not read until after services are under way or finished. A separate designation form is the instrument that arrives in time.

### Who decides if there is no written designation?
A ranked list set by state law, typically starting with a spouse. Rankings differ between states, and most statutes also require the person to be an adult, competent, and willing to take on the costs.

### What happens if siblings disagree?
Some states resolve same-rank disputes by majority vote of that group, and where no majority forms, a court decides. Maine's statute is written that way.

### Can someone be removed from the priority list?
In some states, yes. A Washington bill analysis describes authority passing down the list where a person has certain protection-order or abuse-related convictions.

### Does my agent have to follow my instructions exactly?
No. Sources are consistent that a designated agent is not obliged to carry out wishes that are highly impractical, illegal, or financially burdensome. The designation assigns authority; it does not create an enforceable order.
