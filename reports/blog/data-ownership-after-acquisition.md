# Who Owns Your Data If the Company Is Acquired?

**The short answer: in a bankruptcy there is a referee. In an ordinary acquisition there is not.** US bankruptcy law contains specific machinery for the sale of personal information, including a court-appointed **consumer privacy ombudsman**. A normal company sale has none of that. It has a clause in the terms you agreed to, usually the word **assign**, and an email telling you the terms have been updated. **That is the whole difference, and almost nobody knows it runs that way round.**

## Everyone plans for the wrong failure

Ask someone what worries them about a service holding their photos, recordings or genetic data, and they will describe a shutdown. The site goes dark, the company stops answering, the files are gone.

Shutdowns happen. They are also **visible**. Something breaks, you notice, and if you are quick you can export.

The commoner outcome is quieter. **The company does not stop. It changes hands.** The product keeps working, the logo eventually changes, and one day you get an email about updated terms. Nothing was broken. Everything moved.

## The clause that decides it

Open the terms of any service you have given something to and search for the word **assign**.

You will usually find a sentence saying the agreement may be transferred if the company is **acquired, merged, or sold**. Try **merger** and **bankruptcy** too. Most consumer terms permit the transfer freely, and many privacy policies say explicitly that your information is among the assets that may change hands in a corporate transaction.

That one sentence is doing more work than the entire privacy policy printed above it, because it decides **who ends up holding your things**. It takes about ten seconds to find and most people have never read it.

## What bankruptcy has that an acquisition does not

Here is the part that surprises people.

**If a company goes bankrupt, US law takes your data more seriously than if it is simply bought.**

**Section 363(b)(1) of the Bankruptcy Code** restricts a debtor's ability to sell or lease **personally identifiable information** outside the ordinary course of business, where the company had disclosed a policy prohibiting the transfer of that information to non-affiliated persons and that policy was in effect when the case began.

**Section 332** creates the **consumer privacy ombudsman**: a person appointed by the court when a hearing is required under section 363(b)(1)(B), who may appear and be heard at the sale hearing to help the court weigh the facts and conditions of the proposed sale.

Read that carefully, because there is a catch inside it. **The protection is triggered by what your privacy policy promised.** A company that promised never to transfer your data faces the ombudsman. A company whose policy always said your data may move in a corporate transaction has, in effect, pre-cleared the sale.

**The machinery is real, and it has worked.** Two cases are the standard references:

- **Toysmart (2000).** An online toy retailer failed in the dot-com crash and tried to sell customer data, including information about children, despite a privacy policy saying it would **never** do so. The **Federal Trade Commission** brought a deceptive-practices claim.
- **RadioShack (2015).** In Chapter 11 it proposed selling customer personal information. The **FTC warned the court-appointed consumer privacy ombudsman** that the sale would violate the FTC Act's prohibition on unfair or deceptive practices, and **state attorneys general objected** on the basis that it would violate the company's own privacy policy and state consumer-protection statutes.

**In an ordinary acquisition, none of that exists.** No bankruptcy court. No ombudsman. No sale hearing. No structured window for a state attorney general to object in. The transfer happens because a contract you accepted said it could.

## The genetic case, and why it was different

The largest recent test was **23andMe**, and it is worth knowing how it actually ended.

After filing for Chapter 11, the company ran a court-approved sale process. **Regeneron** was initially declared the successful bidder under an asset purchase agreement worth **$256 million**. Bidding was then reopened, Regeneron declined to go higher, and the assets went to **TTAM Research Institute**, a California nonprofit public benefit corporation led by co-founder Anne Wojcicki, for **$305 million**. The sale completed on **14 July 2025**.

**Several state attorneys general objected**, arguing that under their own statutes customers hold strong property or privacy rights in genetic material and data, and that those laws require explicit permission for any sale or transfer. A group of states, reported to include **California, Kentucky, Tennessee, Texas and Utah**, remained actively opposed. The company and the buyer argued that no separate opt-in was legally required because the acquirer would honor the existing privacy commitments and maintain the privacy policy.

You can think what you like about the outcome. **Notice the mechanism.** Every part of that argument happened **in public, in a court, with regulators in the room, because it was a bankruptcy.** Had the same assets moved in a straightforward acquisition, most of those levers would not have existed.

*(Bankruptcy filings and court-approved sale processes are public record; the state-by-state objection list comes from reporting on the proceedings. Check the docket rather than a summary, including this one, before relying on any detail.)*

## What to actually do

1. **Search the terms before you hand anything over.** `assign`, `merger`, `acquisition`, `bankruptcy`. Ten seconds.
2. **Read what the privacy policy promises about transfer.** A policy that promises never to transfer your data is worth more than one that reserves the right, and in a bankruptcy that difference is the difference between an ombudsman and a formality.
3. **Ask where the thing itself is held**, not just who bills you. A company holding your material in its own office and a company using an independently accredited third-party facility are in different positions if either is sold.
4. **Export on a schedule, not on a notice.** The notice is the end of the process.
5. **Check the app's last-updated date.** Public, free, and the most honest health metric a small company has.

## FAQ

**What happens to my data when a company is acquired?**
Usually it transfers with the business, under a clause in the terms you agreed to. Search those terms for the word **assign**. Most consumer agreements permit transfer on acquisition, merger or sale.

**Is my data more protected in a bankruptcy than in an acquisition?**
In some respects, yes. Bankruptcy Code **section 363(b)(1)** restricts the sale of personally identifiable information where the company's disclosed policy prohibited transfer to non-affiliated persons, and **section 332** provides for a court-appointed **consumer privacy ombudsman**. An ordinary acquisition has no equivalent.

**What is a consumer privacy ombudsman?**
A person appointed by a bankruptcy court when a hearing is required over the sale of personally identifiable information. They may appear and be heard at the sale hearing to help the court assess the proposed sale.

**What happened to 23andMe's customer data?**
After a Chapter 11 filing and a court-approved sale process, the assets were acquired by **TTAM Research Institute**, a nonprofit led by co-founder Anne Wojcicki, for **$305 million**, completing on **14 July 2025**. Regeneron had earlier been declared the successful bidder at **$256 million**. Several state attorneys general objected and some remained opposed.

**Can I stop my data being transferred?**
Usually not after the fact. Your leverage is before you sign up and while you can still delete or export. That is why the ten-second search matters more than any complaint afterwards.
