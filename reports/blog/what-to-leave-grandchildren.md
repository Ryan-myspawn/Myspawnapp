The most useful things you can leave grandchildren are the ones they cannot reconstruct without you: recorded answers in your own voice, a written family health history, provenance notes for the few objects worth keeping, and preserved records like documents and a DNA sample. Money and property transfer automatically through an estate plan. Everything on that first list disappears silently unless someone deliberately captures it, and the window closes on a date nobody gets told in advance.

## The backdrop: a lot of money is moving, and most families are underprepared

An estimated **$124 trillion** is expected to change hands to heirs and charities **by 2048**, including roughly **$54 trillion** going to widowed spouses first ([RBC Wealth Management](https://www.rbcwealthmanagement.com/en-us/campaign/wealth-transfer-are-you-ready); Cerulli Associates figures widely reported through 2026). The transfer is broader than cash: Bank of America found that among wealthy Americans in 2026, the share of businesses **inherited** is projected to reach **23%** versus **11%** purchased ([Fortune, July 15, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/07/15/124-trillion-dollar-great-wealth-transfer-more-inherited-businesses-bofa-report/)).

Two caveats worth stating, because honest framing beats hype. First, that wealth is highly concentrated and often reduced by debt, longevity, and healthcare costs, so many heirs overestimate what is coming ([CFA Institute](https://www.cfainstitute.org/insights/articles/great-wealth-transfer-myths-reality)). Second, most families have not done the paperwork at all: a nationally representative survey of 5,000 U.S. adults fielded in early 2026 found **56% have no estate planning documents whatsoever**, and will ownership fell from 31% to 26% in a single year ([Trust & Will 2026 Estate Planning Report](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/trust--wills-2026-estate-planning-report-56-of-americans-still-have-no-estate-plan-ai-trust-hits-an-all-time-high-302735330.html)).

So: do the legal part. Then read on, because the legal part transmits your assets and nothing about you.

## The uncomfortable finding about stuff

Here is the result that should change how you think about heirlooms. In a 2026 survey of 1,200 U.S. adults by Talker Research (commissioned by 1-800-GOT-JUNK), **49% said they would rather inherit nothing at all than have to deal with a relative's belongings**, and **51% would prefer a select few items** over receiving everything a loved one owned. More than half of those who had cleared out a deceased relative's home said the experience made grieving *harder*, not easier.

Read that twice if you are the person currently filling a storage unit "for the kids." Volume is not legacy. Volume is a logistics problem you are scheduling for someone who will be sad and busy.

The corrective is not to leave nothing. It is to leave **fewer things with more context**, plus the categories below that weigh nothing at all.

## The inventory

### 1. Recorded answers, in your voice

The single highest-value item on this list, and the cheapest.

A [YouGov poll of 6,090 Americans](https://yougov.com/en-us/articles/42718-regret-not-preserving-memories-death-loved-ones) found that many people have deliberately recorded or documented conversations with loved ones to preserve memories of them, and that even more **regret not preserving memories** of people close to them after they died. Regret is the reliable finding in this literature. Nobody reports regretting the recording.

What to record, concretely:
- **Origins:** where the family came from, what was left behind, what was never spoken about.
- **The naming session:** sit with the oldest photo album and identify every face, every relationship, every place. This index exists in exactly one head, and it is the first thing lost.
- **Work:** what you actually did all day, and what you were proud of that nobody noticed.
- **Advice you would not put in a letter:** the honest version.

Format matters less than existence. Video beats audio, audio beats notes, notes beat nothing. If you want a structure, our guide to [recording a video message for an 18th birthday](/blog/video-message-18th-birthday) lays out five questions that work.

### 2. A written family health history

Ask what your parents and grandparents actually died of, at what age, and what they had that nobody discussed. Write it down with names, relationships, and ages at diagnosis.

This document quietly outperforms most genetic testing for everyday clinical use, and physicians treating your grandchildren in 2070 will not be able to reconstruct it from anywhere else. It takes twenty minutes and costs nothing.

### 3. Provenance for the few objects that survive the cull

Given the finding above, pick a small number of objects and write one paragraph for each: where it came from, who used it, what happened while they had it. An object with a story gets kept. An identical object without one gets donated by an exhausted 40-year-old on a deadline.

A useful rule: if you cannot write the paragraph, the object was never the point.

### 4. Documents, in a form that opens in fifty years

Birth and marriage certificates, military records, immigration papers, deeds, photographs. Digitize them in boring, ubiquitous formats and store them somewhere with a named owner and a plan, rather than a hard drive in a drawer. Our [digital time capsule guide](/blog/digital-time-capsule) covers format choice and the migration ritual that keeps files readable across decades.

### 5. The biological record

This is the category nobody's checklist includes yet, so it is worth being precise about what it is and is not.

Everything above transmits *information about* a person. A preserved DNA sample is the one item that transmits the underlying biological record itself: the source file rather than the notes. It cannot be reconstructed from photographs, testing results, or memory. It either was kept or it was not.

### 6. A named custodian

The most-skipped line. Every category above needs a person who knows it exists, knows where it lives, and has been told they are responsible for it. Legacies do not fail because the recording was bad. They fail because nobody knew there was a recording.

Write the names down. Tell the people. Put it with the will.

## A realistic sequence

1. **This month:** twenty minutes of family health history, written.
2. **Next family gathering:** the album-naming session, filmed on a phone.
3. **This quarter:** the legal documents, if you are in the 56% without them.
4. **This year:** digitize the paper archive; write provenance paragraphs for the ten objects that stay.
5. **Whenever:** the biological record, if it fits your priorities and budget.

None of this requires wealth, which is the point. The families that hand down the most are frequently not the ones that hand down the most money.

## FAQ

**What do grandchildren actually value most from grandparents?**
Recorded stories and identified family history consistently outrank objects. Survey data shows about half of Americans would rather inherit nothing than deal with a relative's belongings, while regret about *not* preserving conversations is widespread.

**Is it worth leaving heirlooms at all?**
Yes, but fewer of them and with written provenance. An object with a documented story gets kept across generations; an undocumented one becomes a burden during an estate cleanout.

**What is the great wealth transfer and will my family see any of it?**
It refers to roughly $124 trillion expected to pass to heirs and charities by 2048. Analysts caution that the wealth is concentrated and often reduced by debt, longevity, and healthcare costs, so many families will see far less than headlines imply.

**How do I record a grandparent who hates being filmed?**
Record audio during an activity instead of a sit-down interview: cooking, driving, sorting photos. The album-naming session works well because their attention is on the photos, not the camera.

**Should I write a letter to my grandchildren?**
A recording generally outperforms a letter, because voice and face carry what handwriting cannot, and a recording can be addressed to a specific future date. Either beats the far more common outcome, which is neither.

**Is storing DNA useful if nobody in my family is sick?**
It is a records decision, not a medical one. A stored sample preserves information that cannot be rebuilt later; it does not diagnose, predict, or treat anything today, and no honest provider will tell you what it will enable decades from now.
