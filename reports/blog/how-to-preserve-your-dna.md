Preserving your DNA is simpler than most people expect: you collect a sample (usually saliva or a cheek swab), stabilize it against moisture, heat and enzymes, and store it — either yourself, with serious limitations, or through an accredited biobank built for decades-long custody. Done properly, a preserved sample keeps both your readable genetic record and physical DNA available to your family and to technologies that don't exist yet. This guide covers every method available in 2026, what each costs, what the science actually supports, and how to choose a custodian you can trust.

## Why preserve DNA at all?

A preserved genome is the one family document that can't be reconstructed later. Photos can be rescanned and stories re-told, but when a person is gone, their complete genetic record goes with them — unless it was saved.

There are four concrete reasons people do it:

**Medical history for descendants.** Your genome carries information your grandchildren's doctors may one day query directly — pharmacogenomic markers, disease-risk variants, carrier status. Clinical genetics keeps finding meaning in variants that were unreadable noise a decade earlier, which means a saved genome quietly gains value over time.

**Genealogy that doesn't dead-end.** Family historians routinely hit walls where records burn, migrate or blur. A physical DNA sample is a primary source that future relatives can re-test with whatever tools their era has.

**Proof that biological time can be paused.** In July 2025, a healthy boy was born from an embryo frozen for more than 31 years — created in 1994, transferred in 2025 — the longest storage-to-birth interval on record, as reported by [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/07/29/1120769/exclusive-record-breaking-baby-born-embryo-over-30-years-old/) and certified by [Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/2025/8/the-30-year-old-infant-worlds-oldest-baby-born-from-embryo-frozen-in-1994). That is preserved *reproductive tissue*, not a DNA record — a distinction we'll keep sharp below — but it is hard evidence that properly stored biological material stays viable across generations.

**A seat at the table for future science.** Nobody can honestly tell you what will be possible with a preserved genome in 2076. What is certain is that every future technology will share one requirement: a sample has to exist. Preservation is the only step that cannot be taken retroactively.

## What happens to DNA if you don't preserve it

DNA is a physical molecule, and it decays. The landmark study in this field, published in [*Proceedings of the Royal Society B*](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2012.1745), measured degradation across 158 dated moa bones and calculated a DNA half-life of roughly **521 years** — meaning half the chemical bonds in an unprotected sample break within about five centuries, with readable fragments surviving far longer but growing uselessly short.

Five hundred years sounds comfortable until you read the fine print: that figure describes DNA locked inside dense bone at cool, stable temperatures. DNA in a decomposing body, a buried casket or a humid attic degrades orders of magnitude faster. Enzymes, water, heat, UV light and microbes shred unprotected genetic material on a timescale of days to years, not centuries. Cremation destroys it outright.

The practical takeaway: *whether* your DNA survives is almost entirely a question of how it's stabilized and stored, which is why the method you choose matters more than anything else in this guide.

## The four ways to preserve your DNA in 2026

### 1. Home storage (DNA cards and DIY kits)

Chemically treated collection cards (the same family of technology as forensic FTA cards) bind DNA and suppress the enzymes that degrade it. Kept genuinely cool, dark and dry, cards can preserve amplifiable DNA for years to decades.

Honest limitations: home conditions are uncontrolled. Humidity swings, heat waves, floods, house fires and simple loss are the realistic failure modes, and there's no chain of custody — nothing that proves to a future lab whose sample it is or how it was handled. Home storage is better than nothing, and a reasonable *backup*, but it isn't an archival plan.

### 2. Consumer genetic testing (data, not DNA)

Ancestry-style tests and consumer whole-genome sequencing produce a digital file, not a preserved sample. Sequencing is cheaper than ever — consumer 30× whole-genome sequencing runs roughly **$169–$495 in 2026** ([GenomeTesting.org price survey](https://genometesting.org/blog/genome-sequencing-cost.html)) — and a stored genome file is genuinely valuable.

But a file is a snapshot taken with today's instruments. It contains only what today's sequencers read and today's formats record; a physical sample can be re-read by every future generation of technology. The gold standard is both: sequence now, preserve the physical sample for whatever comes next. (This is the model MySpawn's $99 founding-member kit follows — physical preservation first, with the sample available for sequencing whenever you choose.)

### 3. Accredited biobanking (physical, professional custody)

A professional biobank stabilizes your sample in a controlled facility under documented standards, monitored conditions and a legal chain of custody. This is the only option engineered for the multi-decade timescales DNA legacy actually requires, and the newest storage science is encouraging: a 2025 peer-reviewed evaluation of **ensilication** — encapsulating DNA in a glass-like silica shell — found ambient-temperature samples matched cryogenically frozen controls on sequencing fidelity, and that *frozen* samples actually accumulated up to 65% more artifactual C>T mutations than ensilicated ones ([*NAR Molecular Medicine*](https://academic.oup.com/narmolmed/article/3/1/ugag011/8456380)). A companion study found ensilicated DNA supported clinical long-read sequencing equivalent to frozen storage ([*Genome Biology*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13059-026-04137-4)). Modern ambient-temperature vaulting is no longer a compromise; done right, it's the state of the art.

### 4. Cryopreserved cells and gametes (a different product)

Egg, sperm and embryo freezing preserve *living reproductive cells* for family-building, at fertility-clinic prices ($5,000–$15,000+ plus annual storage) and under medical supervision. Be precise about the difference: a DNA record preserves *information and physical genetic material*; cryopreserved gametes preserve *reproductive capability*. No DNA-preservation service — MySpawn included — can turn a stored DNA sample into a future child, and any company implying otherwise is selling ahead of the science. If reproductive options matter to you, talk to a fertility clinic as well.

## How to choose a custodian (the part most people skip)

2025 delivered a masterclass in why custody terms matter. When 23andMe went bankrupt, the genetic data of millions of customers was nearly auctioned to the highest bidder — a pharmaceutical company initially won at $256M before a nonprofit led by the founder ultimately took the assets at $305M, as covered by [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2025/06/30/nx-s1-5451398/23andme-sale-approved-dna-data). Customers had no vote. The lesson isn't "never trust anyone" — it's *check the structure before you need it*.

Ask five questions of any DNA preservation provider:

1. **Is the facility independently accredited?** [ISO 20387](https://a2la.org/accreditation/iso-20387-biobanking/) is the international biobanking-competence standard — it requires audited SOPs for acquisition, preservation and data management, qualified personnel, risk controls and periodic re-assessment. CAP (College of American Pathologists) accreditation and FDA facility registration are further independent layers.
2. **What exactly is stored — data, physical DNA, or both?**
3. **What happens if the company fails?** Look for named contingency custody, not silence.
4. **Who can access the sample, and under what legal process?**
5. **How is the sample stabilized, and is the method published science?**

## FAQ

**How long can preserved DNA last?**
Unprotected DNA degrades quickly, but stabilized DNA is a different story: the bone-DNA decay study implies a ~521-year half-life even *without* modern stabilization, and 2025 peer-reviewed work shows silica-encapsulated DNA at room temperature matching or beating frozen storage on fidelity. Properly vaulted samples are expected to remain readable for generations; claims of "forever" are marketing, not measurement.

**Can I preserve my DNA at home?**
Yes, with treated collection cards kept cool, dark and dry — but you accept uncontrolled conditions, no chain of custody, and single-point-of-failure risk. Treat home storage as a backup, not an archive.

**Is preserving DNA the same as cloning?**
No. DNA preservation stores genetic material and information. Human reproductive cloning is illegal in dozens of countries, isn't offered by any legitimate service, and no DNA-storage company can or should promise any future use of your sample beyond keeping it intact and readable.

**Can my family use my DNA after I die?**
They can access whatever your custody agreement and estate documents authorize — typically re-testing for medical or genealogical insight. This is exactly why step 5 above (naming the sample in your estate plan) matters as much as the preservation itself.

**Is my genetic privacy protected?**
In the US, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (2008) bars health insurers and employers from using genetic data against you, but it does not cover life, disability or long-term-care insurance — and the 23andMe bankruptcy showed corporate custody terms matter as much as law. Choose providers whose failure plans are written down.

**What's the difference between DNA banking and egg/sperm freezing?**
DNA banking preserves your genetic record and physical DNA for information, identity and legacy. Egg/sperm/embryo freezing preserves living cells for medically supervised family-building. Different products, different prices, different promises — and only the second one is a fertility service.
