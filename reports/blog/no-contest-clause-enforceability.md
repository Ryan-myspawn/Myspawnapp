A **no-contest clause**, also called an **in terrorem** clause, says that anyone who challenges the will or trust forfeits what it leaves them. **Whether that threat is real depends almost entirely on the state, and the spread between states is far wider than most people expect.** In one state the clause is void no matter what. In others it is enforced only if the challenger had no good reason to sue. In at least two, it is reported to be enforced even against a challenger who had excellent reasons and simply lost.

**So the honest answer to "does a no-contest clause work" is: it depends on which state's law governs the document, and that is the first thing to establish, before anything else.**

## The three regimes

**LABEL FOR EVERYTHING BELOW: statutes read through secondary legal commentary, law-firm writing and legal-education sources. No source was opened at its own domain. Nothing here is legal advice, and every state point needs checking against that state's current code.**

**Regime one: void regardless.** **Florida does not enforce these clauses at all**, under **Fla. Stat. 732.517**, and the sources are consistent that this holds **irrespective of probable cause**. A clause can be drafted perfectly and simply have no effect.

**Regime two: enforceable unless the challenger had probable cause.** This is the Uniform Probate Code position, at **UPC §§ 2-517 and 3-905**. A 2012 ACTEC survey counted **22 states** in this group, **and that figure is fourteen years old and is quoted here as a rough shape rather than a current count.**

**Regime three: enforceable even with probable cause.** **New York is described in the sources as the most prominent state enforcing these clauses strictly**, and **Oregon is reported to enforce them against losing parties even where there was probable cause to contest.**

## Indiana, and why this article will not tell you what Indiana does

This is the part worth slowing down for, because it is a live disagreement in the sources rather than a detail.

**Older material lists Florida and Indiana together as the two states whose statutes simply void no-contest clauses.** **More recent material says Indiana changed course: that the Indiana General Assembly's 2018 enactment of Senate Bill 247 made a no-contest provision enforceable, subject to enumerated exceptions, including that a challenge brought for good cause will not trigger the clause.**

**And a 2026 article still lists Indiana among the states that refuse to enforce them.**

**We could not resolve that from the sources available to us, so we are not going to pick one.** What we can say is the useful part: **if your document is governed by Indiana law, the thing to check is whether the 2018 amendment applies to it, because the answer changes the clause from decorative to dangerous.**

**That is also a general lesson, and it is the reason this section exists.** A reference chart on this subject can be perfectly accurate on the day it is written and wrong four years later, and **nothing in the chart tells you which rows have moved.** Charts on will-contest law are maintained less often than the law changes.

## What "probable cause" actually asks

In the probable-cause states, the test is not whether you win.

**California's version is the clearest written formulation we found.** Under **Probate Code §§ 21310 to 21315**, a no-contest clause is enforceable only in three narrow situations, the main one being a **direct contest brought without probable cause**, at **§ 21311(a)(1)**.

**And § 21311(b) defines the standard: whether, at the time of filing, the facts known to the contestant would cause a reasonable person to believe there is a reasonable likelihood that the requested relief will be granted.**

Three things follow and each one matters:

1. **It is judged at the moment of filing**, on what you knew then, not on what came out later.
2. **Certainty is not required**, and **winning is not required.** A contestant can lose and still keep their inheritance if the contest was reasonable when brought.
3. **It is an objective test.** Sincerity is not the question. A reasonable person's view of the facts is.

**One complication the sources flag: older California case law used a "substantial likelihood" formulation, and sources differ on which wording controls.** **That gap between "reasonable likelihood" and "substantial likelihood" is not academic; it is the difference between a close case won and lost**, and it is a question for a lawyer in that state rather than for an article.

## The trap is the word "contest"

California's other two enforceable scenarios are narrow and conditional: **a pleading challenging the ownership of property, and a creditor's claim, each only if the clause expressly addresses that kind of action.** That looks protective, and mostly it is.

**But courts have read "direct contest" more broadly than the plain word suggests.** Commentary describes a case in which **a change to a trust's terms, labelled as an amendment, was treated as effectively a revocation and therefore as a direct contest** for the purposes of the clause.

**The practical reading: what counts as a contest is a question of effect, not of label.** Filing something you would never call a challenge can still trigger the clause because of what it does.

## What this means if you are writing the document

**A no-contest clause is a deterrent, not a wall**, and it is worth being clear-eyed about which one you are buying.

- **In Florida it does nothing.** If your estate plan's peace depends on it, your plan has no peace.
- **In a probable-cause state it deters bad-faith challenges and permits good-faith ones.** That is arguably the right outcome, and it also means the clause will not stop the challenge you are most worried about if that challenge has merit.
- **In a strict-enforcement state it is genuinely powerful**, and it will also punish an heir who had legitimate grounds and lost. **That is the cost, and it should be a decision rather than a side effect.**

**The clause is also only as strong as the document around it.** A no-contest clause in a will whose signing is vulnerable is a lock on a door with no frame, which is why [testamentary capacity](/blog/testamentary-capacity-explained) and [a self-proving affidavit](/blog/self-proving-affidavit-will) do more work than most people realise. And no clause of any kind overrides a spouse's statutory share, which we cover in [can a spouse override a will](/blog/can-a-spouse-override-a-will).

## What this means if you are deciding whether to challenge

**Find out which state's law governs, first.** Everything else is downstream of that.

**Then ask what you knew, and when.** In probable-cause states that is the whole case on this question. **Evidence you obtain after filing may win you the contest and will not retroactively give you probable cause.**

**And weigh what is actually at risk.** A no-contest clause only has teeth against someone who was left something. **A person left nothing has nothing to forfeit**, which is why these clauses are usually paired with a deliberate bequest large enough to lose.

## FAQ

### What is a no-contest clause?
A provision in a will or trust stating that a beneficiary who challenges the document forfeits what it leaves them. It is also called an in terrorem clause, from the Latin for "in fear".

### Are no-contest clauses enforceable?
It depends on the state. Florida does not enforce them at all under Fla. Stat. 732.517. Many states follow the Uniform Probate Code approach of enforcing them unless the challenger had probable cause. New York is described as enforcing them strictly, and Oregon is reported to enforce them even where probable cause existed.

### What counts as probable cause?
In California, Probate Code § 21311(b) asks whether the facts known to the contestant at the time of filing would cause a reasonable person to believe there is a reasonable likelihood the requested relief will be granted. Certainty is not required and winning is not required.

### Does Indiana enforce no-contest clauses?
Our sources disagree. Older material lists Indiana alongside Florida as voiding them; other material says a 2018 statute made them enforceable with a good-cause exception; and a 2026 article still lists Indiana among non-enforcing states. We could not resolve it and recommend checking the current Indiana Code directly.

### Can I challenge a will without triggering the clause?
Possibly, depending on the state and on what you file. Courts have treated filings by their effect rather than their label, including treating an amendment that worked as a revocation as a direct contest. Assume the clause reaches more than its wording suggests.

### Does a no-contest clause affect someone left nothing?
Generally no. A forfeiture clause has nothing to take from a person who inherits nothing, which is why these clauses are usually paired with a bequest large enough that losing it would hurt.
