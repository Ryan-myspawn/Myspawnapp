# Who Owns Your DNA After You Die?

Freshness check: written 28 September 2026. Heading-checked against every prior article in our archive before drafting: ownership during a company's bankruptcy or acquisition is covered, control of stored reproductive samples is covered, and **the plain question of what happens to your genetic material when you personally die is not.**
Primary audience: Men 25-55.
Trend-hook used: Connecticut's genetic-privacy provisions take effect on 1 October 2026, three days from publication, and they are one of the few statutes that reaches consumer genetic data at all.

**Direct answer: almost nobody legally "owns" DNA, alive or dead, because US courts have generally refused to treat separated biological material as property at all. What actually decides the question is three separate systems that rarely talk to each other: the contract you agreed to when you handed the sample over, health-privacy law in the narrow cases where it applies, and your estate, which controls your account rather than your molecules. When someone dies without addressing any of the three, the contract usually wins by default.**

That is an unsatisfying answer. It is also the true one, and knowing which of the three governs your situation is worth more than any amount of certainty about "ownership."

## The word "own" is doing work it cannot do

The foundational US case is **Moore v. Regents of the University of California**, decided by the California Supreme Court in **1990**. John Moore's spleen cells were used, without his knowledge, to develop a valuable cell line. He sued on a property theory and lost that part of the case.

The court held that **he had no continuing property interest in cells once they had been removed from his body** ([case summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore_v._Regents_of_the_University_of_California)). **Label: state supreme court decision, 1990, still the anchor.** What he retained instead was a claim rooted in **informed consent and disclosure**, not in ownership.

**Most states have followed that logic.** Separated biological material is generally protected through **consent rules rather than property rules** ([legal scholarship overview](https://yjolt.org/sites/default/files/simana_shelly_-_genetic_property_governance.144.pdf)). **Label: peer-reviewed legal scholarship.**

**The practical consequence is the part people miss.** If a thing is not property, it does not pass through your will as property. **You cannot bequeath a right you were never held to have.** What you can do is direct what happens to the *relationship* you have with whoever is holding the material, which is a contract question, and to the *account*, which is an estate question.

## Three systems, and knowing which one you are in is the whole skill

### 1. The contract you agreed to

For almost every consumer genetic service, **the terms of service are the operative law of your situation.** They say what happens to the sample, what happens to the data, who may request what, and what a death does or does not trigger.

This is why we keep telling people to read one specific sentence: **the assignment clause**, which usually says the data travels with the company in a merger or sale. **We wrote that up in detail in [who owns your data if the company is acquired](/blog/data-ownership-after-acquisition).**

For death specifically, the relevant provisions are usually **account access, account closure, and sample destruction**, and they differ so much between providers that the only honest general statement is: **go and read yours.** Our survey of retention practice found providers that destroy a sample unless you opt in, and providers that retain it unless you separately ask for destruction, with deleting your results doing neither ([how long labs keep your DNA sample](/blog/how-long-labs-keep-dna-sample)).

### 2. Health-privacy law, in the narrow cases where it reaches

**The HIPAA Privacy Rule protects a deceased person's identifiable health information for 50 years after the date of death** ([HHS guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/health-information-of-deceased-individuals/index.html)). **Label: federal regulator guidance on 45 CFR 164.502(f).** During that window it generally protects the decedent's information to the same standard as a living person's, with specific exceptions for disclosures to family and others involved in care.

Access during that period runs through a **personal representative**, meaning an executor, administrator, or another person legally empowered to act for the estate.

**Here is the catch, and it is the single most important sentence on this page: a direct-to-consumer genetic testing company is usually not a HIPAA-covered entity.** HIPAA binds health plans, healthcare clearinghouses, and healthcare providers who transmit certain transactions electronically. **A company that sells you a spit kit online is typically none of those.** So the fifty-year protection you may have assumed covers your genome very often **does not apply to the copy held by a consumer service**.

State law is where movement is actually happening. **Connecticut's Public Act 26-64 was signed on 27 May 2026 and its genetic provisions take effect on 1 October 2026**, with further phases on 1 January 2027 and 1 July 2027. We covered the wider statutory landscape in [genetic privacy laws in 2026](/blog/genetic-privacy-laws).

### 3. Your estate, which controls an account rather than a molecule

Your executor's authority is real, and it is authority over **things the law recognizes**: accounts, contracts, and records. **It is not authority over a sample the law declines to call property.**

In practice this means the executor can usually **contact the provider, close the account, request deletion, and exercise whatever rights the contract grants**: and cannot usually **demand a physical sample be handed over** unless the contract says so.

This is the same structure as digital assets generally, where a platform's own legacy setting often does more work than a will does. We mapped what each of the major platforms actually permits in [what happens to your accounts when you die](/blog/platform-legacy-settings-explained).

**One genuine limit on our own knowledge, stated plainly:** adoption figures for the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act vary between sources we have checked, so **we do not quote an adoption count**. Check your own state directly.

## What actually happens when nobody addressed any of this

The common outcome is not dramatic. It is this:

**The account keeps existing.** Subscriptions may lapse or may not. Nobody at the company knows the person has died, because nothing tells them.

**The family finds the login, or does not.** If they do, they are technically violating the terms in most cases by using someone else's credentials, which is a rule almost nobody enforces and almost everybody breaks.

**The sample sits under whatever the retention default was**, which the person almost certainly never read.

**And if the company changes hands**, the assignment clause governs, and the decedent is in no position to object.

**None of that is a scandal. It is just a default**, and a default is what you get when three systems each assume one of the others is handling it.

## FAQ

### Do I legally own my own DNA?
Generally not as property, once it has left your body. **Moore v. Regents (California, 1990)** held there is no continuing property interest in excised cells, and most states protect separated biological material through informed-consent rules rather than ownership rules. You have rights about consent and disclosure, not a title deed.

### Can my family access my 23andMe or Ancestry account after I die?
Only as the contract allows. These companies are usually **not HIPAA-covered entities**, so the federal health-privacy route often does not apply, and access runs through the terms of service plus whatever authority an executor has over accounts. Check the specific provider's death and account-closure provisions, because they differ substantially.

### Does HIPAA protect my genetic information after death?
Where HIPAA applies, yes: **the Privacy Rule protects identifiable health information for 50 years after the date of death**, with access through a personal representative. **The problem is the "where it applies" part.** A consumer genetic testing company is typically not a covered entity, so that protection frequently does not reach the copy it holds.

### Can I leave my DNA to someone in my will?
You can leave instructions, and you can leave the account. **You generally cannot bequeath the material as property**, because the law mostly declines to treat it as property in the first place. Direct the relationship and the account, and name a person who knows it exists.

### Who decides about stored sperm, eggs or embryos after death?
That is a different and much more developed area of law, governed by consent documents, clinic contracts and posthumous-conception statutes rather than by general ownership rules. We covered it separately in [posthumous conception law](/blog/posthumous-conception-law). **MySpawn stores no reproductive material of any kind.**

### If a genetic company is sold, does my data go with it?
Usually yes, under the assignment clause you accepted. Bankruptcy has some protections that an ordinary acquisition does not, including restrictions on selling personally identifiable information and a court-appointed consumer privacy ombudsman. **An ordinary sale has no trustee, no ombudsman and no objection window.** Full detail in [who owns your data if the company is acquired](/blog/data-ownership-after-acquisition).
