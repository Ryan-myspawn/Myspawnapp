# What Happens to My DNA Sample If I Cancel?

**Cancelling is not one action. It is three, and most companies treat them as three separate requests. Closing your account, deleting your data, and destroying the physical sample are different things, and doing the first does not reliably do the other two. On top of that, simply stopping payment is not a cancellation at all: a lapsed card leaves you in whatever the contract calls non-payment, which is usually a worse position than cancelling deliberately. Two states now write part of the answer into law. Everywhere else it is whatever your provider's policy says, and a published analysis found only 29 percent of the companies that had testing policies at all had one addressing sample destruction.**

If you only remember one thing: **ask for the sample in writing, separately, and keep the reply.**

**Epistemic note, stated up front.** Company policies and published analyses below were **read through a search index rather than opened at their own domains.** Treat every company-specific statement as reported rather than verified, and check the current setting in your own account. Policies in this area changed during 2026 and are still changing. This is not legal advice.

## Cancelling is three different acts

The word "cancel" collapses three things that providers keep apart.

**One: closing the account.** This stops billing and removes your access. It says nothing about what the company still holds.

**Two: deleting the data.** This is the digital record, the file. It is often irreversible, and it is usually incomplete by design: reporting on 23andMe's process notes that **some information is retained to meet legal obligations even after deletion**, including certain genetic information, date of birth, sex, email address and the deletion request identifier. ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/03/25/1105488/how-to-delete-your-23andme-data/), via search index.)

**Three: destroying the physical sample.** This is the tube of saliva or the swab sitting in a freezer. **It is a separate request at several providers**, and the distinction is explicit in reporting on 23andMe's privacy statement: deleting data and discarding the sample are handled as different processes, and a customer who previously opted to store their sample may need to address it directly. ([23andMe privacy statement, account deletion and sample discard provision](https://conductatlas.com/platform/23andme/23andme-privacy-statement/account-deletion-and-sample-discard/), via search index.)

There is often a fourth act hiding behind these: **withdrawing research consent.** At companies where research participation is opt-in, leaving the research program is its own setting, and withdrawing does not undo analyses already completed. Treat it as a fourth request, not a side effect of the first three.

**So the honest answer to "what happens if I cancel" is: whichever of those four you actually asked for, and no more.**

## Stopping payment is not cancelling

This is the part people get wrong in the other direction.

If you want out, **do it as an affirmative act with a record.** A card that simply expires puts you into whatever your contract calls non-payment or lapse, and non-payment clauses exist to tell the company what to do when **it cannot reach you.** That is a different clause from the one governing a customer who wrote in and asked.

We saw the same structure when looking at [what happens to stored embryos when a fertility clinic closes](/blog/fertility-clinic-closes-embryos): the agreement people sign lists outcomes for death, divorce, completed treatment, a decision to stop, and failure to pay, and the failure-to-pay branch is the one that fires by default when nobody answers. **Being contactable is worth more than being right.**

## What the policies actually look like, in numbers

Two published analyses of direct-to-consumer genetic testing policies are worth knowing, and both are less reassuring than the marketing.

**Nearly 40 percent of the companies examined had no specific policies applying to their testing practices at all.** Of the companies that did have policies, **29 percent had one that addressed destroying samples**, and many of those deferred the detail, saying destruction would follow **the laboratory's standard operating procedures** or happen **as required by a certifying or accrediting agency** rather than on a stated timetable.

An earlier peer-reviewed analysis in *Genetics in Medicine* found that **14 of 30 companies, 46.7 percent, promised data would eventually be destroyed or could be destroyed on request**, with several more coded partial because they said data or samples "may" be destroyed, or gave no timeframe. The same analysis found **28 of 30 companies, 93.3 percent, carried at least a blanket statement that data or samples may be shared with third parties**, with highly variable detail about who those parties are. ([*Genetics in Medicine*, "All your data (effectively) belong to us"](https://www.nature.com/articles/gim2016136), peer-reviewed; the 29 percent and 40 percent figures are from a later published analysis of DTC testing policies, both via search index.)

**Read those together and the practical lesson is specific.** The question is not whether a company will destroy your sample. It is whether the company has written down **who decides, on what timetable, and what proof you get.** A policy that points at a lab's internal procedures is not a commitment to you.

## Two states where part of the answer is law

**California** requires direct-to-consumer genetic testing companies to honor a consumer's revocation of consent and to **destroy the biological sample within 30 days of that revocation.** ([California SB 41, Genetic Information Privacy Act](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202120220SB41), via search index.) **A named deadline is the thing to look for**, because it converts a policy into an obligation.

**Connecticut's genetic provisions took effect on 1 October 2026** and give consumers rights to access and delete genetic data, **require destruction of biological samples**, and revoke consent, with express consent required for collection, use, disclosure and retention. **Enforcement runs through the Attorney General under the state's unfair trade practices act, and there is no private right of action**, which we covered in [state genetic privacy laws](/blog/genetic-privacy-laws).

Everywhere else, you are relying on the contract. **That is not a scandal. It is simply the thing to know before you assume a federal rule has you covered.**

## The request to send, in one paragraph

Put all four acts in one message, in writing, and keep the reply.

> I am closing my account. Please confirm in writing: (1) the account is closed; (2) my genetic data has been deleted, and what, if anything, you are retaining and under what legal obligation; (3) my stored biological sample has been destroyed, and on what date; (4) my consent for research use is withdrawn as of today. Please confirm each item separately.

**Numbering them matters.** A single-line "please cancel my account" invites a single-line answer that addresses one of four things. **Keep whatever comes back:** a dated confirmation is the only evidence you will ever have, and if the company later changes hands it may be the only record that survives, which is the point we made in [what happens when a DNA company shuts down](/blog/what-happens-dna-company-shuts-down).

## FAQ

### Does deleting my account destroy my DNA sample?
Not reliably. At several providers, **data deletion and sample destruction are separate processes**, and some companies also retain limited information after deletion to meet legal obligations. Ask about the sample explicitly and ask for a date.

### What happens if I just stop paying?
You fall under whatever your contract calls non-payment or lapse, which is the clause written for customers the company **cannot reach**. That is generally a worse position than cancelling deliberately, because it is designed for a situation where nobody is answering. Cancel affirmatively and keep the confirmation.

### Can I get my physical sample back?
Usually no, and most policies do not address return at all. **They address destruction.** If being able to move the material matters to you, ask before you sign up rather than after, because an answer you get at cancellation time is a courtesy rather than a term.

### Is there a deadline by which a company must destroy my sample?
In some states, yes. **California requires destruction within 30 days of a revocation of consent.** **Connecticut's provisions, in force from 1 October 2026, include a right to require destruction of biological samples.** Outside states with a specific rule, the timetable is whatever your provider's policy says, and a published analysis found many such policies defer to a laboratory's internal procedures rather than naming a date.

### Does withdrawing from research undo analyses already done?
No. Withdrawing consent stops future use. **It does not retroactively remove your data from studies that have already been completed**, which is why it is worth treating research consent as a separate decision at signup rather than something to unwind later. The broader question of what you own at all is covered in [who owns your DNA after you die](/blog/who-owns-your-dna-after-you-die).
