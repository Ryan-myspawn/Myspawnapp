# Where Your Money Goes When Nobody Hears From You

**If an account of yours goes quiet for long enough, the bank does not keep it. It hands the balance to a state government, which holds it in your name until you ask for it.** The process is called escheatment, the trigger is a dormancy period rather than death, and **the clock usually runs from the last time you contacted the institution, not from the last time money moved.**

Most people meet this concept for the first time when they discover a state is holding money they did not know existed. **The more useful time to meet it is before that.**

## What actually triggers it

**Dormancy is about contact, not activity.** An account that pays interest into itself every month, with no login, no call and no returned mail, can still be dormant. **What ends dormancy is you making contact in a way the institution records.**

**The period is commonly three to five years, and the variation is wider than that summary suggests.** South Carolina's published schedule runs from **one year** for wages and utility deposits to **fifteen years** for travellers' cheques. **The property type matters as much as the state.**

**Before the handover, you are supposed to get a letter.** Most states require the holder to send a due diligence notice, typically **no sooner than 60 to 90 days before the reporting deadline.** Some states dictate the format: California requires a specific heading on the notice.

**The threshold for sending that letter is a live piece of law.** **Washington lowered its due diligence notice threshold from $75 to $50 under 2025 legislation, generally effective 1 January 2026**, which means smaller balances now get a warning that previously did not.

**And the letters do not work very well.** One compliance vendor estimates response rates commonly sit around **10 to 15 percent**. **LABEL: that figure comes from a company selling escheatment services, and it is the weakest source in this article.** Treat it as an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

## The number everyone quotes, and what is wrong with it

You will see the figure **$70 billion** attached to this subject everywhere.

**It is the National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators' estimate of the aggregate value held across all fifty states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico, and it is from 2023.** **It is three years old and it is still being published as a current fact.**

For scale, earlier NAUPA data put the total at about **$42 billion**, up from **$32.8 billion in 2010**. **The direction is clear. The precise current figure is not, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.**

**The return side is worth knowing too.** In fiscal year 2024, covering 1 July 2023 to 30 June 2024, states returned **over $4.49 billion** to owners. **That is the number that tells you the system does function, and also that the stock is far larger than the annual outflow.**

## What ends up there

The category is broader than forgotten savings accounts. It commonly includes uncashed payroll and vendor cheques, utility and rental deposits, insurance proceeds, brokerage balances and unredeemed stored value. **The thread is not the type of asset. It is that an institution owes somebody money and cannot reach them.**

**The contents of a safe deposit box can go the same way**, and we took that specific case apart separately in [what happens to a safe deposit box after death](/blog/safe-deposit-box-after-death). **This article is the general version of the same mechanism.**

## It happens on a schedule, not continuously

**One practical point that almost no coverage makes: escheatment runs on an annual reporting cycle.**

Holders do not transfer property the moment a dormancy clock expires. **They accumulate it and report it in a batch, on a date their state sets.** South Carolina, for example, publishes a **1 November 2026 report and remittance deadline** for several property types.

**Two consequences follow, and both are useful to you.**

**First, the due diligence letter is timed to the reporting deadline rather than to your dormancy date.** That is why the common rule is a notice no sooner than 60 to 90 days before the deadline: **the letter is part of the holder's filing process, not a courtesy triggered by your silence.**

**Second, there is a window.** Between a dormancy period expiring and the next reporting deadline, **the money is still with the institution and a single contact will usually stop the transfer.** **The practical version of that is simple: a letter about an account you had forgotten is not a notification, it is a deadline.**

## A detail that reframes the whole system

Here is the part that changes how you should read it.

**In 2023 the Supreme Court took a case called Delaware v. Pennsylvania**, reported as the first unclaimed property case the Court had heard in more than thirty years. **The dispute was not between a citizen and a state. It was between states, over which one got to hold abandoned money order proceeds** under the Disposition of Abandoned Money Orders and Traveler's Checks Act.

**We could not verify the holding from the sources available here, so this article does not state one.** **What matters for a reader is not who won. It is what the fight was about.**

**Escheatment is frequently described as consumer protection, and the structure of the litigation tells you it is also a custody question between governments.** That is not a scandal and it does not mean your money is unsafe. **It does mean that the system was not designed around your convenience**, which is a useful thing to know before relying on it to find you.

There is federal attention as well: a letter addressed to NAUPA on the subject of escheatment is dated **15 April 2026**. **We did not read it and we are not characterising its contents.** It is noted only because its existence dates the policy conversation.

## What to actually do, in order

**Search first, because it is free and takes a minute.** Every state runs an official unclaimed property database and the multi-state search is operated by the administrators themselves. **Search your own name, former addresses, maiden and previous names, and the names of deceased relatives whose estates you are involved in.** **Never pay a finder a percentage for something you can do yourself in a browser.**

**Then prevent the next one.** Three habits cover most of it:

**Make contact once a year with anything you are not actively using.** A login that the institution records is usually enough. **Interest posting is not contact.**

**Keep the address current on accounts you have stopped thinking about.** **Returned mail is one of the standard ways dormancy starts**, and it starts without you noticing because the notice never arrives.

**Tell one other person what exists.** Not the passwords, just the inventory. We make the same argument about digital accounts in [what happens to your accounts when you die](/blog/digital-legacy-what-happens), and about beneficiary-designated accounts in [the mistakes people make with payable-on-death accounts](/blog/payable-on-death-account-mistakes).

## FAQ

### What is escheatment?

The legal process by which property whose owner has gone silent is transferred from the institution holding it to a state government, which then holds it in the owner's name. The trigger is a dormancy period, not death.

### How long before an account is considered dormant?

Commonly three to five years, but it depends on the state and the property type. One published state schedule runs from one year for wages and utility deposits up to fifteen years for travellers' cheques.

### Will I be warned before my account is handed over?

Usually. Most states require the holder to send a due diligence notice, typically no sooner than 60 to 90 days before the reporting deadline, and some states dictate its format. Notices are only required above a dollar threshold, and those thresholds change.

### Is my money lost once the state has it?

No. The state holds it in your name and you can claim it. In fiscal year 2024 states returned over $4.49 billion to owners. The claim process is free and you should never pay a percentage to a finder.

### How much unclaimed property is there in total?

The widely quoted figure is roughly $70 billion across the states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico. That is a NAUPA estimate from 2023 and it is repeated everywhere as though it were current.

### Does MySpawn have unclaimed property obligations?

No. We hold a physical DNA record rather than money or financial assets, and nothing we store would be escheated to a state treasurer.
