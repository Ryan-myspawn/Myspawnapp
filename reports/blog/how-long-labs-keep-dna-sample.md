# How Long Do Labs Keep Your DNA Sample? What Retention Policies Actually Say

**The short answer: it depends entirely on which of four systems your sample is sitting in, and in one of them, deleting your results does not destroy the tube.** A clinical lab follows federal minimums measured in years. An accredited pathology lab often holds longer. A consumer DNA company follows its own published policy, which in at least one major case means keeping your saliva indefinitely by default. A research biobank usually holds until you withdraw consent, with a permanent exception for work already done. Nobody tells you which system you are in, and the answer lives inside a document almost nobody reads.

Here is what each regime actually says.

## 1. Clinical labs: federal minimums, in writing

If your sample went through a US clinical laboratory, retention is not a matter of company policy. It is federal regulation, set out in CLIA at **42 CFR 493.1105**.

The specified minimums are precise:

- **Cytology slide preparations: at least 5 years** from the date of examination.
- **Histopathology slides: at least 10 years** from the date of examination.
- **Pathology specimen blocks: at least 2 years** from the date of examination.
- **Remnants of tissue** for pathology examination: preserved **until a diagnosis is made**.
- **Quality control and patient test records**, including instrument printouts: **at least 2 years**.

Two things about that list are worth noticing. First, these are **floors, not ceilings**: a lab may hold longer, and many do. Second, there is a provision most people never encounter until it matters. **If the laboratory ceases operation, it must make provisions to ensure that records, slides, blocks and tissue are retained and available for the required timeframes.** Federal rules anticipated the lab going out of business. That is more than most consumer contracts do.

*(Source: eCFR, 42 CFR 493.1105, current text. Regulatory, not commercial.)*

## 2. Accredited pathology labs: often five times the federal floor

Accreditation raises the bar above CLIA. A laboratory accredited by the **College of American Pathologists** answers to a stricter standard, and the clearest example is paraffin blocks: **CAP calls for 10-year retention, five times the CLIA minimum of 2 years.**

This is the same distinction covered in our article on [what biobank accreditation actually certifies](/blog/biobank-accreditation-explained): accreditation is a statement about the facility's competence and processes, not about the terms of your particular arrangement. It tells you the lab knows how to hold a specimen properly. It does not tell you how long they have agreed to hold yours.

## 3. Consumer DNA companies: policy, not regulation, and the policies differ sharply

This is where the answers diverge most, and where the practical trap sits.

**23andMe's published position** is that unless you consent to have your saliva sample stored (biobanked), the sample and DNA are **destroyed after analysis** by its third-party lab. If you do opt into biobanking, the company states it may store samples **beyond 10 years**, describing indefinite storage as what allows it to deliver new reports over time. You can withdraw biobanking consent from Account Settings at any time, after which stored samples are discarded **within the legally applicable timeframe**, and deleting your account results in stored samples being destroyed.

**Ancestry's arrangement works the other way around by default.** Per its privacy statement, after testing, remaining DNA from your test is **archived and stored** in a temperature-controlled, monitored facility. The default is retention. And here is the part worth reading twice: **deleting your DNA results is not the same as destroying your sample.** Destruction requires a separate request to Member Services, with identity verification, and 2026 guidance indicates the sample is destroyed **within up to six weeks** of that request.

So the honest consumer answer is: **with one major provider the physical tube goes away unless you ask it to stay, and with another it stays unless you ask it to go.** If you have ever deleted a DNA account and assumed the spit went with it, that assumption is worth checking against the actual policy.

*(Sources: company help-centre and privacy documentation, plus consumer-press guidance. These are company-published terms, not regulation, and terms can change; check the live document before acting.)*

## 4. Research biobanks: until you withdraw, with a permanent exception

Research collections generally run on consent rather than a fixed clock. International ethical guidance is that a donor may withdraw consent, after which samples should be **destroyed or returned** and future use prohibited. Retention periods in specific programs vary widely, from defined windows such as 15 years to indefinite retention of data derived from processed samples.

The exception matters and it is not a loophole, it is arithmetic: **data already used in completed analyses cannot be un-used.** Withdrawal stops future use. It does not reach backwards into published results.

## What changes on October 1, 2026

Retention has been a contract question. In a growing number of states it is becoming a statutory one.

**Connecticut's SB 4 genetic-privacy provisions take effect October 1, 2026.** They create a consumer **property right** in the biological sample given to a direct-to-consumer genetic testing company and in the results of testing on it, together with rights to access data, delete it, **require destruction of the biological sample**, and revoke consent. Companies must obtain express consent for collection, use, disclosure and retention, including retention of the sample after testing is complete. Enforcement sits **solely with the Connecticut Attorney General**; there is no private lawsuit attached.

Connecticut is not alone. **South Dakota's SB 49**, regulating direct-to-consumer genetic testing companies, took effect **July 1, 2026**, and one 2026 law-firm survey counts **thirteen states** with direct-to-consumer genetic-testing regulation on the books. At the federal level, two bills exist and neither has passed: the **Genomic Data Protection Act (S.863)** would make access, deletion and sample destruction an FTC-enforced requirement, and the **Don't Sell My DNA Act (S.1916)** would bar genetic information from being sold or used in a bankruptcy without written consent obtained after the case begins. S.1916 was referred to the Judiciary Committee on **May 22, 2025** and remains there.

The practical takeaway: **your rights over your own sample currently depend on your state**, and that is a sentence worth saying out loud before you mail anything to anyone. For more on the state-by-state picture, see [genetic privacy laws explained](/blog/genetic-privacy-laws).

## How to find out, in about ten minutes

1. **Identify the regime.** Clinical lab, consumer company, or research study. The answer changes completely.
2. **Open the actual document**, not the marketing page. Search the privacy statement or terms for the words *retain*, *retention*, *store*, *biobank* and *destroy*.
3. **Separate the two deletions.** Ask explicitly: does deleting my results destroy the physical sample, or only the data?
4. **Ask in writing** and keep the reply. "What happens to my sample if you cease operations?" is the question most worth asking and least likely to be answered on a web page.
5. **Re-check after a policy update.** Terms change; your consent from four years ago was given to a different document.

## FAQ

**How long does 23andMe keep your saliva sample?**
Per its published policy, the sample is destroyed after analysis unless you consent to biobanking. If you consent, storage may continue beyond 10 years. Withdrawing consent or deleting your account results in destruction.

**Does deleting my AncestryDNA results destroy my DNA sample?**
No. Per Ancestry's documentation, deleting results and destroying the physical sample are separate actions. Destruction requires a direct request to Member Services with identity verification, reported as taking up to six weeks.

**How long is a DNA sample legally required to be kept?**
For clinical laboratories under CLIA, the mandated minimums are specific: 10 years for histopathology slides, 5 years for cytology slides, 2 years for pathology blocks, and 2 years for test records. Consumer DNA companies are not covered by those rules and follow their own policies plus applicable state law.

**What happens to my DNA sample if the company goes out of business?**
For CLIA-regulated laboratories, the regulation requires provisions for retention if the lab ceases operation. For consumer companies, the answer is whatever the contract and the bankruptcy process produce, which is precisely the gap the Don't Sell My DNA Act was written to close. We covered the pattern in [what happens when a DNA company shuts down](/blog/what-happens-dna-company-shuts-down).

**Can I force a company to destroy my sample?**
In Connecticut, from October 1, 2026, yes, as a statutory right enforceable by the Attorney General. Elsewhere it depends on state law and on the company's own terms.

**Does a stored DNA sample degrade over time?**
Storage conditions matter more than duration for DNA as a record. We explain the chemistry and the evidence in [how long DNA lasts](/blog/how-long-does-dna-last).
