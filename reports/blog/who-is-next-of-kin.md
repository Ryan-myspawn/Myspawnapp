**There is no federal definition of next of kin, and that is the whole answer. It is a state-law term, and worse, it is several different state-law terms that do not have to agree. The person with authority to make your medical decisions, the person with authority to bury or cremate you, and the person who inherits from you are three separate questions answered by three separate statutes, and in many families they are three different people. Anyone who tells you "next of kin" is one list is describing a country that does not exist.**

## The three questions people mean when they say next of kin

Almost everyone asking this question is actually asking one of three things, and the answers diverge immediately.

**Who can make medical decisions if I cannot?** This is governed by your state's surrogate consent or health care decisions statute.

**Who can decide what happens to my body?** This is governed by a different statute, usually in the funeral, cemetery or health and safety code.

**Who gets my things?** This is intestacy, and it only operates if you die without a will. We have covered the inheritance question in [who inherits your DNA testing account](/blog/who-inherits-your-dna-testing-account) and [who owns your DNA after you die](/blog/who-owns-your-dna-after-you-die), and it is deliberately not the subject here.

**The mistake that causes the most damage is assuming the first two follow the third.** They do not. An heir is not automatically a decision maker, and a decision maker does not automatically inherit anything.

## Medical authority: the default surrogate list

If you have not named a health care agent, most states fall back to a statutory priority list. A typical ranking runs: spouse, unless divorced or legally separated; adult child; parent; adult sibling; then other relatives, and in some states a close friend.

**Two things sit above that list.** A health care agent you named in an advance directive generally outranks the defaults, acting within the scope of the document. A court-appointed guardian generally does too, acting within the scope of the court order.

**And the variation between states is not cosmetic.** Washington's statute, RCW 7.70.065, amended in 2022, places a guardian above the health care agent and ends its list with an adult close friend. New York's Family Health Care Decisions Act lists a guardian, then spouse or domestic partner, then adult child, then parent, and permits only one surrogate at a time. California, after amendments in 2022, has no legally mandated hierarchy of default decision makers, though a provider must select a decision maker from a defined set of persons.

**Read those three again. One state ranks the guardian above your chosen agent, one allows exactly one surrogate, and one declines to rank at all.** *(These summaries come from a 2023 American Bar Association update on default surrogate statutes and from clinical reference material, read through a search index. The ABA's broader overview of surrogate consent laws dates from 2014 and is old enough that it should not be relied on for any current ranking.)*

**What happens when two people share a rank.** Most statutes prefer consensus. Some permit a clinician to rely on a majority decision among surrogates of equal rank, which is the most common dispute-resolution provision. **Note what that means in practice: three adult children who disagree two to one can produce a decision the third child never agreed to, and nothing about that is a malfunction.**

### The HIPAA consequence nobody expects

For HIPAA purposes, a personal representative is the person with authority under applicable state law to make health care decisions for the patient. **So the surrogate question and the records question have the same answer.** Whoever state law puts at the top of that list generally acquires the patient's own rights of access and disclosure. If you want someone to be able to get records, the lever is the health care decision document, not a separate request. We cover the mechanics of asking in [how to make a records request](/blog/records-request-process).

## Disposition authority: a different statute, a different list

Your body is governed by its own priority order. Texas is worth reading closely because its list is explicit and because it contains a deadline most people have never heard of.

**Under Texas Health and Safety Code section 711.002, the right to control disposition, including cremation, runs in this order unless the decedent left written directions:** a person named in a signed written instrument; the surviving spouse; a surviving adult child; either surviving parent; a surviving adult sibling; the executor or administrator of the estate; and finally any adult person in the next degree of kinship in the order named by law to inherit the estate.

**Now the deadline.** Under subsection (a-1), a person who fails to make final arrangements, or to appoint someone else to make them, before the earlier of the sixth day after receiving notice of the death or the tenth day after the death, is presumed unable or unwilling, and the right passes down the list.

**Sit with that for a second.** A grieving spouse who does not act within those windows can be presumed unwilling by operation of statute, and the authority moves to the next person in line. **Not through anyone's bad faith. Through a calendar.**

*(Two labels on that, both of which matter. The section has been amended repeatedly, including by H.B. 3070 in 2015 and S.B. 1139 effective 1 September 2021, and the text above was read through a search index rather than confirmed against the current official code. Verify the current statute before relying on the order or the deadline. And it is Texas: every state writes its own list.)*

Florida handles the same question through a definitional provision, section 497.005, which sets out an order of priority for who makes disposition decisions. **Different code, different structure, same idea: a ranked list of strangers to each other's intentions.**

## What next of kin does not get you

**Next of kin is a default, not a power.** Three limits are worth stating plainly.

**It is overridden by designation.** A named agent, in a valid document, displaces the default. In the disposition statutes this often appears as the first item on the list itself.

**It can be waived.** A person holding the role can step aside and let the next person act, which is how families resolve the common case where the eldest child lives two thousand miles away.

**It confers no automatic legal powers of the kind people assume.** Being next of kin does not by itself let you close accounts, access a safe deposit box, or compel an institution to hand anything over. Those are separate processes with separate proofs, and we have written about two of the hardest in [what happens to a safe deposit box after death](/blog/safe-deposit-box-after-death) and [the small estate affidavit](/blog/small-estate-affidavit-vs-probate).

**And the omission that catches the most people: unmarried partners hold no automatic next-of-kin standing in most states.** A twenty-year relationship with no marriage and no paperwork can rank below a sibling nobody has spoken to since 2009.

## What to actually do about it

The useful response is not to memorise your state's list. It is to make the list irrelevant.

1. **Name a health care agent in an advance directive.** This is the single highest-value document in this article, because it outranks the default in most states and because it is what HIPAA keys off.
2. **Write down your disposition wishes and name someone to carry them out**, in whatever instrument your state's statute recognises. In Texas that is the first item on the priority list.
3. **Tell the person.** An unread document naming an agent who does not know they are the agent is a document that fails on the day it is needed.
4. **Check your state, not a summary.** Including this one. Every ranking above comes from secondary sources read through a search index.
5. **Separate the three questions on purpose.** Decide who decides, who buries, and who inherits as three deliberate choices. If one person ends up in all three roles, let that be a decision rather than an accident.

## FAQ

### Is next of kin a legal term in the United States?

Yes, but only at state level, and not as one definition. There is no federal next-of-kin statute. Different state statutes use the term, or a ranked list that functions like it, for medical decisions, for disposition of remains, and for intestate inheritance, and those lists do not have to match each other.

### Does next of kin mean the person who inherits?

No. Inheritance without a will is governed by intestacy law. Next of kin, in the sense most people mean it, is about who has authority to decide something, usually a medical decision or what happens to a body. The same person often holds both roles, but nothing requires it.

### Can an unmarried partner be next of kin?

In most states, not automatically. Unmarried partners generally hold no default next-of-kin standing, which is why a named health care agent and a written disposition instrument matter far more for unmarried couples than for married ones. Some states include a domestic partner in the statutory list, so check the current text in your state.

### What happens if two people have equal priority and disagree?

Statutes vary. Consensus is generally preferred, and some states allow a health care professional to rely on a majority decision among surrogates of the same rank. That means a decision can be made that one person at that rank never agreed to.

### Can a next of kin give up the role?

Generally yes. A person holding the right can waive it so that the next person in the order can act. In the disposition statutes, some states go further and presume a person is unwilling if they do not act within a set number of days, which moves the right down the list without anyone waiving anything.

### Does being next of kin let me access someone's medical records?

Usually only if you are their personal representative under HIPAA, which means you hold authority under state law to make health care decisions for them. The route to records is the decision-making authority, not the family relationship by itself.
