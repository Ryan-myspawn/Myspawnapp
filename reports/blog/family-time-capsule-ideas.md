Most family time capsules are never opened. The International Time Capsule Society, based at Oglethorpe University, home of the 1930s Crypt of Civilization, estimates that more than 80 percent of all time capsules are lost before their intended opening date, and that roughly 95 percent are lost track of within five years of being sealed ([Oglethorpe University](https://oglethorpe.edu/news/time-capsule-society-to-preserve-records/), [Johns Hopkins Magazine, Spring 2026](https://hub.jhu.edu/magazine/2026/spring/in-search-of-lost-time/)). So the honest answer to "what should we put in our family time capsule" starts somewhere unexpected: the contents matter less than the plan for opening it. This guide covers both, in that order.

## Why time capsules fail (and what that teaches you)

Time capsules don't fail because families stop caring. They fail for three boring, fixable reasons:

- **Nobody owns the calendar.** A capsule "to be opened in 25 years" has no named person responsible in year 25. People move, divorce, downsize, and die; the date belongs to no one.
- **Nobody remembers the location.** Buried capsules are the worst offenders (landscaping changes, houses sell). The ITCS's "most wanted" list is full of capsules whose burial sites simply cannot be found ([ITCS](https://www.itcsoc.org/most-wanted-time-capsules)).
- **The contents die before the date.** Batteries corrode, rubber perishes, newsprint yellows, and digital media formats go extinct faster than people expect.

Every idea below is filtered through those three failure modes.

## Rule one: give the capsule an opening trigger, not just a date

A date is easy to forget. An event is not. The strongest family capsules are tied to milestones that will announce themselves:

- **A child's 18th or 21st birthday.** The single most reliable trigger, because the calendar is enforced by the person it belongs to.
- **A wedding, a first child, a graduation.** Milestone-triggered capsules are how digital services now structure this too; it's the model we built our Memory Vault around, video messages that unlock at life events rather than arbitrary dates.
- **A fixed family ritual.** "Every tenth Thanksgiving" works because the gathering itself is the reminder.

Then name an owner. One adult is the capsule's custodian, the location and opening trigger are written in that person's will or estate documents, and a second person holds a copy of the note. That single paragraph of planning beats any waterproof container on the market.

## Physical time capsule ideas that survive the wait

A capsule stored inside the house (closet shelf, fireproof box) beats a buried one on every measure that matters. What goes in:

### Things that age well
- **Printed photographs** on quality photo paper, ideally with names and dates written on the back in pencil (many inks fade or transfer).
- **Handwritten items with function**: the family recipe card in the cook's own handwriting, a hand-drawn map of the neighborhood, a kid's homework page. Documents that show a hand at work carry more information than posed portraits.
- **Newspaper front page or receipts from the sealing day**, with one caveat: newsprint and thermal receipts fade badly, so photocopy them onto acid-free paper and include both.
- **Small objects with stories attached**: a ticket stub, a fishing lure, a house key from a home you're leaving. Attach an index card per object saying why it's there; the story is the payload, the object is the container.
- **A "prices and predictions" sheet.** Everyone writes what a gallon of gas, a phone, and a house cost today, plus one prediction. This is the item every capsule-opening video lingers on.

### Things to leave out
- Batteries and anything containing them (they leak), rubber bands, PVC plastics, food of any kind, and unlabeled storage media. A USB stick with no note is a mystery; a USB stick with a printed index and a "formats inside: JPEG, PDF, MP4" note is a fighting chance.

## Digital time capsule ideas (do this in parallel, never instead)

Digital is where the format-extinction problem lives: families in 2026 are discovering VHS tapes with no player and burned CDs that no longer read. Digital capsules work when you treat them like an archivist:

- **Use boring formats.** JPEG for photos, PDF for documents, MP4 (H.264) for video. Exotic formats die; boring ones get migrated.
- **Three copies, two places.** One copy on a local drive with the physical capsule, one in a mainstream cloud account, and, critically, a plan for who can access that account later. Apple's Legacy Contact and Google's Inactive Account Manager exist for exactly this; we covered how to set them up in our [digital legacy guide](/blog/digital-legacy-what-happens).
- **Assume services die.** Several digital-legacy startups have already shut down, taking content with them, which is why anything that matters should exist as files you hold, not only as an account you rent. (The same custody logic applies to companies storing anything for your family; it's the question we tell people to ask even of us.)

## The highest-value item: a recorded interview

If a capsule holds one thing, make it a video of a family member talking. Not a montage: a seated interview. Five questions, one camera, twenty minutes. What was your first job? What did your father do all day? What's the family dish, and how do you actually make it? What do you want a grandchild who never met you to know? Prompt-based services like StoryWorth productized the written version of this (see our [comparison of story-preservation services](/blog/storyworth-alternative)); a video adds the voice and the hands, which are the parts families report missing most. Store it in MP4, in all three places, and put a printed still from the video inside the physical capsule with a note saying where the file lives.

Here's a gap worth noticing: a well-built capsule preserves what a person made, said, and looked like, and nothing of what they physically were. That layer is preservable now too. ## A one-afternoon build plan

1. Pick the trigger (18th birthday beats "25 years").
2. Name the custodian; write location + trigger into their estate note; give a copy to one other adult.
3. Fill a fireproof box: printed photos, two handwritten documents, three objects with index cards, the prices-and-predictions sheet.
4. Record one five-question interview; save as MP4 in three places; print one still for the box.
5. Add the index page: what's inside, what formats, where the digital copies live, who the custodian is.
6. Seal it, photograph it sealed, and put the opening trigger in two people's calendars anyway.

## FAQ

**What should you put in a family time capsule?**
Printed photos with names on the back, handwritten documents (recipes, maps, notes), a few small objects with their stories on index cards, a prices-and-predictions sheet, and a printed pointer to digital files, especially a recorded family interview. Avoid batteries, rubber, food, and unlabeled media.

**How long should a family time capsule stay sealed?**
Tie it to a milestone (a child's 18th, a wedding) rather than a fixed number of years. Event-triggered capsules get opened; date-triggered ones are the reason more than 80 percent are estimated lost.

**Are digital time capsules reliable?**
Only with archivist habits: mainstream formats (JPEG, PDF, MP4), three copies in two locations, and legacy-access settings on the accounts involved. Never rely on a single app or service surviving the wait.

**Should a time capsule be buried?**
No. Burial is the leading cause of lost capsules. A fireproof box on a closet shelf, documented in the custodian's estate papers, dramatically outperforms anything underground.

**Can you include DNA in a time capsule?**
Not loose in the box; DNA degrades with heat, humidity, and light. If you want the biological layer preserved, use a professional service with an accredited biorepository and note its existence in the capsule instead.
