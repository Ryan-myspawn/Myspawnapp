THE LEAD: A BABY FROM 1994

On July 26, 2025, a boy named Thaddeus was born in Ohio. Nothing unusual about him, except the date on his paperwork: the embryo he grew from was created in April 1994 and spent 31 years and 56 days in storage before his birth. Guinness World Records certified it as the longest storage-to-birth interval ever.

Here is the part we keep thinking about. Three decades in a tank cost that embryo nothing detectable. Properly stored biology does not age; it waits. The miracle was not the freezer. It was the chain of people who kept a storage bill paid across eight presidencies, a dot-com crash, and a pandemic, without dropping the thread.

Time is undefeated. Storage, it turns out, is how you argue with it.

THREE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

1. THE SCIENCE: In January, Gameto, the company already in an FDA Phase 3 trial for maturing human eggs outside the body, quietly licensed a set of Harvard genes that push human cells into meiosis, the exact chromosome-halving step where all lab-made-egg research is stuck. To be precise: this is an IP deal, not a breakthrough, and nobody has made a mature human egg from a stem cell. But when the referees write rulebooks (the US fertility establishment published IVG ethics guidance this year) and the players buy missing pieces, you learn how seriously they take the decade ahead.

2. THE FAKE: That viral "pregnancy robot" video making rounds again? Not real. AI-generated, no product, no company behind it, debunked by Live Science and Snopes. The real frontier is quieter: an artificial-womb company with 350+ animal tests is working toward FDA review, aimed at saving babies born too early. Rule of thumb we use: real breakthroughs name a journal and a date; fakes name a render farm.

3. THE READ: New on our blog this week: How to Write a Letter to Your Future Grandchildren. It includes the Emory University research showing that children who know their family's stories measurably cope better with life, and a five-part structure that beats the blank page. The short version: specifics survive, perfection is the enemy, and most letters fail at logistics, not writing. Read it at myspawn.me/blog.
