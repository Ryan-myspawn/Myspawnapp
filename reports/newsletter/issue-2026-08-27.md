## PLAINTEXT VERSION

THE LEAD: THE GENOME IS ACTUALLY FINISHED NOW

You may remember 2003, when the Human Genome Project announced the genome was "complete." Quietly, it wasn't: about 8% was unreadable with the technology of the day. In 2022 a group called Telomere-to-Telomere closed most of the gaps, with an asterisk: one composite copy, and you carry two, one from each parent.

This month, in the journal Cell (peer-reviewed, August 2026), researchers published the first complete DIPLOID human genome benchmark. Both parental copies, end to end, near-perfect accuracy across 99.4% of it. That adds 701 million base pairs no benchmark ever included, plus both sex chromosomes in full.

Why it matters beyond the lab: every "finished genome" milestone is really a milestone about the reading tools. The DNA never changed; what changed is how much of it we can read. 2003: 92%. 2022: one copy. 2026: both. The researchers' stated goal is complete, personalized genomes for anyone, with sharper rare-disease diagnosis to follow. The tools keep getting better on a schedule nobody has to guess about anymore.

THREE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

SCIENCE: A Brandt's bat banded in Siberia in 1964 was found alive 41 years later, at 9.8x its expected lifespan, the mammal record. This week a Nature paper (peer-reviewed) on eight bat genomes found why bats barely get cancer: an unusual DNA-repair setup, forged by millions of years of fighting DNA-damaging viruses. In the co-lead author's words: "Pathogen adaptation, longevity, and cancer resistance — they are fundamentally linked."

CULTURE: Thirteen years after the last one, the first extended look at Grand Theft Auto VI premiered this week. Whatever you think of the game, sit with the number: tens of millions of people cheerfully waited 13 years for something they cared about. Patience for the things that matter isn't dead. It just needs a reason.

FROM THE BLOG: New this week: a gift guide with a thesis. Peer-reviewed research says givers systematically buy safe, forgettable gifts while recipients actually prefer the sentimental ones, and that heirlooms beat new purchases because they carry what researchers call "essence." Eight gifts that still exist in 2046, on the blog now.

FROM THE VAULT

This week's prompt: it's first-day-of-school season. Record one 30-second video, on your phone, addressed to a milestone you can name: a graduation, an 18th birthday, a wedding day. Not a letter. Your face, your voice, thirty seconds. Seal it to the date in the Memory Vault and forget about it. Future-you handles the rest.
