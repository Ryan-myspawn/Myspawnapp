## (c) PLAINTEXT VERSION

**THE LEAD**

If you have ever deleted an account at a DNA testing company, you probably assumed the physical sample went with it.

At some companies it did not.

Deleting your data and destroying your sample are two separate requests. One major provider states that saliva is destroyed after analysis unless you opt into biobanking, in which case it may be kept beyond ten years. Another archives the leftover DNA by default, and destroying it takes a separate request with identity verification, reported as up to six weeks.

Clinical laboratories are different again, because they are regulated. Under CLIA, at 42 CFR 493.1105, the minimums are exact: histopathology slides at least ten years, cytology slides five, pathology blocks two, test records two. And there is a line in that rule most people never meet: if the laboratory ceases operation, it must make provisions so the material is still retained for the required period.

Somebody wrote down what happens when the lab stops existing. The consumer DNA industry has no equivalent.

That gap is what Connecticut's SB 4 starts to close on **October 1, 2026**, creating a consumer property right in the sample and the right to require its destruction. Enforcement sits solely with the state Attorney General. Whether you have those rights depends on where you live.

**THREE THINGS WORTH KNOWING**

**Science.** Three unrelated 2026 papers landed on the same part of the cell this year: the lysosome, where damaged proteins and worn-out organelles get broken down. One of them is a drug. Retro Biosciences dosed its first participants in a Phase 1 trial of RTR242, a molecule meant to restore lysosomal function, in Adelaide. Worth saying clearly: a Phase 1 in healthy volunteers is a safety study, not a test of whether something works, and the progress reports so far are the company's own account rather than peer-reviewed data.

**Culture.** The most-used sound on TikTok right now is a home video from 2009. It exists in 2026 only because somebody moved that file every time they changed phones, for seventeen years, probably without thinking of it as work. The moment was free. Keeping it was not.

**Product, and this is the honest half.** We published a piece this week on autophagy, and the most important sentence in it was about us: storing a DNA record confers no health benefit, no access to any trial, and no treatment of any kind. If you ever see a preservation company implying otherwise on the back of a longevity paper, close the tab. Ours included.

**FROM THE VAULT**

This week, record one 30-second video and seal it to a date you will not choose lightly. Not a message about how you feel. A message about something specific you know how to do, said out loud, to someone who will need it at an age you have not met yet.

Thirty seconds. The choosing of the date takes longer than the recording, and that is the point.
