## PLAINTEXT VERSION

**THE LEAD — They found 1,260 genes for personality. Then they published the deflating part.**

The largest study ever done on the genetics of personality landed this week in *Nature* (peer-reviewed). The scale is real: a meta-analysis across 46 cohorts, between 611,037 and 1.14 million participants depending on the trait, including European-like and African-like genomes. It identified 1,260 lead genetic variants linked to the Big Five traits, 824 of which had never been found before.

Here is the number the headlines will skip. All those common variants together explain roughly **4.8% to 9.3%** of the differences between people (7.6% to 13.4% using within-family data). Twin and family studies have long estimated personality heritability at 40-60%, and the authors say plainly that their figures are markedly lower than that.

Which means you need over a thousand variants precisely because no single one does much. There is no extraversion gene. The study even found no straightforward mapping from personality traits to specific neuron types.

Personality is heritable. Personality is not predictable. Both are true, and the second one is what the next round of headlines will get wrong.

**THREE THINGS WORTH KNOWING**

*Science.* On September 2, a peer-reviewed paper in *Nature Communications* showed RNA polymerase — one of biology's most conserved enzymes — accurately transcribing an eight-letter genetic alphabet (the four natural letters plus four synthetic ones, a system called Hachimoji). This is engineered biology in bacteria, using letters found in no wild organism, with no human application in sight. What it shows is that the reader didn't need replacing to handle a bigger alphabet.

*Culture.* We published something wrong, and someone caught it. In August we repeated the "fertility gap" — the idea that people have fewer children than they want. This week a well-argued critique pointed out that the big surveys mostly ask what's *ideal* for a hypothetical family, which is a question about social norms, not about you. Gallup's own senior scientist has said as much. We've marked our earlier claim for correction. If a statistic flatters what you're selling, it owes you receipts.

*Product.* We wrote down the four questions worth asking any company that stores something of yours for decades: who physically holds it, what happens if they're acquired or fail, whether you can get it back and at what cost, and which accreditations are verifiable on the accreditor's own site. Send all four in one email — to us, or to anyone else. Evasion is the answer.
