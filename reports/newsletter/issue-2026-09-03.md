## PLAINTEXT VERSION

**THE LEAD: The most interesting deadline in medicine this month**

Here's a date we've been tracking for you. This month, September 2026, is the listed completion date for VIBRANT, a Columbia University pilot study testing whether rapamycin, a cheap generic drug, can slow ovarian aging. That completion date isn't a rumor; it's on the study's official ClinicalTrials.gov registration (NCT05836025).

Why it matters: early signals from this line of research suggested roughly 20 percent slower loss of ovarian follicles, and a follow-up called VIBRANT II is being planned toward 1,000+ participants. To be clear about the science: these are pilot-stage findings, not a proven therapy, and completion means the data collection ends, not that answers drop the same day. But if the readout is positive, "the fertility window is fixed" stops being a law of nature and becomes an engineering problem. That's the kind of month it might be. We'll read the results the day they land and tell you what they actually say, hype-free. That's the deal.

**THREE THINGS WORTH KNOWING**

1. (Science) Eight babies in England are turning one right now after being born free of the mitochondrial disease their mothers carried, via a technique called pronuclear transfer. The results were published in the New England Journal of Medicine in July 2025, imperfections included: in three of the eight, trace levels of the mutation remain, published openly by the team. Real medicine shows its work.

2. (Culture) The International Time Capsule Society estimates more than 80 percent of time capsules are lost before their opening date, most within five years. The fix isn't a better box; it's a better trigger. Tie the opening to a birthday, not a date, and put the location in writing with a named keeper.

3. (From the blog) Two new guides this week: an honest price map of DNA banking in 2026 (every model, both the entry fee AND the annual line), and a family time capsule playbook built around that 80 percent stat. Both at myspawn.me/blog.
