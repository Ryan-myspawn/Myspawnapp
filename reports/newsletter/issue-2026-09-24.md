## (c) PLAINTEXT VERSION

**Nobody broke in**

In April, one of the most-used health research resources in the world found that data from its analysis platform was being offered for sale online. Deidentified, and already exported.

It did not put out a statement and carry on. It stopped researcher access completely for close to five months. This month it started reopening and published what it had rebuilt, and that is the part worth your time, because it was not a bigger lock.

When researchers come back, they can go in, and compute, and download nothing at all. A manual output-checking system arrives later this year, an automated one in early 2027. Every affected project was extended by six months.

Read that list again and notice what every single item is about. Not the way in. The way out.

Which tells you something about the incident. Nobody broke in. The data had already been exported, by people allowed to have it, through a door open to them on purpose.

That is a different theory of what a leak is, and it produces a different question. Not "who can get into my account", which is the one we have all been trained to ask, but **what can leave, who approves it, and is there a record that it happened.**

It would be easy to tell this as a scandal and it is not one. They disclosed, paused rather than pressing on, commissioned an independent review and accepted it, compensated every affected project, and published a staged plan with dates. Most organizations manage about two of those.

*(Sources: the institution's own reopening material. A widely circulated figure for affected participants comes from security press rather than the institution, so we left it out. How the data got out has not been publicly established and we are not going to guess.)*

**THREE THINGS WORTH KNOWING**

**Science.** A phrase turned up on a consumer supplement label this week that will be on hundreds by spring: "AI-assisted". It is not a lie and usually not even a stretch. The useful question is not whether AI was used, but **at which step**. A literature search, a pathway analysis, a compound-ranking model: all of them sit upstream of the only thing that decides whether something works, which is what happened when people took it. AI can make a hypothesis faster and cheaper. It cannot make one true. *(Company announcement on a paid press-release wire. Not peer review, not a trial result, and the company's own language was careful.)*

**Culture.** We spent three weeks checking a claim we were about to publish, about how many companies in this category had failed. It came from a summary written by a competitor. We checked all four companies individually. **One of the four claims held.** One company genuinely closed; one filed Chapter 11 and came back out; one raised ten million dollars and changed markets; one we could find no evidence about at all. The corrected version is duller and less alarming, which makes it worse marketing, and we are telling you anyway.

**Product.** Nothing new shipped. We did write down the question above and point it at ourselves, and we do not yet have a published page answering it. A draft exists. It is not live, and saying so is more useful to you than saying nothing.
