## (c) THE LEAD

**Somebody has to mow it in 2126, and a body of law exists because that arithmetic does not work on its own.**

A cemetery sells a plot once and then owes maintenance for as long as it exists. One payment, an obligation with no end date. So states wrote rules.

Mississippi requires an irrevocable trust whose principal must stay intact, with only the income available for upkeep. The fund cannot be reached by creditors and cannot be pledged as collateral. Where plots are sold in instalments, a share of each payment has to go in as it arrives. And where the Secretary of State has reason to believe a cemetery is insolvent or underfunded, the office can order the deposits made.

Michigan requires 15 percent of gross proceeds into the cemetery trust where the seller is not the cemetery itself. South Carolina sets a 15,000 dollar minimum and bars withdrawing principal without the board's permission. Virginia lets an operator recover its own original deposit, but only once the trust holds twice that amount. Georgia's attorney general issued an unofficial opinion saying the operator may not charge the customer an add-on fee to cover it.

Read those together and a design appears. You cannot spend the principal. You cannot borrow against it. You cannot bill the customer separately for it. And a named official can force the deposit.

That is what forever looks like when somebody has costed it. Not a promise or a brand. A ring-fenced fund and a regulator.

Every business that promises to hold something for a long time is making a version of the same promise. Almost none of them is regulated like a cemetery. Worth knowing which kind you are dealing with.

## (d) THREE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

**Science.** A 2026 paper in *Aging Cell*, peer-reviewed, separates two things the biological-age industry treats as one. Technical reliability asks whether the same sample run twice gives the same answer. Biological reliability asks whether the same person sampled on two ordinary days does. The paper reports that the first does not predict the second, with pooled agreement in the moderate-to-good range and none of the clocks reaching excellent. We led on epigenetic age back in October and used a general margin-of-error figure. This is the sharper version of that point, and it came from a better source.

**Culture.** In November 2026, five-star ratings appeared on a storefront for a video game that nobody outside the publisher had played. They are unverified and the accounts behind them have not been identified, so treat the story carefully. The durable part is the mechanism: a rating is a compressed experience, and if you remove the experience the number stays exactly the same size and stops meaning anything.

**Product.** We wrote this week about dormant accounts, the ones a bank eventually hands to a state treasurer because nobody answered. The trigger is contact, not activity. That rule applies to us too. If your card expires and your email bounces and nobody else knows the record exists, our procedure was not written with you specifically in mind. Make contact once a year with every custodian you have stopped thinking about, including this one.
