## THE WEEK'S ANGLE: **EVERY CUSTODIAN HAS A CLOCK. ALMOST NOBODY HAS READ THEIRS.**

**The commercial insight, in one sentence: people assume the things holding their records keep them by default, and nearly every one of those things is running a countdown they have never looked at.**

**The four proof points, all verified this week, all checkable by the reader.**

1. **HIPAA does not set a retention period for medical records at all.** It requires six years for HIPAA's own administrative paperwork. **Your actual medical record is governed by state law and payer rules: Florida requires at least two years after a practice closes; Massachusetts requires seven years from the final visit.**
2. **A safe deposit box does not need you to die. It needs you to go quiet.** Escheatment dormancy clocks run **three years in Florida, Arizona and California, five in New Jersey, Virginia and Washington.** Contents are remitted to the state, appraised and sold at public auction. **If it sells before you claim, the cash proceeds stay claimable indefinitely. The object does not.**
3. **Colorado legislated the opposite of what the privacy conversation assumes.** SB 22-224 makes donor records **subject to permanent retention**, because for donor-conceived people **deletion was the injury rather than the protection.**
4. **So the real question was never "how fast will they delete it."** It is **how long, who can open it, and who decides.** Three questions, in writing.

**AND THE TURN THAT MAKES IT AN AD RATHER THAN AN ESSAY: we are the custodian whose retention is the product rather than the side effect, and the number is on the page.** **$99 a year, billed annually. First 1,000 members lock the founding rate.** Samples held by **GenVault LLC**, an independently accredited third-party biorepository in New Jersey.

**ONE PROOF POINT DELIBERATELY EXCLUDED FROM PAID, and the reason is recorded.** This week's strongest retention research is **residual newborn blood-spot retention**, which runs from about 30 days to indefinite by state. **It is not used in any paid asset here.** Two reasons: **it sits adjacent to the retired government-biobank lane**, and **a paid ad aimed at parents about their newborn's blood is fear-mongering however carefully it is written.** **It stays in organic, where the full caveats fit.**

Three years in Florida, Arizona and California. Five in New Jersey, Virginia and Washington. The state appraises the contents and sells what nobody claims. Here is the part worth sitting with: if your grandfather's watch is sold before you file, the cash proceeds stay claimable forever and the watch does not. The system keeps what it can value. ### M3 · Audience: childfree-legacy
- **Primary text (lead, 113 chars):** `One state decided that deleting the record was the harm. Not keeping it. That inverts the whole privacy argument.`
- **Longer variant:** Almost every genetic-privacy argument of the last five years treats indefinite retention as the villain. Colorado legislated the opposite: under SB 22-224, donor records are subject to permanent retention, because for donor-conceived people the injury was records disappearing before they were old enough to ask. Both positions are right about different people. Which means the question was never how fast something gets deleted. It is how long, who can open it, and who decides.
